# Episode 11: Reading a marked lease

**Mira:** A Dubai maintenance desk has a serviceable spare pump and a departure in two hours. Its manager forwards a lease with a green comment saying the airline needs freedom to exchange parts. Can she fit this spare?

**Leo:** That green comment just tells me what someone wanted during drafting. It might explain a problem, ask a colleague a question, or instruct a lawyer to propose words. It tells me absolutely nothing about what the lessor actually agreed to.

**Mira:** Exactly. Welcome to episode eleven, Reading a marked lease. To give that desk an instruction it can act on today, we have to find the controlling words, explain which request survived, and close the loop with the actual executed counterparts.

**Leo:** I see engineering teams press Accept All Changes in Word and treat the result as a clean copy. Or they receive a clean PDF that repeats every insertion from yesterday's markup, with a covering email saying it was generated for ease of reading. What can you actually report from that PDF?

**Mira:** You can only report that the PDF shows how yesterday's proposal reads when its edits are applied. It does not mean the proposed exchanges were approved yesterday. It certainly does not supersede the signed parts schedule.

**Leo:** What if you have two matching files? Let's say the Word document and the PDF have identical text.

**Mira:** If the second was generated from the first, both describe one proposal. Matching words establish the text of that proposal, but they are weak evidence of an agreement. You still need an effective instrument before authorising a swap. Ask for the independently assembled execution set and any intervening amendments.

**Leo:** Let's work one exchange from a margin comment to closing. We have a fictional lease, effective today, with no later amendments. The asset is a replaceable pump. A pool supplier lends these out while the airline's own units are in the repair shop.

**Mira:** Keep in mind, possession simply puts the unit in the airline's hands. Fitting it requires the applicable lease permissions and a technical release. Receiving a serviceable unit in Dubai does not by itself transfer the supplier's title. Title tells you who owns it.

**Leo:** Right. So we look at the opening draft. Section four says the airline must obtain the owner's written agreement before fitting any pump owned by another person. That gives the owner a veto every single time a borrowed pump is needed.

**Mira:** Which prompts the airline to ask the night shift who can give written approval before departure. Their answer becomes a margin comment in the markup: We need to fit a loan pump during a night stop. Can the shift manager sign it out and tell the owner the next morning?

**Leo:** That comment explains the failure mode perfectly. But as a contractual instruction, it gives the manager no authority. It is just an internal request exposed to the other side.

**Mira:** So the owner replies with a counterdraft. The new section four says a pump may be borrowed from a listed supplier on notice before installation. The airline must restore an owner-owned pump before the next revenue flight.

**Leo:** That counterdraft removes the individual approval for listed suppliers, but the timing still completely defeats the airline's purpose. They can test the pump on the ground, but they cannot dispatch with the borrowed unit. If a lawyer sends a one-line update saying the owner agreed to pooling, they are going to send my shift manager to the wrong conclusion.

**Mira:** Which is why when you summarise a round of counterdrafts, you must report both changes. But let us skip to the signed counterparts. The signed section four says the airline may fit and operate with a loan pump from a supplier listed in Schedule P. It must send the exchange record within one calendar day after fitting, and complete the exchange under Schedule P.

**Leo:** And it says other suppliers require the owner's prior written consent. Then I look at signed Schedule P. Only Harbour Stores is listed. By the end of calendar day twelve after fitting, the airline shall reinstall the repaired original, or fit a qualifying permanent substitute and deliver evidence that the owner has acquired title free of third-party claims. The airline bears exchange and freight charges. The installation date is day zero.

**Mira:** Now we have a clear picture. Two documents answer two different questions. The operating clause, section four, answers whether we may fit it. The parts schedule, Schedule P, answers how we close the swap. The shift manager can now dispatch with a listed loan pump.

**Leo:** Assuming the technical release and all other operating requirements are met. The advance notice requirement from the counterdraft became a post-installation record due in one day. The flight limit became a calendar deadline of twelve days, with two ways to close.

**Mira:** But notice what survived. The individual veto survives for every unlisted supplier. And nothing in the signed pair makes the lessor pay for the flexibility. You need to give the shift manager the supplier list, the reporting address from the notices provision, and the exchange deadline.

**Leo:** Let us test that with a scenario. Harbour Stores has run out of pumps. A sister company with identical technical approvals offers the exact same model. Whose written agreement would let the airline use that supplier tonight?

**Mira:** Does the shift manager just record the identical part number, or send the exchange record the next morning?

**Leo:** Neither. The owner must give prior written consent for the unlisted supplier. The signed schedule lists a legal supplier, Harbour Stores. Matching technical capability and timely reporting satisfy entirely different requirements.

**Mira:** Let us test the calendar deadline. A listed pump is fitted on April seventh. Enter the April day number by whose end the exchange must be completed.

**Leo:** The schedule says the installation date is day zero. So April seventh is day zero. Twelve calendar days later is April nineteenth. That is the deadline to close the swap, though the exchange record has its own earlier deadline of one calendar day.

**Mira:** Now let us test the closure requirements. A qualifying permanent pump is on the aircraft on day eleven. Its supplier reserves ownership until paid. What remains open under Schedule P?

**Leo:** My engineers will say physical fitting completes the replacement. They have the part, it works.

**Mira:** But from a legal standpoint, the airline still needs the required transfer of title and the evidence of that transfer, or it must timely reinstall the repaired original. Delivery of a unit can precede transfer of title. A serviceability certificate describes condition; it cannot by itself eliminate the supplier's ownership claim.

**Leo:** That is exactly where the disconnect happens. The desk thinks the job is done because the metal is on the wing. Let's look at how these permissions trace through a public document. If we open section eight point one point four of the filed LATAM Aircraft Lease Shared Terms, it conditions pooling on the absence of a continuing event of default and identifies permitted counterparties.

**Mira:** Yes, for an engine under those terms, you have to trace the right to get the removed engine back, the continued owner title, and the requirement concerning installation at lease expiry. For a part, you follow the cross-reference to Schedule two, section one point two.

**Leo:** And section eight point two keeps the airline responsible even when someone else has possession. Each step answers a different question for operations. If I copy the default condition from that public Aircastle exhibit into our fictional pump advice, I have silently rewritten the exercise. If I use our fictional twelve-day limit on a public-exhibit engine, I am following a rule for the wrong asset under the wrong instrument.

**Mira:** Which is why you write an issue entry with enough detail that a colleague can recreate it: the agreement, the date, the asset, the action, the source paragraph, the schedule, and the remaining question. Speaking of remaining questions, what if a definition now permits an approved repair station of the airline's choice? Can the airline book the station and say nothing?

**Leo:** No. You have to search the return covenant for notification requirements, required lead time, and addresses. Freedom to choose the station can easily coexist with a duty to give the owner time to attend.

**Mira:** Let us apply that coexistence to the public exhibit. A permitted pool operator takes custody of an engine. Its dispatch team says all lease obligations now sit with the custodian. Which reading resolves this?

**Leo:** You do not just ask whether the custodian has possession. You read the engine conditions in section eight point one point four alongside the airline's continuing responsibility in section eight point two. Permitted custody and continuing contractual liability can coexist. A physical handover establishes who holds the engine today, not who answers to the lessor.

**Mira:** Let us follow the claim and the money separately. Consider a fictional equipment warranty. A valve fails and the airline pays for repairs. The lease lets the airline pursue the maker's warranty while the lease continues and no payment default continues.

**Leo:** But the maker's warranty accepts claims only from the original purchaser. So changing the lease alone cannot make the maker accept an unfamiliar claimant. We need standing.

**Mira:** Exactly. A fallback in the lease obliges the owner, as the original purchaser, to submit a supported claim on the airline's request at the airline's expense. You start a claim file with three named roles. The airline gathers the log entry, invoice, and repair release. The owner signs the claim in the capacity the maker recognises. The maker decides coverage.

**Leo:** So if the airline has paid for a covered repair, and the maker declines to process its claim because only the purchaser may file, the next contractual step is not demanding the full repair cost from the owner immediately. It is giving the owner the supporting records and requesting that it file, while budgeting for the airline's filing expense.

**Mira:** Because the fallback creates a duty to submit a supported claim. Success and the eventual distribution still depend on the warranty and the proceeds schedule. Let us look at a signed proceeds schedule for a repair finished before return.

**Leo:** It says the airline receives the lesser of documented repair cost and cash recovered. Any excess goes to the owner. This entitlement survives return and explicitly excludes filing expenses from repair cost. The maker pays after the aircraft comes back.

**Mira:** Finance should collect the repair completion record and evidence of receipt. Testing only the date cash arrived would send the entire payment to the wrong ledger. Let us run the numbers. The operator documents thirty-nine thousand eight hundred dirhams of pre-return repairs and pays nine hundred dirhams in excluded filing expenses.

**Leo:** And the maker later pays forty-six thousand five hundred dirhams.

**Mira:** How many dirhams go to the owner under this schedule?

**Leo:** The operator receives the lesser of the repair cost and the recovery. The repair cost is thirty-nine thousand eight hundred. So the operator receives thirty-nine thousand eight hundred. The owner keeps the excess: forty-six thousand five hundred less thirty-nine thousand eight hundred, which is six thousand seven hundred dirhams. The separate filing expense of nine hundred stays with the operator.

**Mira:** Perfect. Now we must test the trigger against a calendar. A request to allow time to fix something can land in several places. One draft might lengthen a deadline. Another might leave the deadline alone and limit which consequences follow when a third party delays.

**Leo:** Let's take a fictional records-access letter. A storage provider promises the owner access to specified technical records when the lease trigger occurs. The airline is due to deliver the letter on Wednesday. A negotiated exception says no event of default arises under the letter-delivery paragraph while the airline has made and documented repeated timely requests and delay is solely the provider's.

**Mira:** You must keep the requests, acknowledgements, dates, and the agreed form. A ticket created internally on Tuesday proves little about what the provider actually received. The exception protects one consequence. The letter is still missing and someone still needs to chase it.

**Leo:** What if the provider waited because the airline sent the wrong attachment? What happens to the proposed exception?

**Mira:** Delay now has another cause. The exception's sole-cause condition needs its own evidence. Because the airline contributed to the delay, you must correct the form, update the timeline, and escalate the missed obligation.

**Leo:** Let's look at another signed clause. It gives the airline four calendar days after receipt of written rejection to replace security. The owner learns of the defect on Monday, sends valid notice on Thursday, and the airline receives it on Friday. Which event starts that clock?

**Mira:** Friday's receipt. The signed trigger is receipt, not Monday's discovery or Thursday's dispatch. You keep proof of that Friday event and check counting conventions before giving treasury the replacement deadline.

**Leo:** You have to apply that same care to a suspension. Suppose claim authority resumes on cure during the lease, provided no other specified default continues, and ends when the lease terminates.

**Mira:** A payment cure can restore authority on Tuesday if the lease is still alive. A payment on Tuesday after a Monday termination cannot reactivate that same delegation. You must read any surviving proceeds entitlement separately.

**Leo:** So if payment is cured after termination, and the signed lease ends claim authority on termination but preserves entitlement to proceeds from qualifying pre-return repairs, what should the airline do? They can't file in their former delegated capacity.

**Mira:** No, they cannot. Nor should they write off all prospective proceeds. They must identify the authorised claimant and separately preserve its evidence for the surviving proceeds entitlement. Authority and entitlement have entirely different endpoints. The airline can still document its share without claiming a power that has ended.

**Leo:** An old ticket marked cured is a poor instruction to the team handling a claim after return. This brings us to the final step: giving the desk a decision it can use. You close the paper trail with a short change record.

**Mira:** Under 'asked', put the specific operation the team wanted to perform. Under 'signed', give the words' effect and section numbers. Under 'action', name the person who must do something, the deadline, and the evidence that closes the loop.

**Leo:** You mark an outcome partial if the right exists only for listed suppliers or if the airline bears a new cost. Record an unresolved issue as unresolved. Two endpoint documents rarely identify the person who persuaded the other side or the path between them.

**Mira:** For the pump we discussed earlier, your shift handover could read: Harbour unit cleared under section four and Schedule P, subject to maintenance release. Shift lead to send the record within one calendar day. Materials team to reinstall the original or finish the documented permanent replacement by day twelve. Finance to cover freight and exchange charges. Call legal before fitting a unit from another supplier.

**Leo:** That tells the next shift exactly what it can do tonight and what work it inherits tomorrow. Let's try one more. The first markup asks for free follow-up inspections. The execution set charges the airline only for a visit needed to verify a material defect was fixed. How should you record that result?

**Mira:** You do not record that the airline won its full request, nor do you assume every repeat visit is chargeable. The signed text leaves a defined follow-up cost with the airline. The action is to establish why this specific visit was needed before approving its invoice.

**Leo:** The two endpoints support a comparison of scope and cost, but they do not establish a sequence of unobserved offers. We have covered a lot of ground today.

**Mira:** Let's summarize with three takeaways. First, do not rely on a clean PDF generated from a markup; you must match the body and schedules to the executed counterparts to find the controlling words.

**Leo:** Second, physical possession and technical serviceability do not automatically transfer title or satisfy contractual conditions. You have to track standing and evidence separately.

**Mira:** Third, give the desk an instruction it can act on by naming the actor, the strict calendar deadline, and the evidence required to close the loop.
