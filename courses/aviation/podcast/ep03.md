# Episode 3: From the first advance to the delivery signature

**Leo:** You are wiring tens of millions of dollars to a manufacturer for an aircraft that does not exist yet, and if that assembly line stops, your money is just sitting in a foreign bank account while your competitors are flying.

**Mira:** Which is exactly why we need to map the mechanics from the first advance to the delivery signature.

**Leo:** The buyer may have money at risk years before it has an aircraft. That advance is called a pre-delivery payment, or PDP. The Original Equipment Manufacturer, the OEM, can use an advance to support production and test the customer's commitment. But the airline ties up cash and takes the risk that the aircraft never arrives or its money comes back late.

**Mira:** Keep a dated ledger for each delivery position. Record the amount due, amount invoiced, amount cleared, the beneficiary, the contractual basis, and the balance credited. A schedule might demand payments on fixed calendar dates, a number of months before a scheduled delivery month, or upon completion of defined build milestones.

**Leo:** Take a fictional purchase. Suppose a twenty-four million dollar agreed final price is paid in four steps. Two million dollars is due on signing, three million dollars on a build milestone, and one million dollars on a test milestone. All three amounts are credited at closing.

**Mira:** So treasury needs eighteen million dollars for the final payment. How much cash is already at risk depends on the date you ask. After the second payment, the buyer has five million dollars outstanding with the manufacturer.

**Leo:** Right, the sum of the running balances is not a meaningful measure of the cash paid. You do not add two million, plus five million, plus six million. Your total cash out the door before closing is just six million dollars. Ownership only moves when the closing conditions are met.

**Mira:** And find the base for every percentage. A projected price used to set advances may differ from both the signed base price and the final adjusted price. Never substitute one for another just because the column headings both say 'price'.

**Leo:** Let us look at a real clause. Take Article four point two of the filed LAN aircraft purchase agreement. It identifies the advance payment base price as the denominator. It demands an initial percentage, quote, less the Deposit, unquote.

**Mira:** That specific phrasing calls for a top-up. You must separate a reservation deposit already held from the first scheduled pre-delivery payment. The credit for the deposit sits inside the first payment calculation.

**Leo:** So if an order calls for a first pre-delivery payment of four percent of a twenty-five million dollar advance payment base, less a two hundred and fifty thousand dollar deposit already received, how many dollars must be wired now?

**Mira:** Twenty-five million multiplied by four percent is one million. Subtract the two hundred and fifty thousand dollar deposit. You wire seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars. The deposit counts toward the first million.

**Leo:** What happens when an order is placed close to the scheduled delivery month? A contract may bring forward instalments which would already have fallen due under the normal schedule. That can turn the signature date into a much larger funding event than the published first percentage suggests.

**Mira:** Exactly. And here is a scenario for your treasury team. A schedule sets percentages against the advance payment base price, projected at signing. A year later, the delivery invoice has increased. Can treasury recalculate last year's pre-delivery payment using the new invoice?

**Leo:** Never, because advance schedules cannot change once signed.

**Mira:** Not quite. Only if the contractual schedule or an effective amendment calls for that recalculation. You have to trace the specified denominator and any true-up. The final invoice alone does not change the earlier formula.

**Leo:** Got it. So what does the buyer actually own the day after paying an advance? If the manufacturer keeps the money and owns the work in progress, what is the buyer's position?

**Mira:** You need to read the title-transfer provision. Certain contracts can transfer ownership at a defined stage, including delivery of an unfinished aircraft, but the payment of a percentage alone tells you nothing about ownership. The buyer may simply hold a contractual claim to performance or a refund.

**Leo:** Which means we need to ask when that refund becomes due, who owes it, whether interest accrues, and what the buyer can realistically collect if the manufacturer cannot pay. A segregated escrow arrangement can hold funds until a specified release point.

**Mira:** Or a refund guarantee can add a second source of payment on the terms of the guarantee. But neither device automatically covers every dollar and every event. You have to compare the covered obligations, amount, expiry, demand formalities, and identity of the beneficiary to the pre-delivery payment schedule.

**Leo:** This is where expiry mismatches happen. Suppose a refund guarantee terminates before the first date the buyer can validly demand repayment after a prolonged delay. Which proposed change directly closes that gap? Do we raise the face amount but leave the expiry unchanged?

**Mira:** A larger amount cannot be drawn after expiry. You must extend coverage through the demand period and verify the trigger and demand conditions. The trigger, timing, and documentation must work together. A parent company that has signed a different commercial letter may have assumed no refund obligation at all.

**Leo:** Let us talk about the cash pressure of these delays. Financing pre-delivery payments can relieve immediate cash pressure but introduces a new mismatch. The bank may advance against the buyer's rights under the purchase agreement.

**Mira:** And the bank may seek an assignment of those rights and the manufacturer's acknowledgement that a refund will be paid to a specified account. You must check whose consent is needed and where a refund goes if the lender must be repaid.

**Leo:** Because if the purchase terminates, interest on the buyer's loan may continue while the buyer waits for the OEM to return the cash. Here is another hypothetical. A buyer funds four million dollars of pre-delivery payments for ninety extra days after an unexpected refund delay.

**Mira:** Let us assume an eight percent simple annual interest on an actual over three-hundred-and-sixty basis, with no interest recovered.

**Leo:** Four million dollars, multiplied by zero point zero eight, multiplied by ninety, divided by three hundred and sixty. That is eighty thousand dollars. What additional US dollar financing cost is retained? It is that eighty thousand. The principal refund and the extra financing cost are distinct.

**Mira:** Now suppose delivery fails. Put dates against termination, demand, refund, bank repayment, and release of any guarantee. If the buyer has advanced six million dollars and holds a promise to refund it 'on termination', what additional evidence would justify recording a recovery of six million on the termination date?

**Leo:** You would need the due date and conditions, verified ability to pay or effective support, and the practical timing of collection. You model the timing and possible shortfall until the evidence supports immediate collection.

**Mira:** Exactly. Now let us move to the delivery window. Production initially may be scheduled by quarter or month. Closer to delivery, the seller gives notice of a week, then a day. A delivery window gives a range within which performance is timely.

**Leo:** But a particular notice can narrow that range or simply forecast readiness. We have to look for the clause that makes a date binding. A calendar invitation for the buyer's technical team may authorise travel without starting the cure or delay clock.

**Mira:** So record which notices have been given in the required form and to the specified address, and which dates are still planning assumptions. If a forecast delivery week moves, what entry belongs in the issues list before you budget delay damages?

**Leo:** You identify the binding deadline, permitted adjustments, buyer dependencies, and notice requirements. A moved forecast by itself establishes no compensable period.

**Mira:** Correct. Let us define tender. Tender means an offer to deliver under the agreement. Ask what the manufacturer must have done before a tender qualifies. Identify the delivery location, state of completion, required documents, inspection access, and notice period.

**Leo:** Because a tender of a different configuration or missing essential documents might be disputed. But an airline that simply fails to attend risks missing an acceptance deadline or triggering a wrongful-refusal provision. Here is a scenario: The OEM forecasts Tuesday and invites inspectors. The contract starts the buyer's response clock on conforming tender with listed records. But key records are missing. What should the buyer do first?

**Mira:** Do not remain silent. A dispute about sufficient tender does not make silence safe. You document the gap and issue the required response while preserving inspection and timing rights. The form and timing of the buyer's response matter.

**Leo:** And respond promptly in the prescribed form, specify each unmet condition, and reserve the rights counsel says the contract permits. Have a technical specialist own the evidence supporting each objection.

**Mira:** While you do that, read buyer dependencies at the same time. These may include choosing an interior by a freeze date, providing information for registration, arranging insurance, clearing payment, and sending authorised representatives.

**Leo:** The OEM may extend the timetable to the extent a buyer failure delays production. But ask for the link between the late input and the actual delivery path. A missing seat drawing can stop a cabin installation. An unrelated missing invoice address need not explain a delay in flight testing.

**Mira:** So the buyer should record the original schedule, the affected task, mitigation steps, and the earliest revised date. That brings us to acceptance evidence. Technical acceptance records the buyer's conclusion after the agreed checks.

**Leo:** Those checks might include a review of configuration, records, ground systems, and a demonstration flight. And a demonstration flight has the scope and operating constraints specified for that delivery. White-space in a checklist is dangerous if nobody owns the test.

**Mira:** You must agree who can attend, who bears the flight cost, what happens when an inspector cannot travel, and how repeat tests are scheduled. And identify technical people who may flag a discrepancy and the commercial or legal representative who can bind the buyer.

**Leo:** A discrepancy is a difference between the required condition and what is tendered. Classify it against an actual requirement, including its source, measurement, and tolerance. An inspector's preference for different upholstery does not establish a contractual deviation if the installed item matches the approved drawing.

**Mira:** Conversely, an apparently small record gap can prevent intended use. A signed discrepancy list should say which defects prevent acceptance, which can be cured after delivery, who pays, what evidence closes them, and what remedy survives signing the acceptance certificate.

**Leo:** This is where holdbacks save the deal. Withholding part of the price until a minor item is corrected can make acceptance commercially possible. But a buyer holding cash for a repair it will never permit the seller to perform creates its own dispute.

**Mira:** So define the withheld sum, deadline, access needed, evidence of completion, and release instructions. Ask whether the holdback is the exclusive remedy for that item, whether the agreed amount covers the realistic cost, and who bears downtime.

**Leo:** Let us look at a specific clause. Read the filed Bombardier agreement, articles five point two C and five point three C. Imagine the buyer agrees to take delivery with two defects. One is later fixed. The airline keeps flying instead of presenting the aircraft for the second repair during the agreed period.

**Mira:** Give treasury a separate instruction for each sum. What can the buyer hold, and when must it release each amount? At delivery, obtain written consent to deferred correction, adjust the receipt, and itemise the seller's good-faith repair estimates.

**Leo:** So we pay the first amount once its repair satisfies the buyer acting in good faith. What about the missed visit?

**Mira:** For the missed visit, return the corresponding amount within five business days after the agreed period expires. Settle positioning costs in writing at delivery. The seller's correction obligation excludes downtime.

**Leo:** Unless the failure to present was outside the buyer's reasonable control and occurred without its negligence. Then you agree a new final period.

**Mira:** Here is where the signature matters. A buyer's engineer signs a delivery certificate that says all discrepancies are waived except those listed. A known defect appears only in internal email. What is the immediate exposure?

**Leo:** The seller may rely on the waiver. Internal notice to finance does not automatically amend the certificate. You must record the defect in an agreed reservation before signing. Authority, waiver wording, and reserved items determine the result. An internal email gives the seller no agreed exception.

**Mira:** So which post-delivery repair mechanism is most executable? Is it a promise that both teams will cooperate when convenient?

**Leo:** No. It is an identified defect, responsibility for access and cost, a completion test, a date, and a funded remedy. A verifiable close-out route lets both sides decide when remaining funds may be released.

**Mira:** Now we coordinate cash, documents, and insurance. The bill of sale is the seller's transfer document. The acceptance certificate is the buyer's record of acceptance. Payment confirmation, delivery of the bill of sale, release of security, and transfer of risk can occur at different moments.

**Leo:** You have to lay them out in minutes on closing day. Ask who holds originals or agreed electronic copies before release and how each party confirms the closing conditions are satisfied. Take a final check of the aircraft location because tax and registration analysis may depend on where physical delivery occurs.

**Mira:** And obtain written confirmation from the advisers responsible for those matters. Insurance must work across the precise handover. Suppose title passes while the aircraft remains with the manufacturer for completion work. The new owner might bear some risks while another party retains physical control.

**Leo:** The documents can allocate that exposure specifically. Match insurance coverage to the party, activity, location, and interval, including the ferry flight that moves the aircraft to its operating base. Do not assume the policy that covered production automatically covers the buyer's outbound flight.

**Mira:** Request evidence from the broker in time to adjust the closing sequence. Here is a timing gap scenario: The buyer signs a technical acceptance at noon. The contract shifts risk on delivery of the bill of sale at fifteen hundred hours. Who should arrange protection for that gap?

**Leo:** You identify the risk bearer under the contract and verify applicable cover through fifteen hundred hours, with the buyer's cover ready at transfer. The specified transfer event controls the contractual allocation. Check actual policy coverage for both sides of that event.

**Mira:** Finally, reconcile the price ledger, the signatory's authority, any agreed reservation, and the bank's funding conditions. Verify changes to payment instructions through an independently established contact.

**Leo:** A closing checklist should give every item a document, owner, verifier, and release condition. If a condition remains open, record who may waive it and quantify what changes if it is waived. A decision to close with a documented minor cabin defect and funded cure may be sensible.

**Mira:** But a decision to transfer the entire price while the seller lacks authority to convey the engines is a different order of risk. What would you ask treasury to refuse to release if an engine serial number is wrong on the bill of sale?

**Leo:** The final funds subject to that delivery condition. Have the seller correct the document and the technical team match it to the installed engine and title evidence before release.

**Mira:** One last scenario. After a valid delivery, the seller sends a supplemental invoice for repeat tests. Which first step gives the best response?

**Leo:** Trace the contractual cost allocation to the cause of the repeat and the signed test record. The agreement may allocate costs differently for initial tests, buyer requests, and repeats required to cure a defect. Delivery does not automatically extinguish all disputes.

**Mira:** Let us close this out. First, a pre-delivery payment is a balance at a point in time, and its formula cannot be changed by a final invoice unless the contract or an amendment permits it.

**Leo:** Second, if tender is defective, do not remain silent; document the gap, issue the required response, and preserve your timing rights.

**Mira:** Third, technical acceptance waivers are binding, so any known defect must be recorded in an agreed reservation before your engineer signs the certificate.
