# Episode 16: Make the sign, negotiate or walk call

**Leo:** You are staring at a closing file for an eight million dollar aircraft. The seller just offered a seventy-five thousand dollar price drop to leave an old mortgage outstanding on the airframe. Do you sign, negotiate, or walk?

**Mira:** You walk, or you escalate. You never trade a mandatory clean-title veto for expected value just because the math looks good on a spreadsheet. Make the sign, negotiate or walk call.

**Leo:** Our listener today wants to know how to turn landed terms into a priced recommendation, identify the maximum payable price, and give a concrete commercial instruction that a client can actually execute at the negotiating table.

**Mira:** Then we have to start before the legal text even begins. We have to establish what the client is actually buying. A lot of technologists assume the headline price is the only variable, but a purchase might replace an existing aircraft, add capacity for a new route, or provide an asset to lease out.

**Leo:** If an airline is comparing an operating lease with buying, they need both alternatives on the exact same footing. You have to match aircraft capability, usable service period, expected utilisation, and the end condition.

**Mira:** The end condition is where the models usually break. If a lease avoids residual sale risk, you must avoid assuming the airline owns a highly valuable aircraft at the end. You have to define when operations can actually begin, too. If one aircraft requires a cabin change and crew training first, its headline price is only part of the cost.

**Leo:** Let us test that comparison. An airline compares five years of rent with the full price of an aircraft that it can sell after five years. Which adjustment matters? Do we add the purchase price to the rent to make both cases comparable?

**Mira:** No, you use a common operating horizon and value the owned aircraft's residual proceeds and costs at that horizon. The two alternatives deliver entirely different assets and obligations at year five. You have to compare their net cash flows and respective return or sale costs on one single valuation basis.

**Leo:** That requires a value ceiling that actually belongs to the client. One way to derive it is to value future cash flows from operating the aircraft, taking account of the alternative the client can actually use. Another is to use an approved budget for equivalent capacity.

**Mira:** But you cannot just take that number blindly. Ask whether the number includes maintenance, financing, transaction costs, or risk allowances already. And always ask the finance team to sign off on the currency and the valuation date.

**Leo:** Because present value converts future cash to a common earlier date using an agreed discount rate. A deposit today and a potential refund years from now can have the exact same nominal amount, but completely different economic values.

**Mira:** Once you have that ceiling, you build a loss register from the issues list. This is a short table of events, their financial consequences, and the protection actually available. One line might be that late delivery forces a temporary replacement. Another could be that an engine has fewer usable cycles at return than promised.

**Leo:** For each event, you state the time horizon, the probability, the gross loss, the expected recovery conditional on that event, and the owner of the estimate. You have to define exactly what counts as the event. A delay past the scheduled date and a delay past the end of a contractual grace period have very different probabilities.

**Mira:** And you must use a repair estimate from engineering, an actual substitute quote from procurement, and a reviewed contract clause for the remedy. You read the remedy from trigger to collection. Does it apply on these facts? Is the loss eligible? What does a deductible remove? Does a cap limit one claim, one aircraft, or the whole order?

**Leo:** Collection is the ultimate test. Does the counterparty actually have money? Must the airline spend money on parts to use a credit? Could the credit expire first? You have to answer those questions before recording recoverable value. The largest face amount on a page can be far above the amount that can actually be collected on your specific scenario.

**Mira:** Let us look at an illustrative remedy. The buyer may apply the credit to eligible spares ordered before the first anniversary of delivery. If the buyer expects to need only forty thousand dollars of eligible spares in that window, a one hundred thousand dollar non-transferable credit cannot be valued at face solely because the manufacturer remains solvent.

**Leo:** Right, you check whether the buyer could shift planned purchases without raising their cost, whether the credit stacks with existing discounts, and whether a later aircraft in the fleet can use it. You estimate the avoided net spend on purchases it would otherwise make. Distinguish that conditional value from cash in an escrow account that is already available for release.

**Mira:** Here is a way to count the same loss twice. A model prices every possible delay as excusable and also assigns a separate probability to the exact same supplier interruption under the manufacturer's breach. Where is the flaw?

**Leo:** The flaw is double-counting the recovery paths. You must define disjoint paths for the same incident and assign probabilities to those paths. Recoveries under alternative legal characterisations cannot both apply to a single loss unless the agreement actually permits them.

**Mira:** Let us quantify that with a stacking problem. A loss is three hundred thousand dollars. Cash recovery is capped at two hundred thousand dollars. A one hundred thousand dollar spares credit is an alternative to the cash remedy for the same claim. If the cash is fully collectible, what is the best supported residual loss?

**Leo:** The residual loss is one hundred thousand dollars after taking the cash remedy. You choose the available alternative with the higher economic value. The contract forbids adding both to recover the same loss, so you cannot stack the cash and the credit to get zero.

**Mira:** Now we calculate kept risk and headroom. Kept risk means the expected financial loss left with the client under the package. For each scenario, take the gross loss less the collectible recovery, floored at zero, then multiply by the probability of that scenario.

**Leo:** If several outcomes could happen at once, check for duplicate loss components and interactions such as a shared cap. You can sum expected losses across distinct components without assuming they are independent. Dependence matters heavily for a shared recovery pool, combined tail exposure, and the cash peak.

**Mira:** Exactly. You must model joint outcomes when one event changes how much the other costs or can recover. Let us test a shared limit. Two simultaneous losses of one hundred and fifty thousand dollars each share a single two hundred thousand dollar aggregate limit. All claims qualify, collection is certain, and no other remedy applies. What is the retained loss in that joint outcome?

**Leo:** The retained loss is one hundred thousand dollars because the available recovery is shared. You add the losses to get three hundred thousand dollars before applying the common cap once. A per-row model with two fresh limits would overstate the recovery completely.

**Mira:** Let us walk through the main purchase model. We will use the invented figures from the module. An aircraft is available for eight million dollars. Closing costs are one hundred and twenty thousand dollars. The client's value ceiling is eight point two five million dollars before those costs and the specific unresolved risk we are about to price.

**Leo:** And all amounts are already on the same valuation date and currency. Title clearance and available cash are separate mandatory conditions. Writing these assumptions first prevents a favourable expected value from quietly becoming authority to take a risk the client expressly prohibited.

**Mira:** Our buyer faces a twenty percent chance of a records-related shop visit costing five hundred thousand dollars, all measured on the agreed valuation date. The negotiated remedy would reliably pay one hundred thousand dollars in that event. What is the gross residual loss?

**Leo:** Gross residual loss in the event is four hundred thousand dollars. So, the expected kept risk is that four hundred thousand multiplied by the twenty percent probability, which gives eighty thousand dollars.

**Mira:** Now we build the buyer cost bridge. The effective cost is the eight million dollar price, plus the one hundred and twenty thousand dollars in closing costs, plus the eighty thousand dollars in expected kept risk. That brings the effective cost to eight point two zero million dollars.

**Leo:** Which leaves the headroom. Headroom is the ceiling less the effective cost. Here, the eight point two five million ceiling minus the eight point two zero million effective cost leaves exactly fifty thousand dollars of headroom.

**Mira:** We also need to find the maximum payable price. The maximum headline price that meets the ceiling under these terms is the eight point two five million ceiling less the one hundred and twenty thousand closing costs less the eighty thousand kept risk, which equals eight point zero five million dollars.

**Leo:** Those numbers answer completely distinct questions. You give the negotiator the eight point zero five million dollar price limit, and you tell them they have a fifty thousand dollar remaining cushion at the current offer. But remember, a title or peak-funding veto can still determine the verdict, regardless of the headroom.

**Mira:** Let us drill into conditional recovery. A separate teaching risk has a fifteen percent probability of a two hundred and forty thousand dollar loss. The only collectible recovery would be sixty thousand dollars if that event occurs. What is the expected kept loss in dollars?

**Leo:** The event leaves one hundred and eighty thousand dollars with the client. Multiplying by zero point one five gives twenty-seven thousand dollars. The recovery is conditional on occurrence and is probability-weighted along with the loss.

**Mira:** And if a client values an aircraft at six point one million dollars on today's basis, transaction costs are seventy thousand dollars, and expected unrecovered losses are one hundred thousand dollars. With no other cash flows, what is the break-even headline price in dollars?

**Leo:** You subtract both costs from six million one hundred thousand dollars, giving five million nine hundred and thirty thousand dollars. The ceiling is for the complete package.

**Mira:** Let us shift to peak funding. A loss with a small expected value can still demand a large immediate payment. In the worked purchase, if the shop visit happens and the buyer pays before reimbursement, it must advance the entire five hundred thousand dollars.

**Leo:** The expected kept risk of eighty thousand dollars would fund only a fraction of that. You have to construct a peak-funding schedule with the purchase price, due costs, security, reserves, and downside cash calls on their actual dates.

**Mira:** List the committed funds available on those exact same dates and any restrictions on drawing them. An expected refund after the peak cannot finance the peak. You must agree on a financing facility, a funded retention, or a timing change before a client commits itself beyond its liquidity ceiling.

**Leo:** Here is a liquidity test. Which proposal directly solves a binding four hundred thousand dollar cash limit when the client must initially pay five hundred thousand dollars for repairs?

**Mira:** A committed one hundred thousand dollar facility drawable before payment, with its costs and conditions approved. The facility can meet the one hundred thousand dollar peak shortfall on time. Later recovery or a lower estimated probability leaves the exact same cash need if the event actually occurs.

**Leo:** That brings us to sensitivity. You have to vary the assumptions the client can dispute. For the worked purchase, one hundred and thirty thousand dollars remains under the ceiling after price and closing costs.

**Mira:** Dividing that one hundred and thirty thousand dollars by the four hundred thousand dollar retained loss per shop visit gives a break-even event probability of thirty-two point five percent. You ask the technical adviser whether the unresolved facts could plausibly put the probability above that threshold.

**Leo:** The estimate can be given as a range. If it straddles thirty-two point five percent, collecting more records may be worth far more than negotiating an elegant change to the notice wording. A condition to complete a records check can reduce both uncertainty and peak funding.

**Mira:** Let us run that math on a different deal. Assume a deal has seventy-five thousand dollars of headroom before a single risk. The loss if that event occurs is three hundred thousand dollars after recovery. What break-even probability, entered as a percentage number, exhausts the headroom?

**Leo:** Divide seventy-five thousand dollars by three hundred thousand dollars to obtain zero point two five, or twenty-five percent.

**Mira:** Now we move to the final instruction. You use three verdicts with clear authority. Sign if the actual final terms fit the value and funding limits, the required evidence exists, and no veto remains. A forecast is appropriate before the final draft, but signing authority requires checking the actual executed package against that forecast.

**Leo:** You negotiate if specific achievable changes move the deal inside the mandate or resolve an outstanding condition. State exactly what the counterparty must do, the cheapest acceptable substitute, who can approve it, and the deadline.

**Mira:** And you walk when the only available terms leave an uncured veto or an economic gap that cannot be bridged within the mandate.

**Leo:** Consider two variations on the worked purchase. If the seller raises the price by ninety thousand dollars, the final package is forty thousand dollars above the ceiling. A credible forty thousand dollar price reduction brings it back to break-even if all other assumptions hold.

**Mira:** But a new forty thousand dollar remedy that pays only when the twenty percent event occurs saves at most eight thousand dollars of expected cost. Asking for forty thousand of protection without specifying whether it is money today or conditional reimbursement can turn a viable counterproposal into an unworkable one. The forty thousand dollar price reduction is valuable with certainty in this exercise.

**Leo:** What size concession would actually close the gap? The package exceeds value by thirty thousand dollars. The only disputed event has a ten percent probability. An additional thirty thousand dollar remedy would be collectible only if that event occurs. How much of the gap would it close?

**Mira:** Only three thousand dollars. The additional conditional recovery reduces expected loss by zero point one zero times thirty thousand dollars, assuming the covered loss is large enough. The remaining gap is twenty-seven thousand dollars.

**Leo:** This leads back to the opening veto. The seller offers seventy-five thousand dollars off the purchase price to leave an old mortgage outstanding. The expected loss estimate is twenty thousand dollars. The board requires clean title at transfer. Who can accept this trade?

**Mira:** The deal team has no mandate to substitute expected value for the board's express condition. You seek the discharge. If the seller cannot provide it, decline on the existing mandate. Any reconsideration belongs to the authorised decision maker.

**Leo:** What if the client requires discharge on settlement, and a bank has agreed to a documented simultaneous release, but the escrow and funding instructions still require signatures? Which instruction fits today?

**Mira:** Make authority conditional on receiving the agreed signed release arrangements. A credible and specific cure remains available. Confirm it in the closing file before authorising the relevant release. Do not accept a verbal agreement, but do not walk permanently just because a mortgage existed yesterday.

**Leo:** When you deliver the final memo, lead with the verdict, the money at stake, and the one condition most likely to change the answer. Support it with the issues list, each position and fallback, the comparable rows and excluded rows, and the leverage facts behind the forecast.

**Mira:** Show an expected and a downside column. State the maximum price, funding peak, and a sensitivity threshold. For every material assumption, list the source, owner, and verification status. Attach the relevant excerpts, mark redactions as unknown, and distinguish adviser estimates from promises in the text.

**Leo:** A board member should be able to see exactly where an extra dollar comes from and who has the authority to accept it. This is why you must rerun the decision if the closing draft changes. For example, the closing draft changes only the definition of eligible records. Why rerun the decision?

**Mira:** Because the definition can change which shop visits qualify, how much recovery is collectible, and whether the buyer can fund the retained tail. Reprice the affected row and recheck approvals before treating the change as closed.

**Leo:** What about timing mechanics? A model correctly predicts a five-day cure period. The final form counts from the due date while the approved forecast counted from receipt of notice. What should the deal lead do? Just mark it achieved because both deadlines contain five days?

**Mira:** No, reopen the timing issue, assess the real cash and default consequences, and seek revised authority if material. Keep the forecast as a comparison point. A change to the trigger can shorten the client's effective response time even when the stated number is identical.

**Leo:** Before passing the file on, put the proposed instruction beside the certificate, schedule, and payment direction that someone would actually use. Identify who must sign or supply each, and what treasury or operations should do if it is missing on the day.

**Mira:** Hand the file to a colleague with one question: can they reach your decision from the cited documents without hearing the negotiation story? The desk checks the passage you located as well as the conclusion you drew.

**Leo:** Three takeaways for our listener. One: The value ceiling belongs to the client; calculate the break-even price by subtracting closing costs and expected kept risk from that ceiling.

**Mira:** Two: Test liquidity separately. An eighty thousand dollar expected risk does not pay for a five hundred thousand dollar immediate repair bill. You need a committed facility to meet the cash peak.

**Leo:** Three: Never waive a mandatory veto for an expected value discount. If the board requires clean title, an uncured lien means you walk or return to the authorised decision maker.

**Mira:** That is how you clear the aircraft deal.
