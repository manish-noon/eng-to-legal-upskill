# Episode 7: Rent and security

**Leo:** The aircraft is sitting on the ramp, ready for handover, but your technical team just found a defect. The chief financial officer is asking if we can hold back the cash.

**Mira:** Not if you have already signed the certificate. This is 'Rent and security', where we follow the cash from acceptance to return.

**Leo:** How do we follow the cash from acceptance to return?

**Mira:** You can distinguish a cost from a balance tied up as security, identify who carries downtime, and propose a concession the other side can actually grant. It all starts with the rent clock.

**Leo:** Operationally, a lease buys the airline time with an aircraft. But delivery under the lease can easily precede the first revenue flight. The airline cares about the cash required to put the aircraft into service and keep it there.

**Mira:** And on an operating lease, the owner expects an aircraft back and cares about its future earning power. Those two perspectives produce several payments with similar names. You have to start your review with the payment schedule and the signed form of delivery receipt.

**Leo:** Right, put the first and last dates of basic rent on a calendar. Basic rent is the recurring scheduled consideration for possession during the lease. You also check whether rent is payable in advance or in arrears, who designates the receiving account, and what counts as receipt.

**Mira:** Let us test that with an illustrative deal. Possession and rent start when the airline signs a certificate at the delivery site. The airline then flies the aircraft to its base, changes the cabin, and completes its own entry checks. All of those days consume paid time.

**Leo:** Even if we negotiate a generous maintenance reimbursement for the work we complete later, that cannot change this starting date. If the aircraft is ready but the airline has no pilots available, the owner will resist a condition that lets the airline choose when payment starts.

**Mira:** Take a lease where rent starts on signed acceptance. The airline then needs a week to refit the cabin. What would have to be in the lease for the airline to avoid paying rent that week?

**Leo:** It has to be the agreed rent holiday covering the refit period. Acceptance already started the clock. Our internal forecast can identify the cost, but it cannot change the promise.

**Mira:** Correct. So back to your opening scenario. On delivery day a technical defect is discovered. When is the airline in the strongest position to secure a rent concession?

**Leo:** Before it signs the certificate, subject to its obligation to accept a conforming tender. You have to identify the failed delivery criterion and document the cure or concession before triggering rent.

**Mira:** Exactly. Now, read the rules for partial periods before assuming that half a month costs half a payment. One schedule might use the actual days in the period. Another specifies a thirty-day month, first and last days included or excluded.

**Leo:** And I have seen schedules that demand a full first payment on delivery regardless of the date. You have to reconstruct the dated amounts using the agreement's convention. If the first partial payment is credited against the next full instalment, you record the credit.

**Mira:** Ask the operator for the earliest date it can use the aircraft, then place the cash schedule alongside the operational schedule. Next, you have to follow each dollar.

**Leo:** Meaning we separate the invoice into basic rent, maintenance reserves, and other sums. A maintenance reserve is a usage-based payment against a defined class of future maintenance work. A security deposit covers specified failures to perform.

**Mira:** And even if a security deposit is expressed as several months of rent, it has a completely different job from rent. A deposit the airline expects to recover still needs funding. You need to model receipt, draw, replenishment, and refund as distinct events.

**Leo:** Keep an obligation register that links each event to its clause and evidence. Let us work a simplified first month to show why this matters. Say base rent is one hundred sixty thousand dollars, payable on delivery.

**Mira:** And assume a cash deposit equal to two months of base rent is due at the same time. Reserves at one hundred dollars per flight hour fall due at month end after two hundred hours of flying.

**Leo:** Okay, the first day's outflow is four hundred eighty thousand dollars. That is the one hundred sixty thousand base rent plus the three hundred twenty thousand deposit. Then the month-end outflow is twenty thousand dollars for the reserves.

**Mira:** So of the total five hundred thousand dollars, only one hundred sixty thousand is base rent. The deposit and reserves impose different recovery tests. If you combined them into one rental rate, it would hide the timing and make a competing lease difficult to compare.

**Leo:** Use the payment date for liquidity, then the purpose for economics. Let us try another hypothetical. Rent is one hundred ninety thousand dollars in advance, and three months of rent is payable as a deposit on delivery. No other sums are then due. How many dollars must treasury release on delivery?

**Mira:** One hundred ninety thousand, plus three times one hundred ninety thousand. That is seven hundred sixty thousand dollars. The deposit is additional cash even though it may be recoverable.

**Leo:** Suppose treasury gets the whole deposit back at maturity. What did the deposit still cost the airline?

**Mira:** It is the cost of funding cash while it is tied up. Funding has a time cost. Counting refunded principal as an expense would overstate the cumulative cost.

**Leo:** This is exactly why treasury teams ask who funds the security. They prefer posting a letter of credit instead of cash. A letter of credit is a bank undertaking to pay against a demand satisfying its terms.

**Mira:** Yes, when the owner accepts a letter of credit, a bank undertakes to pay against a complying demand. But you must review the bank's instrument as a separate promise. Look at expiry, drawing documents, currency, and the bank's place for presentation.

**Leo:** Because an agreement in the lease to accept the airline's existing bank means little if the bank refuses the required wording. I also tell teams to inspect the airline's collateral arrangement with the bank.

**Mira:** Why? If the letter is fully backed by cash, it may release little working capital and still attract fees.

**Leo:** Exactly. And a committed credit line may preserve cash but use borrowing capacity needed for another aircraft. We need to calculate the actual savings. Compare a fully refundable six hundred thousand dollar deposit held for one year with an unsecured bank letter costing twelve thousand dollars for the same year.

**Mira:** Let us assume a five percent simple annual cash funding cost, with no other fees or interest. Cash funding costs six hundred thousand times zero point zero five, which is thirty thousand dollars.

**Leo:** Subtract the twelve thousand dollar bank fee to save eighteen thousand dollars. That eighteen thousand is the economic rationale for the letter.

**Mira:** But the letter introduces expiry risk. Say a bank letter expires three months before lease end. The owner has agreed to accept letters from this bank. Which remaining issue affects continuity of security?

**Leo:** Renewal notice, replacement timing, and a draw right on non-renewal. Approval of the issuer does not extend the instrument. An expiry gap is addressed in the lease and the letter.

**Mira:** And from the owner's seat, a deposit reduces exposure to arrears and transition costs. They may want to use it for several aircraft under related agreements. If the clause reaches obligations under other contracts, a dispute elsewhere can immobilise this aircraft's deposit.

**Leo:** That cross-collateralization is dangerous. We have to define the particular contracts, the kind of breach, and whether the owner can apply security immediately or only after notice and an opportunity to fix the breach.

**Mira:** A draw is an application of cash security or a demand under a letter. Once money is applied, check when the airline must restore the balance. That is the top-up obligation.

**Leo:** We also seek a release mechanism tied to verifiable events, which is called a step-down. An airline with improving credit might propose reducing three months to two after twelve timely payments, provided there is no continuing specified default and a replacement letter remains in force.

**Mira:** But an owner financing its fleet may need its lender's consent for that step-down. You have to establish who can grant the release and what evidence its credit committee accepts.

**Leo:** Put an outside date on return of undisputed amounts and retain only a documented estimate for open claims. Price the value of the reduction against any increase in base rent. How would you distinguish an expected deposit refund from money the airline can draw today?

**Mira:** You show the contractual refund test, date, counterparty, and security status. Treasury still needs cash or bank capacity until release. Which brings us to tracing the deposit if delivery falls through.

**Leo:** This is a painful scenario. In a hypothetical lease, treasury has wired two hundred forty thousand dollars and posted a ninety thousand dollar bank letter. The owner must refund the cash and surrender the letter if the aircraft is lost before acceptance.

**Mira:** The clause says when both are due after a missed delivery deadline, but gives no date in the separate loss provision. Meanwhile, the bank keeps the full amount charged against the airline's credit line until it confirms cancellation.

**Leo:** Right. So the owner refunds the two hundred forty thousand dollars on Tuesday. What can treasury use on Wednesday if the bank still shows the letter as outstanding?

**Mira:** Only the cash. The refund restores two hundred forty thousand dollars of cash. The ninety thousand still consumes borrowing capacity.

**Leo:** To fix that, you ask the owner for the release the bank requires and get the bank's cancellation confirmation before counting that capacity. Put a deadline for both actions in the loss provision. The missed-delivery deadline applies to a different event.

**Mira:** You can try the first step on a filed lease. Section 3.1 points to Schedule B, Part II. The schedule distinguishes the deposit already received from the instalment payable at delivery. Even if the figures are redacted, you can identify which instalment would need tracing if delivery fell through.

**Leo:** Let us move to the operational nightmare: who pays when the aircraft stops flying? I have seen operators assume that if the engine fails, they stop paying rent.

**Mira:** That hits the hell-or-high-water obligation. It makes scheduled payments continue despite listed disruptions. Suspending service due to an engine defect is an operating event. It does not itself discharge a promise to pay independent of aircraft condition.

**Leo:** So what changes the result? An express rent suspension, a contractual termination, or a completed loss settlement. You have to identify which event switches off future payments and which amounts accrued first.

**Mira:** Exactly. Remedies for defects may run against a manufacturer under separate warranty rights. The timing of that claim can differ sharply from the next rent date.

**Leo:** Let us test that. An airline has a valid damages claim. Rent is absolute after delivery and set-off is prohibited. No exception or mandatory rule applies. What position should treasury forecast?

**Mira:** Gross rent due and a separate potential recovery. Set-off is the deduction of a counterclaim from an amount otherwise payable. If the lease bars set-off, the airline may have to pay the invoice and pursue its claim separately.

**Leo:** A counterclaim can have value without paying the current invoice. Estimate timing and collectibility separately. Suppose the owner owes an approved maintenance reimbursement equal to one month's rent. The cash forecast should still show gross rent on its due date unless the owner has agreed a credit or the terms permit netting.

**Mira:** Because collecting the reimbursement two months later leaves a financing gap even if the net position eventually closes. You should confirm with counsel how mandatory law affects the particular promise.

**Leo:** So which evidence most directly supports a request for temporary rent relief on a particular tail? Is it the fact that the grounding occurred?

**Mira:** No, a grounding alone may leave rent payable. You need a signed amendment granting abatement for this defined grounding. Abatement is a contractual reduction or suspension of rent. The amendment addresses the specific payment and trigger.

**Leo:** A negotiation about this clause often starts in the wrong place. The owner has promised its financier a stream of payments and may be unable to offer a general abatement right.

**Mira:** So ask instead for a delivery standard that prevents the initial defect, a direct warranty claim, a defined credit for owner-caused delay, or a faster reimbursement timetable. Each targets a particular exposure.

**Leo:** And if another aircraft is available on better terms, document the timing and switching cost. A credible alternative gives weight to a narrowly priced request, especially if the owner faces an idle aircraft. Now, what about the actual mechanics of moving the money?

**Mira:** On a multi-currency operation, confirm which desk carries the exchange exposure between ticket receipts and the date the lease payment clears. A right to send an equivalent amount in another currency can still leave a shortfall if the conversion bank deducts fees.

**Leo:** Assign one person to verify net receipt and book the transfer early enough for both banking calendars. Tie that control to the event of default definition, including any special relief for a documented transfer-system problem.

**Mira:** Let us check those calendars. The invoice falls on a day when the lessee's bank is open and the receiving bank is closed. What resolves the due date?

**Leo:** The lease definition of business day and the payment adjustment convention. Both the calendar and the direction of adjustment may affect the last date for cleared funds.

**Mira:** When the airline asks for its money back, read the repayment condition. Does a continuing Default block the refund, or does it take an Event of Default? Those defined terms can have different triggers.

**Leo:** Those opening words qualify a payment back to the airline after compliant return or loss settlement in an SEC-filed lease. A calendar entry saying 'return deposit at lease end' omits several conditions.

**Mira:** A separate reference to Default can matter if a breach has occurred but a period to cure has yet to expire. Read both definitions in that agreement. You can see this in a filed aircraft lease, clauses 4.4(b) and 6.2, covering post-termination payments and security.

**Leo:** If a two-aircraft group wants each deposit released when its own aircraft is returned, which words deserve the closest attention?

**Mira:** The definition of secured obligations and any cross-contract application right. A broadly defined obligation can carry one tail's exposure into the other's release calculation.

**Leo:** So how do we prepare the issues list for the negotiating team? For each disputed amount, name the payor, recipient, currency, calculation, due date, bank-day adjustment, and late consequence.

**Mira:** Check the payment direction executed by the owner and any financier to whom rents are assigned. Independently authenticate account changes using an agreed process. A payment made to the wrong account may leave the lease obligation outstanding even when the airline acted promptly.

**Leo:** The separate fraud loss should be visible in the scenario memo, alongside the risk of interest, cure costs, and increased security. Then, write an instruction that the negotiating team can actually execute.

**Mira:** For example, retain the owner's unconditional basic rent in exchange for a documented deposit step-down after a credit test, separate treatment of disputed amounts, and a fixed deadline to release the excess.

**Leo:** Attach the cash comparison, the assumption on bank collateral, and the board's peak funding limit. Let us say the owner offers a deposit reduction but your bank demands cash collateral of the same amount for a replacement letter. What should the issues list show?

**Mira:** Recalculate working capital, fees, and draw conditions. A formal reduction can leave the airline's net liquidity unchanged. If the substitute letter consumes the same scarce capacity as cash, the proposed trade loses its rationale.

**Leo:** If the owner will hold the full sum throughout the term, ask how much rent would have to fall to cover the incremental funding cost. The instruction is simple: show me the first day we pay, the last day we pay, and what has to happen before the security comes back.

**Mira:** That is the perfect summary. Let us land with the three takeaways. One: a lease starts rent on signed acceptance, so document the cure or concession for any failed delivery criterion before you sign the certificate.

**Leo:** Two: calculate the real economic cost of a deposit by looking at the time cost of funding cash while it is tied up, not the refunded principal. Compare that funding cost against bank letter fees.

**Mira:** Three: a hell-or-high-water obligation means payments continue despite disruptions, and if set-off is barred, you forecast gross rent due while pursuing your damages claim separately. Target specific remedies rather than broad abatements.
