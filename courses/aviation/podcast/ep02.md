# Episode 2: What exactly did you buy, and at what price?

**Leo:** You sign a contract for a specific aircraft at a specific price, but months later, the delivery invoice arrives and the final number is a complete surprise. What exactly did you buy, and at what price?

**Mira:** A delivery invoice can follow the contract exactly and still surprise the buyer. To prevent that, you have to rebuild it from the signed configuration, the price date, the adjustment formula, and the usable credits.

**Leo:** I see this constantly from the fleet operations side. A buyer thinks they secured a fully equipped aircraft because they locked in a production slot. But a production slot just reserves a place in a build schedule.

**Mira:** Exactly. A slot does not tell you which seats, engines, software, communications equipment, or cabin layout will actually arrive. You need to buy the configuration you can operate. The specification is the agreed technical description.

**Leo:** So where does the technical team look to anchor that technical description in the contract?

**Mira:** Find its issue number and date, the document that incorporates it, and the list of deviations. You have to put the operational requirement right beside the promise. If you need a particular communications capability for a route, a schedule listing physical equipment is not enough.

**Leo:** Right, listing physical equipment leaves installation, software activation, or certification unresolved. Let's test this with a scenario. The marketing brochure promises cabin connectivity. The signed schedule identifies antenna hardware only. Which acceptance test actually addresses the buyer's business requirement?

**Mira:** Could the buyer sign for the hardware today and still be unable to use the service on the first flight?

**Leo:** It has to be the agreed end-to-end service test. The buyer needs an operational capability. A hardware count just proves installation. It leaves activation and integration totally unresolved.

**Mira:** To lock that down, build a small comparison table with four columns. Put the signed baseline in the first, an approved change in the second, the resulting configuration in the third, and the delivery test in the fourth. You must work at a level the technical team can check.

**Leo:** Saying 'premium cabin' is way too broad for an inspector.

**Mira:** Far too broad. A specified seat model, layout drawing and revision, installed quantity, and test protocol give the inspector something to actually verify. Ask who supplies the drawings and by when. If there is a conflict between a marketing rendering and the signed specification, turn that into an evidence request before your choice window closes.

**Leo:** Let's talk about where the equipment comes from. You have to distinguish an option—which is something offered at an extra price—from equipment already included in the base aircraft.

**Mira:** And critically, identify buyer-furnished equipment. Those are items procured by the buyer and supplied to the manufacturer for installation. The OEM may promise to integrate the item only if it arrives on time, fits the agreed interfaces, and comes with the necessary documentation.

**Leo:** This is a massive operational trap. If the buyer supplies the seats, who owns the problem when they arrive late?

**Mira:** You have to trace the promise to procure, the interface date, available relief for the manufacturer, and any recovery under the separate seat order. The purchase agreement alone may give the airline little leverage against a supplier it has failed to contract with directly. The two contracts can leave the buyer bearing a gap.

**Leo:** Someone must instruct the equipment supplier and pay the resulting invoice. Let's move to the timeline. What happens after the specification freeze?

**Mira:** The freeze date is the deadline after which an ordinary selection becomes a change to an agreed build. A change request should describe the new configuration, whether the existing selection is cancelled, the added or removed cost, and the effect on delivery.

**Leo:** People forget that work already performed can have a removal cost. Or a component ordered specially for this aircraft might be unusable on another. If a change request just says 'replace', does that cover everything?

**Mira:** No. If a request says 'replace', the price schedule still needs to show the treatment of the original item. You also need to check whether a deposit for the change counts toward its eventual price.

**Leo:** What about when the manufacturer wants to change something? They often seek unilateral rights to alter the design as production develops or a regulatory requirement changes.

**Mira:** A general promise of 'equivalent' equipment leaves a major question about what counts as equivalent. A right to give notice after installing a substitute is quite different from a right for the buyer to approve an adverse change before installation.

**Leo:** Let's test that. The manufacturer offers a substitute unit with identical performance and weight. The airline maintains all its other aircraft using a different spare. Which missing criterion matters most?

**Mira:** What would your maintenance team have to change to support the substitute?

**Leo:** It is interchangeability and the support burden. Two components can perform the exact same function yet require entirely different spares, crew training, or maintenance tools. Fleet standardisation can have value even where the aircraft's performance is unchanged.

**Mira:** Exactly. You need to ask which dimensions must stay within tolerance, who measures them, and who bears the integration cost. Look for the phrase 'interchangeability or replaceability requirements of the Specification'. A change can affect common spares even when it leaves the headline performance intact. See the specification change mechanism in the filed A330 purchase agreement, clause two.

**Leo:** But in a crowded production schedule, asking for a veto over every small engineering change is a bad move. You will waste bargaining power needed for changes that actually affect your operation.

**Mira:** That is why you treat supplier substitutions, mandatory regulatory changes, and discretionary buyer requests separately. For each, state who initiates it, which party can say no, who pays, whether schedule relief follows, and what happens if agreement never comes.

**Leo:** And you must insist on an updated configuration list that shows effectivity. Effectivity means the specific aircraft or serial numbers affected.

**Mira:** Let's look at a pricing error tied to that. A change request adds a four hundred thousand dollar interior for aircraft three only. Which entry should the buyer make before approving it? Assume the price covers removal because the request says 'replace'.

**Leo:** Do I just add four hundred thousand dollars to every aircraft with the same model name?

**Mira:** Absolutely not. The same model can have different configurations. A gross option price leaves the cost of removal or a credit for the displaced option unresolved. A change agreed for the last aircraft in a series can enter the wrong cost model if the spreadsheet applies it across the whole order.

**Leo:** So the correct entry is to record effectivity, the treatment of replaced work, the net price, and the agreed schedule effect. And you must save the signed approval and the schedule it replaced.

**Mira:** Remember, the manufacturer holds the pen when its team prepares the next draft. If its proposed option cannot be built in your slot, ask engineering to identify the last workable decision date and the configuration that will go down the line if you decline. Give your negotiator a choice it can actually sign.

**Leo:** Because a later slot might buy time for a different interior, but cost a season of flying. You have to weigh those against each other.

**Mira:** Let's look at Open Configuration Matters, letter 3194-02, sections one point two and two point two through two point four. The airline accepts an option proposal. Its finance team adds the full option price to the aircraft budget. Identify two records that still need changing and test the budget entry.

**Leo:** Which documents and amounts would you put in the sign-off pack?

**Mira:** First, amend Exhibit A in writing to record the agreed option. Second, track the revised performance guarantees due from the manufacturer within ninety days of acceptance and incorporate those revisions by written amendment.

**Leo:** And regarding the budget entry, both the aircraft basic price and the advance payment base price already include an estimate. You have to adjust each by the difference between the estimate and the actual price.

**Mira:** Correct. Adding the entire option price would count the estimate twice. And note, the filed estimate is redacted, so the filing alone cannot supply the adjustment.

**Leo:** Let's turn a base price into a delivery price. The base price is a price at a specified reference date and configuration. Escalation adjusts defined portions of it using a contractual formula.

**Mira:** You need to find the base month, reference indices, component weights, the lag between the measurement month and delivery, any minimum or maximum, and the rounding rule. An index is a published numerical measure selected by the parties. The contract can tell you to use an earlier month's value because the delivery month's figure is not yet available.

**Leo:** Read any rule for revised or discontinued index data. And put the formula and its source series beside the invoice rather than accepting a percentage stated in an email.

**Mira:** Let's start with a deliberately simple example in millions of US dollars. A base price of twenty rises by five percent, adding one. An option priced in delivery-date dollars adds two. A credit expressly deductible from this invoice subtracts one. What is the resulting aircraft price?

**Leo:** Twenty million base, plus one million escalation, plus two million for the option, less a one million invoice credit yields twenty-two million dollars. Only the base escalates in this example. The immediately usable invoice credit reduces cash due.

**Mira:** What if the option is itself subject to escalation?

**Leo:** Then you repeat the calculation using the option's own base date and formula. And if the buyer has already paid part of the price, subtract those advances only after calculating the agreed final price.

**Mira:** That is vital. An advance changes the amount still due. It does not make the aircraft cheaper.

**Leo:** Let's do a weighted formula. For a weighted formula, calculate ratios before adding components. A hypothetical aircraft has a thirty million dollar escalating base. Labour carries twenty-five percent weight and rises eight percent. Materials carry seventy-five percent and rise four percent. What is the escalation increment in millions?

**Mira:** The weighted change is zero point two five times eight percent, plus zero point seven five times four percent. That equals five percent. Apply it to thirty million to obtain one point five million dollars.

**Leo:** A quoted four percent increase in a covered amount can mean a smaller percentage increase in the whole invoice if part of the aircraft price is fixed.

**Mira:** Let's try one more. The base is eighteen million dollars at the reference date. Escalation adds four percent on that base only. A fixed one point three million dollar option and a zero point two million dollar invoice credit apply. What is the adjusted price before advances?

**Leo:** Eighteen million times one point zero four is eighteen point seven two million. Add the one point three million option, subtract the zero point two million credit. The adjusted price is nineteen point eight two million dollars.

**Mira:** And remember to record calculations at the precision in the agreement before rounding the invoice. What if a cap says 'three percent per year'? Can you just put three percent in the model for an aircraft due in three years?

**Leo:** No. You have to read the dates, whether annual increases compound, any catch-up, and whether the cap applies to the final price or a particular component. You must model the contractual sequence.

**Mira:** Let's look at what happens when delivery dates slip. Identify who pays for a later delivery. An aircraft due in June is delivered in November. If the formula uses the actual delivery month, later delivery can change the invoice even though the buyer never changed the specification.

**Leo:** You have to study the rules for seller delay, buyer delay, and delay outside both parties' control. Look for a reference month held at scheduled delivery in one case but moved to actual delivery in another.

**Mira:** On a falling index, the scheduled-month rule might actually favour the seller. Check for an escalation floor, which prevents the adjustment falling below a defined level. Ask for the input values and compare both months before assigning blame or a dollar amount.

**Leo:** Price protection takes several forms. The seller may fix the price of a defined option for a limited period. The buyer may seek a maximum cumulative increase before it can cancel.

**Mira:** Another bargain might switch to a fixed delivery month in return for timely buyer configuration choices. Test each change against the actual timetable. If the buyer intends to request a cabin redesign after the freeze, a promise tied to meeting all selection dates may offer little protection. Price the concession in the scenario where the buyer can actually comply.

**Leo:** Here is a scenario. A contract freezes the base aircraft escalation date for seller delay. A separate option letter indexes the cabin equipment to actual delivery. What belongs in the forecast? Do you freeze both prices because the aircraft is delivered once?

**Mira:** No. You use separate measurements unless an agreed precedence or amendment changes the option rule. The option letter can allocate a different exposure. Reconcile the scope of the freeze before applying it to the option.

**Leo:** Let's move to credits. How do you value a credit the buyer can actually use? A credit memorandum records an entitlement to offset a specified future charge or receive a benefit.

**Mira:** Read the permitted products and services, expiry, assignment restrictions, eligible group companies, and whether the buyer must pay cash first.

**Leo:** Suppose the OEM offers a five hundred thousand dollar spares credit that expires in a year. The buyer expects to purchase only one hundred and twenty thousand dollars of eligible parts before expiry and cannot transfer the balance. What is the maximum undiscounted benefit on that forecast?

**Mira:** Put yourself in procurement. How much cash would you actually avoid spending?

**Leo:** It is exactly one hundred and twenty thousand dollars. Only eligible expenditure can absorb the credit. The unused three hundred and eighty thousand dollars is the shortfall against its face value. And if the airline would otherwise buy equivalent parts more cheaply elsewhere, even that estimate needs adjusting.

**Mira:** What if the credit is transferable to 'Customer Affiliates', but the operator will be an independently owned airline? What changes in the model?

**Leo:** You have to confirm the contractual definition of affiliate, obtain consent, or remove that airline's purchases from eligible spend.

**Mira:** A training package requires the exact same discipline. Count available places, eligible courses, notice for booking or cancellation, language, location, and costs borne by the buyer. An offered simulator slot after the aircraft enters service can leave the airline paying for outside training now.

**Leo:** Read what happens if the manufacturer postpones a course or if the buyer's nominated crew fail entry requirements. Training capacity, service bulletins, technical publications, and spare-parts access can affect whether a delivered aircraft is commercially usable on day one. Put commencement, duration, and support contacts in an implementation list.

**Mira:** You must separate an invoice credit already reflected in price from a future conditional benefit. Counting it as both an invoice reduction and future savings gives the credit twice the value it can deliver.

**Leo:** Here is a classic treasury mistake. A credit memo pays only for eligible future training, but treasury wants to deduct it from the closing wire. Which issue needs resolution before sending funds?

**Mira:** You must resolve whether the documents allow set-off against that invoice or provide a separate entitlement. A restricted entitlement may leave the entire closing invoice payable. Deducting it from the closing payment without contractual authority could put the buyer in payment default. A short wire can have contractual consequences.

**Leo:** Where the agreement uses credits as compensation for delay, estimate the amount the buyer can absorb within the permitted period. A manufacturer may be more willing to extend the expiry or broaden eligible services than to pay cash. Compare these alternatives using the airline's actual procurement plan.

**Mira:** For an offer that expires, ask what the deadline measures. A part ordered before expiry might ship afterward. Procurement can count that purchase only if the letter makes the order date sufficient. Ask finance to test a sample invoice against the named purchaser, eligible items, and redemption event before it puts the credit into the fleet budget.

**Leo:** So how do you write a price instruction that can actually be tested?

**Mira:** Give the commercial lead a bridge from signed base price to cash due at delivery. Attach the dated specification, authorised change register, index observations, side letters, and advance receipts. Mark any inaccessible or redacted inputs as unknown.

**Leo:** What if an escalation coefficient is hidden in a filing, but an earlier order on the same model reveals a coefficient. What may you conclude?

**Mira:** Do you use the earlier coefficient as the signed term, treat the hidden coefficient as zero, or mark the hidden coefficient unknown and test assumptions separately?

**Leo:** You mark it unknown and test assumptions separately. The visible contract shows a possible mechanism, but a redaction does not reveal a number. A different agreement may show a plausible scenario without proving this buyer's deal. It would be misleading to offer a precise expected delivery price using a coefficient from another customer's agreement.

**Mira:** Use a sensitivity case with a clearly labelled assumption until the missing input can be verified. Put the largest negotiable exposures first. For a scarce, early slot, the OEM may have little incentive to grant a blanket cap.

**Leo:** But the buyer can still ask to measure escalation at the agreed month if the OEM alone delays delivery, or exchange a firm selection deadline for a fixed-price option. Describe the alternative slot, the deadline, and the cost of delay that make this trade credible.

**Mira:** The issues list should state which signed document implements each concession and who will check the final invoice against it. That last step turns the negotiated term into money the buyer can actually retain.

**Leo:** Time to wrap up. Three takeaways. One: Rebuild the delivery price from the base date, the exact escalation formula, and approved change requests. Do not accept a percentage increase stated in an email without running the weighted ratios yourself.

**Mira:** Two: A production slot is not a technical specification. Tie the operational requirement to a specific seat model, layout drawing, and test protocol before the choice window closes, and track effectivity so changes hit the right serial numbers.

**Leo:** Three: Value conditional credits based on the airline's actual procurement forecast before expiry, not face value. And never deduct a future credit from a closing wire without express contractual set-off rights.
