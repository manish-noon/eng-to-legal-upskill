# Episode 17: Three mandates at the deal desk

**Leo:** Treasury is ready to wire the price for a used aircraft. The inspection is over and the liens have been released. The seller is putting money into escrow for missing parts. Would you tell the buyer to release its wire?

**Mira:** Welcome to 'Three mandates at the deal desk'. Today we are walking through three fictional client mandates to show exactly how you convert landed terms into a priced recommendation. We are going to start with the spare airframe.

**Leo:** This is a used purchase with a headline price of four million eight hundred fifty thousand dollars. The buyer has an alternative with equivalent verified value, setting a firm baseline. The board has a strict value ceiling of five million one hundred thousand dollars.

**Mira:** First set the ninety thousand dollars in known transaction costs beside the price. Together they use four million nine hundred forty thousand dollars of the ceiling, before allowing for any missing parts.

**Leo:** The physical inspection and the lien releases are already complete. As a reader, you have to separate those cleared hurdles from what remains open. A clean title search yesterday does not pay for a missing component tomorrow.

**Mira:** That distinction is critical for a document reader. When you check the closing file, you verify the executed release documents to confirm the encumbrances are truly gone. You never let a completed physical check bleed into your assessment of the remaining parts shortfall.

**Leo:** The open issue here is exactly that specific parts shortfall. If that shortfall materializes, it can cost three hundred sixty thousand dollars. We are not guessing at other maintenance events or adding hypothetical expenses to the board's plate.

**Mira:** The buyer puts the probability of that shortfall at twenty-five percent. That is our operational estimate for the risk. The seller is solvent and has agreed to fund one hundred twenty thousand dollars of that loss through escrow.

**Leo:** Let us pause and look at the two different limits you have to clear. The first is the expected cost, which determines if the deal makes economic sense. The second is the cash required in the bad state, which tests actual liquidity.

**Mira:** We calculate the expected kept risk first. The gross loss is three hundred sixty thousand dollars. The seller's escrow covers one hundred twenty thousand dollars, leaving a net residual loss of two hundred forty thousand dollars if the shortfall happens.

**Leo:** You multiply that two hundred forty thousand dollar residual loss by the twenty-five percent probability. That gives us an expected kept risk of sixty thousand dollars. We have to add that figure directly to our baseline costs.

**Mira:** So our effective cost is the four million eight hundred fifty thousand dollar price, plus the ninety thousand dollars in closing costs, plus that sixty thousand dollars of expected kept risk. That totals exactly five million dollars.

**Leo:** Our ceiling is five million one hundred thousand dollars. Subtracting the five million dollar effective cost leaves one hundred thousand dollars of headroom on an expected-value basis. That answers what the buyer can afford to pay on average. How much cash would it need on a bad day?

**Mira:** But the math is only half the job when you are reading these agreements. Expected cost is just a probability-weighted average used for valuation. If the shortfall actually happens, the supplier does not send an invoice for twenty-five percent of the parts.

**Leo:** They bill the full three hundred sixty thousand dollars. The client has to fund the two hundred forty thousand dollar downside out of pocket, assuming the escrow pays the rest. The brief explicitly states the board accepts this full downside and has the cash for it.

**Mira:** Now we have to interrogate that escrow premise. We are relying on one hundred twenty thousand dollars from a solvent seller. I want the listener to pause right now and actually say the escrow instruction out loud. What releases that money?

**Leo:** If you just say the seller covers it, you are assuming a legal effect without examining the document. Does the escrow agreement require joint written instructions to release the funds, or can the buyer draw unilaterally upon presenting an invoice?

**Mira:** If a countersignature is needed, read the dispute procedure. Who holds the cash while the parties disagree, and what ultimately authorises the agent to release it? A right to recover money and a wire the buyer can send to the supplier today are different things.

**Leo:** Is it a quote from a repair station, a purchase order for the parts, or a final paid invoice? If the buyer has to buy the parts first to get reimbursed, they need to float the entire three hundred sixty thousand dollars.

**Mira:** Check the deadline for a claim. If the buyer can discover or prove the shortfall only after the claim window closes, it may lose this protection. Does timely notice preserve the claim while final evidence is gathered?

**Leo:** That is a concrete change we could propose. Give the buyer time to discover the shortfall and a defined route to finish a timely claim. Check who holds the money, who controls its release and what evidence the agent must accept.

**Mira:** Those are things to verify. Counsel can check whether a supplier quote supports a draw, whether the buyer must first pay an invoice, and what happens if the seller objects. For the calculation, the stated one hundred twenty thousand dollars is collectible.

**Leo:** And if the proven claim is smaller than the amount in escrow? Follow the instructions for the surplus. Ask when it goes back to the seller and whether any other agreed claim can use the same balance before this one is paid.

**Mira:** Exactly. You can propose reserving that amount for the parts exposure. Then show the client whether the draft actually does so. Two claims cannot each treat the same limited pot of money as a full recovery.

**Leo:** Once we verify the demand evidence and collectibility, we can finalize the recommendation. We have separated the completed lien releases from the pending parts issue. We have quantified both the expected cost and the downside cash.

**Mira:** Let us review the final arithmetic for the client. The expected kept risk is sixty thousand dollars. The current terms leave exactly one hundred thousand dollars of headroom below the five million one hundred thousand dollar ceiling.

**Leo:** The buyer has an equivalent alternative with verified value. That gives it a credible way to decline a worse draft. Here the board has already accepted the funded two hundred forty thousand dollar downside, and the agreed terms fit the ceiling.

**Mira:** The mandate is satisfied on both the economic value and the liquidity constraints. Assuming the escrow instructions check out and the funds are verified, your verdict here is clear. You instruct the client to sign.

**Leo:** We are picking up right as the call comes in from the airline’s fleet desk. They are looking at a bridge lease, strictly an operating lease, and they have a credible competing offer. The non-cancellable rent has a present value of seven point eight million dollars.

**Mira:** Before examining the return conditions, we establish the budget. The client has an absolute value ceiling of eight point zero seven million dollars. This is our hard constraint before we factor in any unresolved risks. We already know the closing and transaction costs are sitting at one hundred forty thousand dollars.

**Leo:** That leaves a specific margin for the return conditions. Expected return-condition loss must be priced separately from base rent. My engineering team sees a forty percent chance of a seven hundred fifty thousand dollar invoice when we hand this aircraft back.

**Mira:** We need to look at the maintenance reserves already baked into the lease. The drafting shows two hundred fifty thousand dollars is collectible from eligible reserves in that specific return state. The reserve payments are already in the base package.

**Leo:** Let us distinguish reserve payments from an eligible reimbursement. Paying reserves does not establish which works qualify or when the airline gets cash back. Read the eligibility test, claim documents and payment timetable. Here we are told what is collectible in the loss state.

**Mira:** Exactly, and we must separate the gross invoice from the cash timing. The drafting might require the airline to settle that seven hundred fifty thousand dollar invoice out of pocket first, before claiming the two hundred fifty thousand dollars back.

**Leo:** If the airline has to pay first, the gross invoice sets the initial funding peak. Check the available cash or facility against that date. The mandate tells us the client accepts the tail if the overall expected cost meets its ceiling.

**Mira:** Then let us run the arithmetic on that expected kept risk. The gross loss is seven hundred fifty thousand dollars, minus the two hundred fifty thousand dollar collectible recovery. That leaves a net residual loss of five hundred thousand dollars.

**Leo:** We apply the forty percent probability to that five hundred thousand dollar net loss. That gives us exactly two hundred thousand dollars of expected kept risk. We do not discount the probability just because they have cash for the tail.

**Mira:** Now we build the effective cost bridge. We take the seven point eight million dollars in present-value rent, add the one hundred forty thousand dollars in costs, and add that two hundred thousand dollars of expected kept risk.

**Leo:** That totals eight point one four million dollars in effective cost. Compare that directly to the client ceiling of eight point zero seven million dollars. We are exactly seventy thousand dollars over the mandate. We cannot sign this draft.

**Mira:** Let us take that to the table. I'll take the airline side. You have the lessor's authority. We need seventy thousand dollars of value on the same present-value basis. Try offering me something that sounds like seventy thousand but pays less.

**Leo:** I am the lessor. I hear your budget constraints, but I cannot rewrite the return conditions. Instead, I will offer you a seventy thousand dollar credit applied directly against that final return invoice, only if the discrepancy actually occurs.

**Mira:** As the airline, I reject that structure entirely. A conditional seventy thousand dollar credit only pays out forty percent of the time. That reduces our expected kept risk by twenty-eight thousand dollars, leaving us forty-two thousand dollars over our ceiling.

**Leo:** That is the difference between a promise triggered only by a loss and a reduction in scheduled rent. In that hypothetical counteroffer we are still forty-two thousand dollars short. We can use the calculation to explain why the airline is asking for another structure.

**Mira:** Back in character as the airline, I tell the lessor that contingent protection does not solve our mandate. We require a minimum of seventy thousand dollars in unconditional present-value rent credit. You can take it out of base rent.

**Leo:** As lessor, I would look to the rent schedule. I have authority for a credit worth up to ninety thousand dollars in present value. A front-end rent holiday is one way I could propose delivering it. The numbers depend on the dates we put in the schedule.

**Mira:** In the issues list, distinguish that authority from a concession actually made. Record the request, the minimum credit the client can accept and the schedule awaiting revision. Keep it open until the unconditional reduction is in the operative terms.

**Leo:** Read the revised schedule against that entry. Which instalment comes down, by how much and on what date? If the credit moves to a later instalment, calculate its value again before marking the issue agreed.

**Mira:** Yes. A nominal credit paid later may buy less than the same nominal amount now. Use the client's valuation date and rate, then check that the resulting saving still reaches seventy thousand dollars. Don't count the credit again in the return claim.

**Leo:** Let us summarize the minimum acceptable credit. The effective cost exceeds the ceiling by seventy thousand dollars. Therefore, the absolute floor for this negotiation is seventy thousand dollars in verified, unconditional present-value savings. Anything less, and the mandate fails.

**Mira:** We know the lessor can authorise ninety thousand dollars in present-value credit. That gives a feasible route past the seventy thousand dollar minimum. Authority alone does not tell us whether the lessor will offer the full amount.

**Leo:** We also confirm the client understands the tail risk they are accepting. Even with the rent credit bringing expected cost under the ceiling, that forty percent chance of a five hundred thousand dollar net cash outflow at return remains real.

**Mira:** The client accepts the tail if we meet its ceiling. Record that instruction and separately get treasury to confirm the dated cash requirement, particularly if the gross invoice falls due before the reserve claim is paid.

**Leo:** The distinction matters even for a well-funded airline. A facility may let the airline pay the invoice while its board still rejects an overpriced package. An acceptable expected cost can also hide a temporary cash shortfall. Ask for two answers.

**Mira:** We have a credible competing offer. Compare it on the same basis, including return obligations. That gives the negotiator a reason for the rent request and a real alternative if the owner offers only a contingent credit.

**Leo:** Let us talk execution steps. You write the instruction for the negotiator. It reads: require an unconditional present-value rent credit of at least seventy thousand dollars. Do not accept contingent maintenance credits as a substitute. The available ninety thousand dollars is enough.

**Mira:** The verdict is negotiate. The effective cost exceeds the ceiling by seventy thousand dollars. We require at least that much unconditional present-value credit. We instruct the client to execute that specific trade at the table, and we reopen the rent schedule on the next draft.

**Leo:** We are looking at a finance lease where title sits with a financing vehicle, and the prior creditor just refused to release its interest. They also refused to consent to quiet enjoyment. For an airline operator, putting an aircraft on a critical route with a superior interest hanging over it is a massive, unacceptable exposure.

**Mira:** Even if the airline pays its rent, it could lose the aircraft if the prior creditor enforces. Counsel has to check the creditor's actual rights and the applicable procedure. The board requires protection binding on that creditor, through a release or consent to quiet enjoyment.

**Leo:** So we cannot just rely on a covenant from the lessor saying they will keep the creditor at bay. We need the creditor’s actual signature on a document binding them to respect our lease. Without that, the operator will lose its route if the aircraft is taken away.

**Mira:** Right, and we cannot imply a legal effect from an unexamined document or a vague commercial promise. If the creditor has not signed a waiver, the risk remains active. Let us look at how the deal desk initially modeled the ordinary equipment loss, which entirely excluded this title problem.

**Leo:** The ordinary model starts with a six million dollar base cost for the finance lease and one hundred ten thousand dollars in transaction costs. The gross equipment loss is five hundred fifty thousand dollars, with a collectible recovery of three hundred fifty thousand dollars.

**Mira:** They assigned a fifteen percent probability to that ordinary equipment loss. Subtracting the three hundred fifty thousand dollar recovery from the gross loss leaves two hundred thousand dollars. Weighting that by fifteen percent gives an ordinary expected kept risk of thirty thousand dollars.

**Leo:** Adding the six million dollar price, the one hundred ten thousand dollars in closing costs, and that thirty thousand dollar kept risk brings the effective cost to six million, one hundred forty thousand dollars. The client has a value ceiling of six million, three hundred fifty thousand dollars.

**Mira:** That arithmetic leaves two hundred ten thousand dollars of headroom. If you only look at the spreadsheet, this looks like a highly favorable transaction. But that ordinary model completely omits the route loss that would trigger if the prior creditor repossesses the airframe.

**Leo:** A discount changes the economics of the lease. It does not answer whether this aircraft will be available to fly the route. The board has put that question outside the authority of the person negotiating rent.

**Mira:** The board recognized that. They issued a strict veto stating there will be no closing without the creditor protection. A price concession does not buy back the route, and a discount cannot waive a mandate that the board explicitly reserved.

**Leo:** How do you answer the lessor if they point to the two hundred ten thousand dollars of headroom and propose a price cut? They may say it ought to compensate the operator for the risk of the creditor stepping in.

**Mira:** You tell them the board veto is binary. We ask if they can propose alternative drafting or a different financing vehicle holding clean title. If they offer a discount, you clarify that economic headroom cannot cure a legal defect breaching a non-waivable mandate.

**Leo:** What evidence would actually change our advice in a counterfactual? Suppose the lessor calls and says the creditor has changed its mind. What exactly do we need to see before we can advise closing?

**Mira:** We would need to review the actual executed quiet enjoyment letter or the recorded lien release from the prior creditor. We must verify that the signature block matches the entity holding the superior interest, ensuring no other conditions in their document undermine the protection.

**Leo:** But in our actual scenario, counsel confirms this protection cannot be obtained by the final slot date. The airline has a hard deadline to commit this aircraft to the schedule. The slot deadline defeats any alternative route to curing the title defect here.

**Mira:** Counsel has checked the fixed slot date. Under this mandate the required protection cannot be delivered in time. Taking delivery first and hoping to obtain the signature later would expose the very route the board told us to protect.

**Leo:** So when I read this lease, I mark two separate lines. One concerns the lessor's promises. The other concerns the creditor that can disturb possession. Only the second line answers the question the board asked.

**Mira:** That is why the verdict here is to walk. The unresolved superior interest breaches a non-waivable mandate. The ordinary expected kept risk of thirty thousand dollars is irrelevant when the board has flatly prohibited closing without the creditor protection.

**Leo:** On the client call, I am leading with that verdict. I would say the arithmetic shows two hundred ten thousand dollars of headroom, but counsel confirms the prior creditor will not sign before our slot deadline. Therefore, we must decline the aircraft.

**Mira:** You also explain why we are not countering with a lower price. You tell the client that accepting a discount violates their own board veto. It is crucial to show we respected the boundary between a commercial variable and a mandatory policy.

**Leo:** What if the lessor suggests holding the rent in escrow until it gets the signature? That would control where the money goes. Would it give the airline the right to keep flying if the creditor enforced?

**Mira:** No. The rent account would not bind the creditor to respect possession. Under this instruction we need the protection in time for closing. The proposed substitute leaves the airline exposed on day one.

**Leo:** That makes sense. If the operator loses the route because the aircraft is taken, an escrowed rent payment does not cover the lost revenue. The deal desk must identify the maximum payable price, but they also have to identify the hard stops.

**Mira:** Exactly. We walk away, and we document for the file that the unbridgeable gap was a legal veto, not an economic shortfall. Let us end this module with three takeaways, drawing one from each mandate we have covered today.

**Leo:** The first takeaway is from the spare airframe. When the two hundred forty thousand dollar downside is funded, the expected kept risk is sixty thousand dollars, and current terms leave one hundred thousand dollars of headroom, you sign.

**Mira:** The second is the bridge lease. When effective cost exceeds the ceiling by seventy thousand dollars, you negotiate. Require at least that much unconditional present-value credit, and since the available ninety thousand dollars is enough, you can close the gap. Remember that credit is not automatically cash.

**Leo:** And the third takeaway is our encumbered delivery. You walk when the unresolved superior interest breaches a non-waivable mandate. An uncured veto that threatens the route cannot be fixed with a spreadsheet adjustment.
