# Episode 10: Casualty and default

**Leo:** Casualty and default. We are looking at what happens when the metal breaks, or when the money stops.

**Mira:** And we are tracing the exact path from the first notice of damage, all the way to settlement or enforcement. You can identify the person with a claim, the source of recovery, and the cash the airline must produce before that recovery actually arrives.

**Leo:** Let us start with a scenario that terrifies operators. The airline pays its rent on time, every month. But the lessor defaults on its own loan, and the lessor's bank seeks to enforce against the aircraft. What document most directly addresses continued possession against that bank? Is it the monthly utilisation report? The airline's cabin warranty?

**Mira:** Neither. It is the bank's signed non-disturbance undertaking and its conditions. A promise solely from the lessor may leave the airline with only a claim against a distressed owner. A promise from the bank can constrain its own action.

**Leo:** Quiet enjoyment is the concept here. It is a promise about interference with the airline's permitted use and possession. Its value depends entirely on who makes it, who is bound through that party, and when it stops.

**Mira:** Exactly. If the lessor's financier has a mortgage over the aircraft, the airline needs to know whether that financier has given a direct undertaking and what conditions apply. You must obtain the signed form applicable to this aircraft and identify any later transferee who must accede to it.

**Leo:** What if the airline subleases the aircraft? A permitted sublease has been approved by the lessor. Does the suboperator automatically enjoy the same promise of quiet enjoyment from the financier?

**Mira:** No. You must check who is named or included in the direct undertaking and the conditions for protection. Permission to sublease alone does not answer that question.

**Leo:** So if you are reading a filed aircraft lease, and you see an acknowledgement pursuant to section ten point two b, you need to read the precise trigger for the loss of protection.

**Mira:** Yes. Look at this operative text: 'so long as no Event of Default has occurred and is continuing'. These words qualify the quiet possession undertaking in a filed financing acknowledgement.

**Leo:** Notice the capital letters. Event of Default. Do I just infer what that means because it is capitalized?

**Mira:** Absolutely not. Locate the definitions rather than inferring them from capitals. Some documents use these words differently or subject the promise to other exceptions. A default can mean an underlying breach before notice or time to cure has run.

**Leo:** Whereas an Event of Default can mean a more mature breach which already satisfies the stated conditions for remedies. That distinction is critical. A quiet enjoyment letter that falls away on any outstanding default could matter even when the lessee has the money to cure within a grace period.

**Mira:** Precisely. And look at the phrase 'and is continuing'. You must ask whether the breach still exists, whose breach it is, and whether an earlier cured breach matters under this wording. A service interruption alone seldom tells you who broke which promise.

**Leo:** Let us shift to how we draw the insurance recovery. You have hull insurance, which concerns physical loss or damage to the aircraft. And you have liability insurance, which addresses covered liability to others, such as passengers or third parties.

**Mira:** The roles here are frequently confused. An additional insured is granted specified cover under the policy. A loss payee is designated to receive specified proceeds. Being named in one capacity does not automatically create rights in the other.

**Leo:** So if a financier wants direct payment of hull proceeds on a total loss, which policy role addresses that payment instruction? Is it the broker? Or the aircraft operator on the flight plan?

**Mira:** It is the loss payee under the relevant endorsement. Review the loss payable endorsement and settlement mechanics. A broker may place coverage but does not thereby become its beneficiary.

**Leo:** A lot of operators just rely on the broker's certificate. They file it away and assume they are covered.

**Mira:** That is dangerous. A broker's certificate can record the intended arrangements but cannot cure an exclusion in the policy. You must obtain the policy endorsements incorporating the required terms and compare them with the lease schedule.

**Leo:** And the exclusions can be highly specific. You inspect amounts, territories, excluded operations, deductibles, expiry, cancellation notice, and the treatment of particular risks. War risk, for example, can require separate coverage.

**Mira:** Compare the insured aircraft and engines by identifier with the property definition, including removed engines or spare parts where relevant. An airline changing routes or placing an engine with a maintenance provider could move outside an assumption on which the cover was priced.

**Leo:** If I am moving that engine, I need to ask the broker for written confirmation of the particular operation. Because if the insurance fails, the party making an indemnity promise may still owe the other party.

**Mira:** Let us work one example from first loss to final bank receipt. This is where the payment clock and the insurance clock misalign. Assume a total loss payment of twenty-four million dollars.

**Leo:** Okay, loss on Day zero. The lease requires twenty-four million dollars at Day thirty.

**Mira:** And you have an admitted insurance claim of twenty-three million dollars, but it is payable to the owner in sixty days. Ignore other amounts and assume the insurer's proceeds are fully credited or refunded against settlement.

**Leo:** The final economic shortfall is one million dollars. But that is just the math at the end of the day. The treasury reality is that the airline could need up to twenty-four million dollars at Day thirty if it must pay first and has no bridge from the insurer.

**Mira:** Exactly. A later insurance receipt leaves a funding need at the contractual settlement date. You must distinguish replacement capacity and continuing rent from both of those numbers.

**Leo:** Let us run another quick calculation. Total loss requires eighteen million, eight hundred thousand dollars. You have seventeen million, nine hundred thousand dollars in insurance credited at settlement. Ignoring rent and fees, what additional amount in dollars is still required?

**Mira:** Eighteen million, eight hundred thousand, less seventeen million, nine hundred thousand, equals nine hundred thousand dollars.

**Leo:** Right. Nine hundred thousand dollars is the shortfall. Now, we need to distinguish damage from an event of loss. People use those words interchangeably, but they are completely different contractual triggers.

**Mira:** A casualty is an incident involving loss or damage. An event of loss is a defined contractual condition that can trigger a special settlement or replacement process. You have to read the definition for permanent destruction, constructive total loss, prolonged deprivation, and compulsory acquisition.

**Leo:** Because they start the clock on different dates and require different proof. Temporary grounding for repairs may just continue under the repair regime. Even if a component has been destroyed, the aircraft as a whole may remain subject to the lease.

**Mira:** Record the affected object before calculating the amount due. Take a destroyed engine. The airframe is undamaged, but the engine is destroyed and can be replaced. What decides whether scheduled aircraft rent stops? Is it an assumption that all insurance write-offs terminate rent?

**Leo:** No. It is the separate engine-loss and aircraft-loss provisions, including the rent covenant. Object-specific remedies may require replacement while aircraft rent continues.

**Mira:** For a lost engine, the remedy may require a replacement of the specified model, utility, condition, and title quality. Read whether owner approval is required and whether any encumbrances must be released before title vests. A cheque equal to the estimated value may be insufficient to perform that obligation.

**Leo:** What about repairable damage? The operator can face a significant funding gap even under a policy that covers the damage in full. You have to determine who approves repairs, whether the owner holds insurance proceeds as security, and when they are released to pay the shop.

**Mira:** And if it is a total aircraft loss, compare the agreed value schedule with the insurance actually recoverable on the date in question. The agreed value is the contractual amount specified for the relevant loss settlement.

**Leo:** Check the settlement deadline, any earlier deadline triggered by receipt of proceeds, continuing rent, arrears, and rights in surviving parts. If insurance pays the security trustee—the person who holds security for financiers—identify how its receipt discharges the airline.

**Mira:** Require a receipt or statement showing amounts applied. If the airline pays before the insurer, track its right to receive later proceeds and any insurer rights to recover or take salvage.

**Leo:** Let us say a hull insurer accepts the claim but has yet to pay. Does acceptance stop rent under a lease that stops rent upon payment in full?

**Mira:** No. Trace actual receipt and discharge. Acceptance of insurance liability alone has not met the stated condition.

**Leo:** Let us do a read-along for a temporary government requisition. In this hypothetical lease, an authority takes the aircraft for temporary use. The airline still owes rent. The authority pays a hire fee for the days of use and separately pays the owner for damage.

**Mira:** Before crediting either payment against rent, find who is entitled to each. Assume the airline receives the hire fee, the owner reimburses documented repairs from the damage payment, and no default exists.

**Leo:** The airline must still book the shop visit and fund the invoice until reimbursement. You need to give treasury three exact dates: when rent falls due, when the hire fee arrives, and when the owner releases repair money.

**Mira:** And if the aircraft stays away longer, reopen the event-of-loss definition and check when the temporary-use rules give way to a different settlement. Let us move to how we bound the indemnity.

**Leo:** An indemnity is an obligation to bear covered losses or expenses of a protected person. You have to identify the protected people, the activities creating exposure, and the exclusions.

**Mira:** One clause may cover operating claims by third parties. Another may shift taxes or financing losses. The beneficiary may include the owner, a trustee, lenders, and their employees. Check whether new financiers enter automatically and whether a transfer can increase the airline's burden.

**Leo:** A claimant named in the indemnity can still need to prove causation and its actual losses. But what about the timing? A claimant sues after the aircraft has been returned, over an incident that happened during the lease term. What is the best first test of an indemnity demand?

**Mira:** Do not reject it solely on the claim date. And do not accept it just because all indemnities last forever. You must check the covered event, survival language, exclusions, and claims procedure.

**Leo:** Survival is the continuation of an obligation after expiry or termination. Incident and demand dates serve different functions. The words decide whether the late claim falls within scope. 'Arising from operation during the term' may cover a claim asserted later for an incident during possession.

**Mira:** Review exclusions for the protected person's wrongdoing, pre-delivery events, and events occurring after compliant return. Match insurance consequences carefully. Recoveries actually received can reduce reimbursable loss if the wording requires credit.

**Leo:** An obligation to pursue available insurance should assign responsibility, cost, timing, and control. And control is huge. A right to settle a claim without consulting the paying party may enlarge the bill.

**Mira:** Let us test that. The airline agrees to defend an operating claim. A proposed settlement also bars its use of a profitable route. Which negotiated protection matters most? Is it a promise to pay settlement cash quickly? Or more frequent rent invoices?

**Leo:** Neither. It is consent before a settlement imposes a non-monetary obligation on the airline. A cash-only cap misses the effect on operations. A settlement-control provision can protect the route decision. You want consent for admissions and material settlements.

**Mira:** In a sample issues list, the airline accepts responsibility for passenger claims arising during its operation but resists covering an owner's unrelated financing dispute. Draw a boundary using the event, the protected person, and the causal connection to the aircraft.

**Leo:** Specify when the airline takes over the defence and whether separate counsel can bill it. A cheap settlement can become expensive if it restricts future operations or admits a breach in another proceeding. Reserve consent over these non-cash consequences.

**Mira:** Ask for immediate notice of claims, preservation of evidence, and consultation over offers. Decide whether late notice reduces recovery only to the extent it prejudices the defence.

**Leo:** Let us run the numbers for the board model on an indemnity. Assume a covered claim costs three hundred and ten thousand dollars. The indemnity credits two hundred and forty thousand dollars of insurance actually received for the same claim, with no other offsets.

**Mira:** At a twenty percent scenario probability, what expected retained loss in dollars belongs in the airline's model?

**Leo:** The conditional shortfall is seventy thousand dollars. Multiply by zero point two zero to get fourteen thousand dollars. That is your expected retained loss. Cap any assumed reimbursement at amounts attributable to the covered claim, and test a scenario in which a defence succeeds after substantial fees have already been paid.

**Mira:** Now let us look at the path from late payment to a remedy. Write a dated sequence for each alleged breach. Does the event arise on the due date, on notice, on expiry of a cure period, or after a materiality threshold is crossed?

**Leo:** A cure period is the contractual window to fix a breach before a specified consequence. Which days count, what address is valid, and what proof of receipt exists? Non-payment, an insurance lapse, and breach of a reporting promise may all have different cure mechanics.

**Mira:** Here is a classic trap. Rent default requires notice and five business days to cure after receipt. Notice has been drafted but never delivered; no other event exists. Has this event of default matured? Can the owner ignore the agreed procedure?

**Leo:** No, the stipulated notice and elapsed cure period are missing. Drafting starts nothing. A missed payment may already be a breach, but this remedy trigger has further conditions.

**Mira:** What about a cross-acceleration trigger? It requires third-party debt above a threshold to have been declared immediately due. The debt is overdue but no declaration has been made. Does this trigger apply on those facts? Is every missed payment an acceleration?

**Leo:** No, the separate declaration condition remains unsatisfied. The agreed test requires acceleration. A cross-default test might have a different result, linking another agreement's failure to this aircraft. But cross-acceleration requires the other creditor to accelerate its debt first.

**Mira:** So, how would you tell the board that a notice defect matters without suggesting the airline can ignore its unpaid rent?

**Leo:** You give the amount due, the current breach status, the next valid remedy trigger, and the immediate cure instruction as separate facts. You strip the emotion out of it.

**Mira:** If the event matures, the owner may have a choice among terminating possession, demanding amounts under a formula, drawing security, and seeking return. But a contractual remedy still requires attention to applicable law, registrations, rights of other creditors, and any court or authority whose involvement is required.

**Leo:** Never translate a contractual right to request return into a promise of immediate physical recovery. Get advice on the law at the aircraft's location and the operator's insolvency forum. Check continued insurance, document access, and arrangements for safe custody pending resolution.

**Mira:** Finally, price the outcome after crediting amounts already paid, deposits applied, and resale or re-lease proceeds where required. The owner may have transition, storage, and remarketing costs. Interest and holdover rent may accrue for different periods.

**Leo:** Identify any restriction on double recovery and the point at which the airline receives an accounting. If the draft leaves the buyer of the risk funding the full demand immediately and chasing credits much later, you must quantify the peak cash need separately from eventual loss.

**Mira:** Time to wrap up. Three takeaways. One, distinguish a casualty from an event of loss. A destroyed component is a casualty, but the lease will dictate whether it triggers the special settlement process of an event of loss.

**Leo:** Two, map the cash gaps. If your lease requires twenty-four million dollars on day thirty, but insurance pays twenty-three million on day sixty, your treasury needs a bridge for the full settlement amount on day thirty.

**Mira:** Three, trace actual receipt and exact triggers. Acceptance of an insurance claim does not stop rent if the lease requires payment in full. A drafted notice does not mature a default if it hasn't been delivered.
