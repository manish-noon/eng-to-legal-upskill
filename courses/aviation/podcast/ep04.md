# Episode 4: When the aircraft is late or defective

**Leo:** The manufacturer calls. The aircraft is going to be late. Your airline is already selling tickets for those seats, and the replacement lift is going to drain your cash reserves.

**Mira:** This is 'When the aircraft is late or defective'. The immediate instinct is to claim a breach and demand full compensation, but the contract controls the relief.

**Leo:** An airline might want to describe every single missed delivery as a manufacturer failure. But a purchase agreement usually distinguishes causes. We have to classify the cause before counting the days.

**Mira:** Exactly. We look for an excusable delay, which is simply a delay for which the contract grants specified relief.

**Leo:** People throw around the term force majeure all the time when a timeline slips.

**Mira:** The label has little value on its own. Force majeure is a contractual or legal basis for relief from performance in defined circumstances, often involving events beyond a party's control. But under your specific contract, does the event extend the delivery date, suspend damages, permit termination, or do all three at different times?

**Leo:** If it doesn't fit the relief wording, it is a non-excusable or inexcusable delay. Build a factual timeline before attaching a legal conclusion.

**Mira:** You mark the binding delivery obligation, the first day performance was affected, and the work that could still continue. Then map when the impediment ended and when the seller could reasonably have tendered the aircraft.

**Leo:** Say a supplier's factory closes. That might fit the listed force majeure events perfectly. But it makes absolutely no difference to delivery if the buyer's late selections were already holding up the critical task.

**Mira:** The contract and governing law may treat those overlapping causes differently. You record overlapping days once, state the assumptions in your model, and ask counsel to confirm the allocation.

**Leo:** You also need to look for conditions attached to the relief. The manufacturer might have to notify the buyer promptly, estimate the impact, and take reasonable steps to reduce it.

**Mira:** Decide what evidence the buyer needs, who can ask for an update, and whether failure to give timely notice actually loses the relief. If the text is silent, leave the legal effect as an open question.

**Leo:** A supplier email confirming disruption does not prove that a substitute part could not have been sourced, or that the eventual number of days claimed was unavoidable.

**Mira:** Let us test that with a hypothetical. A strike affects one supplier for twelve days. Other work can continue, and the aircraft is delivered four days late. Which question best tests a twelve-day extension claim?

**Leo:** You ask which contractual trigger and causal path connect the strike to the claimed extension. You do not ask if the seller can prove the supplier employs union members, or if the airline has a strike clause in its contracts with passengers.

**Mira:** Right. An event and its impact on required performance are separate facts. The agreed extension rule controls the measure. For instance, article eight point two of the filed Bombardier purchase agreement addresses detail, expected impact, and mitigation alongside the notice requirement.

**Leo:** A buyer might want immediate repayment of all advances whenever any external event occurs. What makes a narrower fallback commercially intelligible?

**Mira:** The seller can continue a recoverable programme for an agreed period. The buyer gets progress reports and a defined exit if a long-stop is reached, with a funded refund route.

**Leo:** Let us move to the money. We need to calculate the delay payment exactly as written. Liquidated damages, often abbreviated LDs, are an amount or formula agreed in advance for a specified failure.

**Mira:** They can reduce argument over how to measure the buyer's loss. But enforceability and characterisation depend on governing law, so ask counsel to assess the actual drafting. You need to read four variables before estimating recovery.

**Leo:** What is the starting event? Does a grace period exclude early days? Is the rate per day or per week, and how are partial units treated? And is there a cap, meaning a maximum payment for one aircraft or for the order as a whole?

**Mira:** Let us work through a numeric teaching example. We count only eligible calendar days of delay. The first five earn nothing. Each additional day earns ten thousand dollars up to a one hundred thousand dollar cap per aircraft.

**Leo:** So in my model, at day six the amount is ten thousand dollars. At day fifteen it reaches the cap. At day twenty-three it remains one hundred thousand dollars.

**Mira:** Those assumptions create a specific curve. A different clause might make a payment retroactive to day one once the threshold is crossed. Read the words between the threshold and the payment formula before selecting the shape for your chart.

**Leo:** But delay can keep costing the airline money long after the payment curve has flattened. Continue that example. The airline incurs documented incremental replacement costs of twelve thousand dollars for each of twenty-three days.

**Mira:** The gross cost is two hundred and seventy-six thousand dollars.

**Leo:** Assume the allowed LD payment is the sole collectible recovery, pays in cash at once, and requires no proof beyond the qualifying delay. After recovery, the buyer still bears one hundred and seventy-six thousand dollars of cover costs.

**Mira:** A cap of one hundred thousand dollars is a ceiling on that specific payment. It says nothing about the airline's total loss.

**Leo:** Let us try another teaching clause. It pays seven thousand five hundred dollars per eligible day after the first four, capped at forty-eight thousand dollars. There are twelve eligible days. How many dollars does the clause pay?

**Mira:** Twelve eligible days minus the four grace days leaves eight payable days. Eight days at seven thousand five hundred would produce sixty thousand dollars. The cap reduces payment to forty-eight thousand dollars.

**Leo:** And what if that payment arrives as a restricted service credit? Its cash-equivalent value might be lower. You only count the credit to the extent the airline can actually use it.

**Mira:** Here is a scenario for that. An airline incurs two hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars incremental cover cost. Its only recovery is a ninety thousand dollar parts credit of which it can use sixty percent at full economic value. What dollar loss does it retain, ignoring discounting?

**Leo:** Usable recovery is ninety thousand times sixty percent, which equals fifty-four thousand dollars. Retained loss is two hundred and twenty-five thousand minus fifty-four thousand, leaving one hundred and seventy-one thousand dollars.

**Mira:** Now consider an aggregate cap. A daily payment is capped in aggregate for all affected aircraft. Two aircraft are late on separate dates. What is the safe modelling choice?

**Leo:** You track cumulative claims against the shared cap and verify its scope. An aggregate cap can be consumed by a first claim, reducing the amount available for the next. You do not ignore the cap until all aircraft arrive on the same day, and you do not reset it at every delivery just because each aircraft has its own serial number.

**Mira:** Eventually, delay hits a long-stop. This is an outside limit that can trigger a right to exit or another remedy. It might run from the end of the delivery period, a revised month, or notice that delay will exceed a threshold.

**Leo:** You must define what must remain undone at that time. A clause tied to failure to present an aircraft for inspection can stop running before formal delivery.

**Mira:** In a real agreement, the buyer and seller can have different thresholds for the exact same excusable disruption. Read both parties' rights. If either can terminate, a manufacturer could exercise a later right just when the airline still wants to wait for a scarce slot.

**Leo:** Termination usually requires an election, an affirmative decision communicated in a stated form and time. A right exercisable within five business days after a threshold can expire even while the delay continues.

**Mira:** Capture who must send the notice, the address, when receipt counts, the applicable business-day calendar, and whether a renegotiation step comes first. Also check whether termination concerns only the affected aircraft or other positions, training, and spares.

**Leo:** A fleet buyer relying on uniform equipment may lose economies of scale after one cancellation, but its contract may leave unaffected aircraft orders firmly in place.

**Mira:** You should prepare two cash forecasts. In the wait case, model cover aircraft, financing, and the revised service date.

**Leo:** In the exit case, model the return of pre-delivery payments, interest if provided, substitute purchase or lease price, delay to the substitute, and availability of crew training.

**Mira:** Remember that a refund restores money already paid. Crediting that returned principal as an additional profit or as compensation for all lost time overstates the benefit of cancellation.

**Leo:** If a seller asks the buyer to elect early on the basis of a forecast, ask whether a binding revised date and refund protection can support a decision to wait.

**Mira:** Let us read the filed initial provisioning letter thirty-one ninety-four dash zero one, article eight. Assume the airline validly terminates one aircraft under article seven of the incorporated aircraft terms. It has also ordered initial spares for that aircraft. Some are on the shelf and the rest are due to ship.

**Leo:** Does sending the aircraft termination notice close the spares orders?

**Mira:** No. The letter gives the customer a further election. The manufacturer must receive the written request within fifteen days after termination. Match each item to initial provisioning under this letter for the terminated aircraft and check delivery status when the request arrives.

**Leo:** Items already delivered fall outside this discharge. A shipment delivered between aircraft termination and receipt of the request can change what the buyer owes. Put proof of receipt and the item-level reconciliation in the exit file.

**Mira:** Here is another trigger scenario. The clause permits the buyer to cancel if the aircraft has not been presented for inspection by a long-stop. It was conformingly presented before the long-stop and delivery is still pending. Which conclusion follows from this fact alone?

**Leo:** That specific presentation-based trigger has not occurred. Presentation and delivery are distinct events. Read the remedy linked to the event that actually failed. You cannot assume cancellation is automatic just because delivery is incomplete.

**Mira:** And if prompt notice of delay is missing from the seller, should the model assume full LD recovery?

**Leo:** First determine which party owed which notice, what consequence the agreement states, actual prejudice if relevant, and the governing law. Show the disputed recovery as a scenario.

**Mira:** Next, check which remedies can be combined. An exclusive remedy limits the available responses to a specified failure. The agreement may make delay payments the sole financial remedy while preserving a long-stop termination right.

**Leo:** A broad release might instead settle all claims once a buyer accepts revised delivery. Read the LD clause, termination provisions, liability exclusions, caps, and amendment together.

**Mira:** Do not put both full reimbursement of replacement lift and full LDs in the base case unless the text and applicable law support recovery of both. The operational loss does not disappear because the contract offers a limited remedy for it.

**Leo:** We have to study the buyer's own default as carefully as seller delay. The OEM may have a right to terminate, retain a specified sum from advances, recover additional amounts, or remarket the production slot if the buyer fails to pay or wrongfully refuses a conforming tender.

**Mira:** Find the grace or cure period. A cure is the correction of a default within an allowed period. See who gives notice and whether it applies to this specific default.

**Leo:** Do the agreed damages consume all advances or only a defined amount? If the manufacturer keeps a deposit, verify whether the buyer remains liable for any shortfall.

**Mira:** The two sides may use the exact same term, liquidated damages, for completely different failures.

**Leo:** Here is a common error. The seller may retain a fixed percentage as its sole remedy for buyer default. The issues list labels that number buyer delay recovery. What is wrong with that label?

**Mira:** It reverses the beneficiary and the default. Build separate seller-delay and buyer-default entries. Checking the actor, failure, and beneficiary prevents the model from using the opposite party's protection.

**Leo:** Let us look at what happens after delivery. Run a defect through the warranty.

**Mira:** A warranty is a promise about a condition or performance with defined consequences if it fails. After delivery, identify the covered item, responsible warrantor, and period. The aircraft manufacturer may cover the airframe and its own installed items while suppliers give separate warranties on engines, equipment, or components.

**Leo:** A pass-through right lets the buyer benefit from a supplier warranty under its stated conditions. It may require assignment, direct registration, or claims through the manufacturer.

**Mira:** A long coverage period has little value if the airline cannot establish the required inspection history or submit an eligible claim.

**Leo:** Consider a part that fails during the stated warranty period. Check whether the period runs from aircraft delivery, installation, hours, cycles, or whichever limit occurs first. A cycle generally consists of one takeoff and landing for tracking use, subject to the applicable definition.

**Mira:** Distinguish discovery of a defect from the deadline to notify it, and from the deadline to return the failed part. List required evidence, shipping instructions, approved repair location, and whether the warrantor chooses repair, replacement, or credit.

**Leo:** The airline may still have to pay removal, installation, freight, customs, and substitute capacity. You must assign each uncovered cost to the right party.

**Mira:** If the manufacturer supplies a replacement component free of charge and no cover for downtime, the airline retains the economic cost of the grounded aircraft. A general exclusion of indirect or consequential loss may affect some claims.

**Leo:** Labels and their effect depend on the text and law, so identify each cost specifically. Read whether the repair restarts a full warranty period or covers only the balance of the original term.

**Mira:** Check who can claim after a financing sale and whether maintenance by another approved facility changes eligibility. Let us run a pass-through scenario. An engine component fails in the coverage period. The OEM explicitly passes through the engine supplier's warranty and gives no direct engine warranty. What should the airline obtain first?

**Leo:** The applicable supplier terms, claimant eligibility, deadlines, and transfer documents. A pass-through can have different procedures and remedies. Coverage of the part alone would not establish downtime recovery.

**Mira:** A replacement part is available at no charge, but it will arrive in three weeks. What is missing from an estimate showing zero buyer loss?

**Leo:** The cost of operating without the aircraft, repair labour, movement of parts, and any costs the warranty leaves with the airline. Deduct another recovery only if it applies to that specific loss.

**Mira:** Here is another teaching hypothetical. A warranty reimburses qualifying parts and explicitly excludes labour and service interruption. Which entry correctly models a covered failure?

**Leo:** You deduct the collectible parts recovery from the gross scenario loss, including uncovered labour and interruption. The covered failure can produce uncovered costs. Measure the recovery against the specified heads of loss. You do not set retained loss to zero just because the failure is covered.

**Mira:** Finally, make product support deliverable. A product support package can include technical representatives, maintenance information, spare parts access, and training.

**Leo:** A promise to assist needs an operational test. What support is available, in which location, from when, during which hours, at whose cost, and for how long?

**Mira:** An adviser may have no power to approve a repair or bind the manufacturer. If a service credit is the offered remedy, model the permitted use and expiry.

**Leo:** The buyer can sometimes obtain extra training capacity, an earlier spares allocation, or a longer credit window more readily than a broader warranty.

**Mira:** End the review with one row per failure mode. For late delivery, identify cause, eligible days, cap, exit date, and refundable cash.

**Leo:** For a post-delivery defect, list cost to repair, costs excluded, deadlines, and who can actually pay. For unavailable support, identify the date it affects operations and the value of a substitute.

**Mira:** Write a trade the buyer could accept, such as taking a lower daily cap in exchange for a protected refund and an earlier exit that aligns with a real alternative aircraft.

**Leo:** Test that alternative with the fleet team before giving up a remedy. If the buyer may accept an earlier termination right in place of higher LDs, which fact matters most to the value of that exchange?

**Mira:** Whether an alternative aircraft and an enforceable, timely pre-delivery payment refund are available at the exit date. Exit is economically useful when it permits a better funded alternative. A right with no viable replacement can be worth less than predictable compensation.

**Leo:** Let us close out with three takeaways. One, classify the cause of delay based on the specific relief granted in the contract, building a factual timeline before applying a legal label like force majeure.

**Mira:** Two, model liquidated damages by reading the starting event, grace period, daily rate, and caps, remembering that an LD cap is a ceiling on that payment, not on your total operational loss.

**Leo:** Three, termination requires a precise election. Check whether unfulfilled triggers like presentation versus delivery control the right, and forecast both the wait case and the exit case.
