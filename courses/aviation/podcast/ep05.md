# Episode 5: Buy the used aircraft that is actually there

**Leo:** You get the text from a broker. A seller offers a particular aircraft for sale. It looks perfect on paper, so you want to lock it down immediately. We are going to buy the used aircraft that is actually there.

**Mira:** But before you pay for a detailed inspection, you want access. You need confidence that the seller will keep the aircraft available. A letter of intent, or LOI, records those proposed commercial terms.

**Leo:** I use the LOI to buy time. I want to specify the aircraft and installed engines, the expected price, the deposit, the inspection scope and location, and the intended closing date.

**Mira:** You also need to state whether the proposal assumes a lease is already in place. If there is an existing lease, the buyer may acquire the lessor's position with an airline still in possession. That is a completely different transaction from buying a vacant aircraft ready to fly away.

**Leo:** It changes the risk profile entirely. But let's look at the immediate commitments. I need to identify which promises the parties intend to bind them right now, before a definitive sale agreement is signed.

**Mira:** Exactly. Confidentiality, cost allocation, deposit handling, and exclusivity may operate immediately. Exclusivity prevents specified marketing or negotiations for a stated period. Counsel should confirm how the words and conduct work under the relevant law.

**Leo:** I see people slap a label like 'indicative' on the cover of the LOI and assume they are safe. But then they include an unconditional commitment to pay a non-refundable deposit.

**Mira:** That sits awkwardly together. You have to decide who may retain that deposit, under which events, and what happens if the parties simply fail to agree on the definitive document.

**Leo:** Let's run a hypothetical on the timing. Say the LOI gives me seven days of exclusivity starting at signature. But the seller may provide the technical records at any time. What is my first commercial risk here?

**Mira:** Your protected period may expire before you have evidence to price the aircraft. The promised time has little value without access. The commercial instruction here is to tie delivery of the necessary materials to the timetable.

**Leo:** If they want a larger deposit to take the aircraft off the market, I need concessions. What combination makes a larger deposit acceptable?

**Mira:** You need a clear inspection standard and decision window, a defined right to a prompt refund on rejection, and escrow instructions matching those rights. You have to test seller default and disagreement over a finding.

**Leo:** Access has to deliver an answer in the available time. I need to identify the inspection facility, qualified technical representatives, the records format, and the working language.

**Mira:** And the opening hours, authority for disassembly, and who pays to put the aircraft back together after a rejected inspection. You also need to plan for travel and a ferry flight if the aircraft needs to move to a specialist facility.

**Leo:** I usually want the inspection period to start only after all material records arrive. The seller might accept that if I supply a checklist in advance and raise omissions promptly. Once we are in, we have to turn condition into measurable tests.

**Mira:** The purchase agreement should state the delivery condition. That is the physical and documentary standard to be met at handover. You break it into tests the team can actually perform.

**Leo:** We match serial numbers for the airframe, installed engines, and listed parts. We verify the inspection status, damage history, modifications, remaining service life, and the records supporting them.

**Mira:** A technical team may carry out a visual inspection, evaluate internal engine condition where permitted, and review records of previous maintenance. Counsel can then convert the team's findings into written conditions, cost allocations, and rights when a test fails.

**Leo:** Let's talk about airworthiness. People throw that word around like it guarantees a perfect plane.

**Mira:** Airworthiness simply concerns satisfaction of the applicable requirements for safe and lawful flight. A buyer can, and usually should, require more than that minimum.

**Leo:** Right. I need no major check falling due immediately after entry into service. I need a minimum remaining life on expensive parts, and complete, traceable records.

**Mira:** A part with a specified retirement limit needs evidence showing how much of that life has been used. Without that evidence, future use or resale may be constrained even if the engine starts and runs perfectly on the inspection day.

**Leo:** Let's test that. The aircraft can fly legally today, but a life-limited part lacks the agreed evidence of remaining cycles. Which response fits the sale? Do I just accept it because serviceability today proves compliance with all delivery conditions?

**Mira:** No. A usable part and provable remaining life are different. You must test the records against the contractual condition and quantify replacement or evidence-reconstruction exposure. The remedy and cost depend on the actual gap.

**Leo:** We track that gap on a discrepancy sheet. I want an entry for each failed requirement. We record the contractual reference, the inspection method, the observation, and the evidence.

**Mira:** Then you estimate the cost and downtime of cure, the deadline, and the proposed treatment. Some defects must be rectified before delivery. Others could support a price reduction if the buyer can perform the work efficiently after closing.

**Leo:** But you have to treat legal or safety requirements as real constraints. A price reduction does not make an aircraft airworthy, and it does not satisfy a lender's funding condition.

**Mira:** Ask the appropriate technical or regulatory professional to decide whether the aircraft may move and under what authority. Now, suppose the seller offers a price reduction equal to the repair shop's parts estimate. What else affects whether that is enough?

**Leo:** Parts are just the baseline. I have to calculate labor, transport, downtime, lost program coverage, financing effects, and possible residual-value loss. Then I subtract any duplicated items or enforceable third-party recovery.

**Mira:** Which brings us to the inspection election and the default for silence. A pre-purchase inspection is your opportunity to examine condition before you are committed to accept. Does the buyer have broad discretion to reject, or only a right to identify failures against defined conditions?

**Leo:** And when does the election period begin? I've seen drafts that start the clock on completion of physical checks, even though the written report arrives days later.

**Mira:** You also need to identify whether acceptance may be conditional on specified repairs, and whether the seller must actually carry them out. If the seller declines, does the buyer get to accept at an adjusted price, withdraw, recover its deposit, or pay for the return flight?

**Leo:** Silence is the killer here. Silence can end a transaction.

**Mira:** Exactly. Look at section three point five of the filed Global 5000 agreement. The phrase is 'Purchaser shall be deemed to have rejected the Aircraft'. Read the trigger, deadline, expense, and refund provisions around that phrase. Another seller's draft could give silence the exact opposite consequence.

**Leo:** Let's run a hypothetical on rejection. The aircraft meets the defined airworthiness test, but I dislike its cabin finish. When could this still support rejection under the agreement?

**Mira:** Only when the buyer retains a timely discretionary inspection election broad enough to cover dissatisfaction. A subjective election and an objective delivery test allocate different rights. You have to check the standard and the deadline.

**Leo:** What if the deadline is approaching, and the seller verbally promises to repair a defect? Do I just wait until the repair is finished to avoid appearing difficult?

**Mira:** Never. Technical work does not automatically stop a contractual clock. You must use the mechanism the agreement recognizes. Serve the permitted conditional election or obtain a signed extension before the deadline.

**Leo:** If we elect to proceed with repairs, we specify how the facility signs them off, and who pays for repeat inspection or an evaluation flight. We preserve a route for a dispute about whether the repair meets the agreed standard.

**Mira:** A named expert can decide a narrowly technical question if the contract sets out its remit, evidence, time, and cost. Keep legal disputes about title and contract interpretation in the agreed dispute process.

**Leo:** And do not let a minor repair disagreement lapse into an automatic purchase because the notice period continued running during discussions.

**Mira:** If you are buying a package, read related aircraft together. A right to reject one aircraft may affect the others, or the parties may require separate acceptances and separate allocations of price.

**Leo:** If a single missing document is capable of stopping the whole acquisition, the effect on my fleet plan can dwarf the cost of replacing the document.

**Mira:** Put a decision owner on the technical election and a separate owner on the closing notices. An inspector's email saying 'looks good' may have none of the formal effect of the signed acceptance letter prescribed by the agreement.

**Leo:** Let's pivot to title. We need to separate 'as is, where is' from clean title. An 'as is, where is' term allocates some condition and location risk to the buyer, subject to wording and governing law.

**Mira:** Its scope may incorporate exceptions for express delivery conditions, title promises, or an agreed state at inspection. Read the provision with the rights that survive technical acceptance and closing.

**Leo:** I might agree to take hidden physical defects after an inspection, but I am still insisting on a bill of sale conveying good title free of specified interests. A promise about ownership and a promise about mechanical quality address different losses.

**Mira:** Let's test that distinction. The buyer accepts a used aircraft as is after inspection. The seller separately promises title free of liens except one named lease. What belongs in title diligence? Do you ignore all liens because physical condition has been accepted?

**Leo:** No. The exception permits one identified interest. A condition disclaimer alone does not settle competing title claims. I must investigate other interests, obtain releases, and verify the named lease and transfer terms.

**Mira:** A lien search is one way to identify claims recorded against the asset. Ask counsel which registries and jurisdictions matter for this airframe, these engines, this seller, and this location.

**Leo:** We obtain the prior transfer documents needed to verify the chain of title. Search results should be brought up to date near closing.

**Mira:** And each adverse finding should lead to an effective release, a permitted exception specifically accepted by the buyer, or a stop. Where assets were leased or engines swapped, trace who actually owns each one. The seller cannot cure another owner's claim merely by signing a second description of the asset.

**Leo:** You mentioned buying an aircraft subject to a lease earlier. We have to distinguish that from taking on unknown claims. If I expect the airline to stay in possession, I'm reviewing rent allocation, deposits, security, and maintenance balances.

**Mira:** You also review defaults, consents, notices, and the transfer of the lessor's rights and obligations. One invoice dated after closing may relate to pre-closing use. You must agree on the economic cut-off and adjustment mechanism.

**Leo:** If vacant delivery is required, verify return of possession and termination or release of existing rights before payment. Choosing the wrong transaction type leaves the buyer owning an asset it has no immediate right to operate.

**Mira:** Now we approach closing. We must close the three gates together. The technical team confirms condition and records. The legal team checks title and release documents. Treasury confirms cleared funds.

**Leo:** Escrow then executes agreed instructions. A signed but undelivered bill of sale held to order can be part of the package. We identify who may date and release it, when risk transfers, and which registry filings must be made in what sequence.

**Mira:** Where a lender requires payment before releasing its security, counsel can coordinate its conditional release with a payoff instruction. A draft checklist that says 'all simultaneously' still needs an authorized person to perform each step and evidence of the result.

**Leo:** Let's work through the cash flows aloud. Suppose the price is twelve million dollars. The buyer has already placed point six million with escrow.

**Mira:** At closing, the buyer adds eleven point four million. Escrow pays five million to release a seller mortgage, and seven million to the seller, ignoring fees and other adjustments.

**Leo:** The seller's debt is paid out of the sale proceeds. Requiring the buyer to add five million to the price as well would count the same obligation twice. We check the direction of each wire and the scope of release against the payoff letter's validity date and any daily interest accrual.

**Mira:** Let's do a second scenario. The agreed price is eight million dollars, reduced by a signed point two five million condition adjustment. A point four million escrow deposit applies in full. How many millions does the buyer still have to fund, ignoring fees?

**Leo:** Eight minus point two five minus point four equals seven point three five. I have to fund seven point three five million dollars. The deposit is credited against the reduced price.

**Mira:** One last flow. Escrow holds the entire adjusted price of nine million dollars. A three point two million seller lien payoff and point one million seller closing costs are due out of it. What is released to the seller?

**Leo:** Nine minus three point two minus point one is five point seven. Five point seven million dollars released to the seller. Escrow pays the lender and costs from the gross proceeds.

**Mira:** Now, a hurdle at the finish line. At closing, the seller tenders a clean bill of sale, but its lender has only promised informally to release a registered interest next week. The client requires clean title on payment. Which instruction follows?

**Leo:** I hold release until counsel verifies an effective payoff and release mechanism satisfying the client's condition. A promise from the seller may give a claim for breach while leaving a competing claim outstanding. Match the release to the funding trigger.

**Mira:** Correct. We also need to look at what happens if the aircraft is damaged between inspection and closing. Read who must maintain it, permitted use and hour limits, repair thresholds, notice obligations, and the buyer's election after damage.

**Leo:** Even a repaired aircraft may carry a damage history affecting resale value. A mechanism limited to the cost of physical repair could leave that value loss with the buyer.

**Mira:** Ask whether the buyer can order a repeat inspection, seek a defined adjustment or decline delivery. And check whether seller insurance covers the aircraft until contractual risk passes. Verify the buyer's policy attaches at the handover.

**Leo:** What makes a material damage claim after inspection difficult, even if the aircraft is repaired?

**Mira:** A waiver in unconditional acceptance, an expired election period, inadequate baseline evidence, or an allocation limited to repair cost. Record condition and preserve the specific post-inspection protection.

**Leo:** We have to set the buyer's instruction before price release. A clean decision states which concessions can replace physical cure and which issues bar closing.

**Mira:** Assume the airline can schedule a nonessential cabin repair during an existing maintenance visit. A price allowance supported by a firm quote, a records update, and an adequate margin for overruns may work.

**Leo:** But what if that repair allowance covers the quoted work exactly, and the lender conditions funding on that work being completed before delivery? What is still required to close on the proposed allowance?

**Mira:** Valid lender consent or another funded, contractually permitted route, plus operational clearance where required. The allowance addresses cost, but it leaves the lender's funding condition unresolved. You must obtain the operational and financing sign-offs before advising that a cheaper aircraft is deliverable.

**Leo:** Finally, we prepare a closing record. Signed documents, releases, maintenance status at handover, evidence of time and location, wire confirmations, registry filing receipts, and the exact exceptions reserved.

**Mira:** Assign responsibility for outstanding post-closing filings and agreed repairs. A cheap aircraft with incomplete evidence can remain expensive long after it has left the seller's hangar.

**Leo:** The buyer's best negotiating moment is often while it can still withhold a properly conditioned payment. Once the wire and unconditional acceptance are released, you may have to fund a repair or pursue a contested claim while keeping your route operating.

**Mira:** Let's close with the three takeaways. First, tie delivery of the necessary materials to the exclusivity timetable. The promised time has little value without access.

**Leo:** Second, turn condition into measurable tests. A usable part and provable remaining life are different. Test the records against the contractual condition.

**Mira:** Third, match the release of funds to verified title. A promise from the seller leaves a competing claim outstanding. Hold release until counsel verifies an effective payoff.
