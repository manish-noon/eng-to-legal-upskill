# Episode 9: Running the aircraft under lease

**Leo:** A customer wants your spare aircraft for tomorrow morning. They sold tickets for a route their own metal cannot serve, and their commercial director is offering cash right now for your aircraft and crew. Give them an answer before your crew goes off duty.

**Mira:** You cannot give an answer until you know who has possession of the aircraft tomorrow. We are clearing the aircraft deal, episode nine: Running the aircraft under lease.

**Leo:** Selling the tickets means nothing on the ramp. If you run Cedar Air and Bayline wants your plane, you need to know who decides to start, divert, or cancel the flight. You need to know who supplies and directs the crew, whose operating authority covers the service, and who has possession between flights.

**Mira:** Exactly. Ask for the proposed contract and the instructions actually sent to both dispatch desks. Then open section 8.1 and section 8.1.1 of the filed shared terms. Start one paragraph above the wet lease heading. The general rule restricts surrender of possession without prior written lessor consent.

**Leo:** The paragraphs below that give specific permissions. If your proposed operation fits a permission, you check every condition before relying on it. If it falls outside the permission, you return to the general rule. Keep in mind, the lessor is the party leasing you the aircraft, but the owner and lenders can have entirely separate rights.

**Mira:** Let us look at Exhibit 11, the Aircraft Lease Shared Terms. Section 8.1.1 paragraph 1 states that no possessory rights whatsoever are granted to the wet lessee. Under this wet lease permission, Cedar could operate flights for Bayline with Cedar’s regular employees or contractors.

**Leo:** Meaning Cedar retains sole possession and control, keeps the registration unchanged, and maintains, insures, and operates the aircraft as required. The arrangement must end within Cedar’s lease term.

**Mira:** Yes, and a continuing Event of Default blocks this route. But read the defined trigger before treating a simple accounting query as an Event of Default.

**Leo:** Here is a dispatch reality check. Bayline wants to specify departure times. Can its duty manager order a departure?

**Mira:** A customer can buy a timetable. But check who has final authority over the flight and what happens if the operator declines it. A right to overrule Cedar’s flight decision would contradict Cedar’s claim to sole control. Operational control means authority over initiating, conducting, or ending a flight.

**Leo:** Let us run a hypothetical. Bayline supplies the branding. Cedar supplies its regular crew, but the agreement gives Bayline exclusive overnight possession. Which fact changes the lease analysis?

**Mira:** It is Bayline’s right to take possession between flights. Section 8.1.1 paragraph 1 requires Cedar to retain possession at all times. Having its own crew on each flight cannot cure an overnight handover. The logo on the fuselage is irrelevant to possession.

**Leo:** So an overnight handover breaks the wet lease permission. That pushes us into sublease territory. If Bayline wants to take possession and fly with its crew, we have to read section 8.1.1 paragraph 2.

**Mira:** Correct. First, check whether Bayline qualifies as a Permitted Air Carrier under the executed documents. That defined category matters even if Bayline holds a valid operating licence. The permission has a lease-term limit and a continuing-default condition.

**Leo:** Bayline also has to acknowledge the owner, lessor, and financing parties directly and accept that their rights come first. But let us talk about liability. If Bayline takes possession under a permitted sublease and fails to maintain the aircraft, who answers to Cedar’s lessor?

**Mira:** Cedar remains primarily liable under section 8.2. A compliant handover gives Bayline possession, but it leaves Cedar’s original obligations in force. Cedar may separately have a claim against Bayline, but the lessor will look to Cedar for performance.

**Leo:** Let us read the sublease clock before signing. If the commercial team wants this done yesterday, how far back do we need to start?

**Mira:** Work backwards from signing. Paragraph (i) requires prior written notice of the proposed agreement, including the carrier’s name and the term. For an independent carrier, the minimum is thirty days. A qualifying leasing affiliate has a different clock of ten Business Days.

**Leo:** Notice starts one clock. But paragraph (j) adds a separate delivery of the agreed form at least five Business Days before proposed execution. The agreed form is the final text settled for execution, before signature.

**Mira:** Exactly. And after signing, an officer-certified copy is due within ten Business Days. Identify the notices address, delivery method, and Business Day definition before entering a calendar date into your project plan.

**Leo:** Let us test that timeline. Cedar gave the correct thirty-day notice for an independent carrier. The parties agreed the final sublease today and plan to sign tomorrow. What still needs attention?

**Mira:** The agreed form must reach the lessor five Business Days before proposed signing. Sending the agreed form satisfies a separate pre-signing obligation. The later certified copy merely evidences what the parties signed. You cannot skip the five-day rule just because you gave the thirty-day notice.

**Leo:** While the clock is ticking, you also have to read paragraphs (d), (e), (g), and (l). Obtain satisfactory insurance evidence and the specified legal opinion. Check whether the proposed registrations trigger assignments, priority filings, and additional evidence.

**Mira:** If the plan changes the state of registration, identify the applicable aircraft-specific agreements and obtain written lessor approval. Ask the operator’s regulatory lead to document any authority permissions for this aircraft, operator, and route.

**Leo:** And remember, an insurer can confirm coverage, but it cannot grant operating rights. Let us shift to a different kind of operational headache: a repair claim on its own terms. A lien is a claim against property securing an obligation.

**Mira:** At an outstation, a workshop says local law lets it hold the aircraft for unpaid work. Ask local counsel what right actually exists and how to release it. Separately, ask whether Cedar promised the lessor to keep the aircraft free of that right.

**Leo:** A claim can qualify under the lease while still disrupting the flight. A promise to the lessor cannot make the shop open its hangar. Section 6.2.2 groups different kinds of permitted liens. A permitted lien is a claim falling within a defined contractual exception and satisfying its conditions.

**Mira:** For ordinary-course tax liens, look for tax not yet due or a good-faith contest through appropriate proceedings. For an ordinary-course repair lien arising by law, check whether the obligation is unpaid and how far past due it is. The filed exception runs only through thirty days overdue.

**Leo:** Both branches require adequate reserves and protection against the stated risks, including sale or forfeiture, liability of a protected party, and other liens. The final sentence adds safeguards against exposure to detention and similar consequences. You have to read it even when the invoice falls within the time limit.

**Mira:** Consider this scenario. The shop invoice is disputed and thirty-one days overdue. Does filing an objection preserve the repair-lien exception?

**Leo:** No. The appropriate-proceedings language belongs to the tax branch, not the repair branch. You must obtain a release or another effective resolution and escalate the lease breach. Record any continuing payment dispute separately.

**Mira:** Here is another one. A repair invoice is nine days overdue. Cedar has set aside cash. The shop lawfully advertises the aircraft for sale next week. Is the lien within the exception on those facts?

**Leo:** No. The likelihood of sale defeats the exception. The time limit and reserves satisfy only part of the test. An announced sale requires urgent action to protect the aircraft. You cannot hide behind the thirty-day window if the asset is about to be auctioned.

**Mira:** Let us talk about routine access that the shop can actually deliver. In Schedule 2 section 1.6, you find the ordinary inspection right. At reasonable times, the lessor or its authorised representatives can inspect the airframe and engines and copy the aircraft documentation and maintenance program.

**Leo:** Assuming no continuing Event of Default, the clause requires sixty days of advance notice, protects normal operations and maintenance, and limits the visit to one in twelve months. During maintenance, the visitors may look behind panels already opened for the work.

**Mira:** There is no general instruction here to dismantle the cabin for a photographer. If the aircraft is at a third-party shop, arrange visitor passes, supervision, and a records export.

**Leo:** A password held by the airline may stop working when its storage subscription ends. Establish who can produce readable copies, including revisions and serial numbers, and whether that access survives a change of custodian.

**Mira:** Schedule the inspection around actual shop access. The operator owes reasonable efforts to facilitate access to the premises. Keep in mind, the inspector’s visit does not sign off the maintenance.

**Leo:** Cost is another question. The lessor ordinarily pays. A continuing Event of Default or discovery of a Default during the inspection shifts cost to the lessee under this clause. Distinguish the two capitalised triggers.

**Mira:** A records query from an inspector identifies a question to investigate. It does not by itself establish either trigger. Let us test the boundaries of a visit. No Default exists. A timely annual visit coincides with an open avionics bay. The lessor asks Cedar to open three closed bays and pay its travel invoice. What can Cedar offer under section 1.6?

**Leo:** Cedar offers the open bay and relevant records on reasonable arrangements, with ordinary inspection cost for the lessor. The clause allows viewing through openings already made and copying records. The ordinary rule puts inspection costs on the lessor. They cannot demand you open closed bays on your dime.

**Mira:** Let us move to modifications. You need to price the installation and the reversal. A sales team calls a cabin installation temporary because it will run for one season. Engineering should ask whether removing it would impair value, utility, or airworthiness.

**Leo:** Schedule 2 section 1.3.2 makes that a physical and economic question. A non-removable discretionary modification needs prior written lessor approval and compliance with the specified authority requirements. Prior written consent means affirmative written permission obtained before the specified act.

**Mira:** Removability alone does not excuse a loss in aircraft value. For the minor cabin exception, read the restoration obligation and its standard of cosmetic acceptance. Carry the installation into redelivery.

**Leo:** Under section 1.3.4, the operator supplies a list if requested, sixty days before scheduled expiry. The lessor can require reversal by written request no later than forty-five days before expiry, excluding work to the extent it forms part of a Required Action.

**Mira:** If a request arrives forty days before expiry, flag that missed deadline under this mechanism. Check separate restoration and return duties before releasing the removal budget.

**Leo:** If a change to the approved type design stays on the aircraft, section 1.3.2 paragraph 7 specifies certification evidence and transferable use rights. Paying a design contractor for installation may leave the next operator without those rights.

**Mira:** Could both parties prefer to leave an installation in place? Yes. A future operator might pay for the feature, while removal costs Cedar money. Identify who owns the installed equipment, secure the transferable rights, and record the precise restoration duty that the parties agree to release.

**Leo:** Let us work the math aloud. An invented removable installation costs USD seventy-two thousand to fit and USD eighteen thousand to remove. Reversal adds two idle days costing USD six thousand each. What total life-cycle cost in USD should Cedar budget if reversal is required?

**Mira:** Seventy-two thousand plus eighteen thousand, plus two times six thousand. That is one hundred and two thousand USD. A purchase order covering installation captures only the first cash outlay.

**Leo:** Let us look at compulsory work. An airworthiness directive, or AD, is a binding safety instruction issued by an aviation authority and applicable to specified products. Schedule 2 section 1.3.1 puts applicable AD work and inspections due during the term on the lessee.

**Mira:** Section 1.3.7 can reimburse part of qualifying terminating repairs or modifications. Check the issuing authority, issue date, work, threshold, invoices, and calculation.

**Leo:** A terminating action is work that fulfils a directive on a lasting basis for the requirement concerned. An interim inspection may keep the aircraft compliant today without earning reimbursement under that provision.

**Mira:** Assume all section 1.3.7 conditions are met. Eligible direct cost net of other benefits is USD eighty-nine thousand, six hundred and fifty. The threshold is USD twenty-nine thousand, six hundred and fifty. The amortisation period is forty-eight months and the contract counts twelve months to expiry.

**Leo:** The formula takes forty-eight minus twelve, divided by forty-eight, multiplied by the cost minus the threshold. Cost minus threshold is eighty-nine thousand, six hundred and fifty, minus twenty-nine thousand, six hundred and fifty. That equals sixty thousand.

**Mira:** The time fraction is thirty-six over forty-eight. Thirty-six divided by forty-eight, multiplied by sixty thousand, equals forty-five thousand. The lessor reimburses USD forty-five thousand.

**Leo:** Apply the remaining fraction to the amount above the threshold. And do not include lost flying revenue in eligible cost. Let us look at intellectual property. A cabin antenna stays with the aircraft. Its qualifying type-design change has the required certificates. Cedar’s licence prohibits use by anyone else. What remains open?

**Mira:** Evidence of use rights transferable without charge or restriction to the specified successors. Certificates evidence the approved design, like a supplemental type certificate. But section 1.3.2 paragraph 7 separately asks for transferable rights to use it.

**Leo:** Let us make the call at the dispatch desk. It is sixteen hundred hours. Bayline requests possession overnight and its own crew for a morning flight. Cedar has given no sublease notice. Tomorrow’s service matters to Bayline because it must move passengers. Cedar wants the revenue.

**Mira:** The lessor wants to know who can fly its aircraft and how it will recover possession. Solve the immediate timing problem before negotiating the price. A promise to send the missing papers on Monday gives the lessor neither notice before the agreement nor a chance to check the receiving operator.

**Leo:** Hold the proposed handover. Ask Bayline whether it will buy tomorrow’s service with Cedar retaining possession throughout, using Cedar’s regular crew and dispatch authority, under unchanged registration.

**Mira:** Record the revised agreement and amend the operating instructions. Check the lease term, default status, maintenance, insurance, and actual operating permissions. Have the regulatory lead confirm the authority basis and obtain any required approval before releasing the service.

**Leo:** If any condition remains open by crew cutoff, decline this flight. Bayline can seek capacity elsewhere. Do not issue a conditional aircraft release that the ramp could mistake for authority to hand over the keys.

**Mira:** Changing who controls the flight changes the permission you must prove. Bayline possession requires a sublease review. Missing notice holds the handover. Cedar possession and control allow a wet lease review. Release only when every check clears.

**Leo:** Would a lessor email saying 'route looks fine' release tomorrow’s handover?

**Mira:** No. Identify the actual decision and the person authorised to make it. Support for a route does not waive a missed sublease deadline or confirm authority and insurance for a different operator.

**Leo:** For a later sublease handover, set a feasible signing date. Obtain the agreed form, notices, acknowledgments, insurance evidence, and opinion, then close any applicable filing or consent conditions.

**Mira:** If Cedar wants an earlier date, identify exactly which deadlines must change and who can sign an effective waiver. Log the post-signing certified-copy deadline against a named owner. A release instruction should identify the aircraft, actual operator, permitted dates, evidence, and person who can stop the handover if the plan changes.

**Leo:** Final scenario. Cedar and Bayline sign the revised crewed-service agreement. The insurer confirms cover. The regulatory lead says a required route approval is pending at crew cutoff. What is the executable instruction?

**Mira:** Decline the flight at cutoff and retain possession. The required operating approval is independent of insurance and lease permission. Switching crews reopens the possession and control issue. Wait for every check to clear.

**Leo:** Let us close this out. First, possession dictates the permission. An overnight handover breaks a wet lease and triggers sublease notice clocks.

**Mira:** Second, price the entire lifecycle of a modification. A purchase order is incomplete until you budget for removal, idle time, and transferable use rights.

**Leo:** Third, the dispatch desk cutoff is absolute. If external approvals are pending, decline the flight rather than issuing conditional releases on the ramp.
