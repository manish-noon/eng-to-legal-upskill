M&A Handbook

← Course · Cheatsheet ·

A reference for a non-lawyer who needs to follow a deal, read an SPA, predict where terms land and advise whether to proceed. It compresses the 16 course modules. Use it to look things up; the reasoning is in the modules.

Default frame: English-law style private share sale, as used for most serious UAE deals. US-style differences and UAE points are marked. Market figures name their study and come from the course research file: verify before quoting in writing. This is not legal advice.

What lawyers sell: judgment about risk, knowledge of what is normal ("market"), and accountability for the advice. How they think: issue → rule → application → risk → recommendation. How to talk to them: ask "where does this usually land, and what moves it?" rather than asserting what the law is.

3. Contract concepts you must not confuse

ConceptWhat it isIf breached or triggered
WarrantyA statement of fact about the target, given at signingClaim for damages: buyer must prove breach and loss; duty to mitigate; remoteness applies; reduced by disclosure and limits
RepresentationA statement that induced the contractPossible rescission (unwinding) and misrepresentation damages. English-law sellers refuse the word; US deals use "representations and warranties" together
IndemnityA promise to pay if a specified event occursMoney owed for the loss itself ("pound for pound"); generally stronger and simpler to claim; normally not cut by disclosure
Covenant / undertakingA promise to do or not do somethingDamages, or an injunction to stop the breach
Condition (precedent)Something that must happen before closingIf not satisfied or waived by the long-stop date, the deal can be terminated
  • Damages put the innocent party where it would have been if the statement were true. For shares: value as warranted minus actual value. A recurring profit shortfall is therefore worth roughly the shortfall × the price multiple.
  • Governing law = which rules interpret the contract. Jurisdiction / arbitration = who decides disputes and where. Seat = the legal home of an arbitration. They are separate choices.
  • Words with weight: "material", "reasonable endeavours" < "all reasonable endeavours" < "best endeavours", "subject to", "notwithstanding", "including without limitation", "so far as the Seller is aware", "joint and several".

Auction: seller's draft, bidders compete on price and on how lightly they mark up. Bilateral: one buyer, more balanced or buyer-led drafting.

5. Price

Equity value = enterprise value − debt − debt-like items + cash ± (actual working capital − target working capital)
  • Enterprise value (EV): value of the business regardless of how it is financed, usually a multiple of EBITDA. Cash-free, debt-free: the standard basis for quoting EV.
  • Debt-like items reduce price one for one: unpaid tax, deferred consideration, finance leases, unfunded pensions, bonuses accrued, and in the UAE unfunded end-of-service gratuity. Classifying an item as debt-like instead of working capital is a direct price argument.
  • Working capital peg (target): the normal level the business needs. Deliver less and price falls; more and it rises.
Completion accountsLocked box
HowPrice adjusted after closing using a balance sheet drawn at closingPrice fixed off a past balance sheet; no adjustment
Risk between accounts date and closingSeller'sBuyer's, protected by a ban on leakage (value passing to the seller), often with a daily ticker paid to the seller
Favoured byBuyers; US practiceSellers, private equity, auctions
DisputesCommon: accounting policies, expert determinationRare: leakage claims only

Earn-out: part of the price depends on future performance. Bridges a valuation gap; breeds disputes over who controls the business and how the metric is measured. Escrow / holdback / retention: price set aside as security for claims. Deferred consideration, loan notes, shares, rollover: other ways to pay that also give the buyer something to set claims off against.

Market (course research file): Europe, price adjustment in 48% of deals and locked box in 54% of the rest; earn-outs 27% (CMS 2026). US, price adjustment about 92%.

Legal diligence looks at: title to shares, constitution, material contracts (change of control, assignment, exclusivity, termination), debt and security, IP ownership, employment, litigation, licences, property, data protection, sanctions and anti-bribery. A red-flag report lists only what matters above an agreed materiality threshold.

Add: trade licence activities match the business; history of foreign-ownership and any nominee arrangements; gratuity accruals; Emiratisation compliance; Arabic originals; VAT and corporate tax filings.

8. Warranties and disclosure

  • Two functions: force the seller to reveal what it knows (through disclosure), and adjust the price afterwards for what turns out to be untrue.
  • Fundamental warranties: title, capacity, authority (sometimes tax). Capped at the price, long time limit. Business warranties: accounts, contracts, employees, IP, litigation, compliance.
  • Knowledge qualifier ("so far as the Seller is aware"): moves the risk of the unknown to the buyer. Fight over whose knowledge and whether enquiry is deemed (constructive) or not (actual). ABA 2025: 90% constructive in the US.
  • Materiality qualifier: only material breaches count. Materiality scrape: ignore those qualifiers when deciding if there is a breach (single) and/or when measuring loss (double).
  • Disclosure: a matter fairly disclosed cannot found a warranty claim. General disclosure (the whole data room) favours the seller; specific disclosure against particular warranties favours the buyer. Consequence: known problems need an indemnity or a price cut, never a warranty.
  • Sandbagging: can the buyer claim for something it already knew? Pro (yes), anti (no), or silent. ABA 2025: 68% silent, 28% pro, 4% anti.
  • Repetition / bring-down: warranties repeated at closing put gap risk on the seller.

Worked example. Price AED 76m; de minimis 0.1% (76k); basket 1% (760k); cap 20% (15.2m). Claims: 50k, 300k, 600k. The 50k claim is below de minimis. Counted claims = 900k, which clears the basket. Tipping: recover 900k. Deductible: recover 140k. Had counted claims been 700k: recover nothing under either.

Duration logic: long enough for one or two audit cycles to surface problems. Tax follows the tax authority's reassessment window.

10. Indemnities, security and insurance

  • Specific indemnity: for a known risk; pays the loss itself; sits outside basket, general cap and disclosure; has its own cap and time limit. Tax covenant: seller pays pre-closing tax, pound for pound.
  • Recourse: a right to claim is worth only what can be collected. Ask: who is the seller after closing, and where is the money? Security: escrow, holdback, deferred price with set-off, guarantee, joint and several liability.
W&I / RWI insurance
What it doesInsurer pays the buyer for unknown warranty breaches above a retention, up to a policy limit. Seller's own cap drops to a nominal amount.
Never coveredKnown issues, price adjustments, forward-looking statements, fines, areas not diligenced; in the Middle East, war and sanctions exclusions (course research file)
Effect on negotiationSeller stops fighting warranty scope; buyer negotiates with the insurer; known risks still need indemnity or price

11. Conditions, the gap and walking away

  • Conditions precedent: regulatory clearance (merger control, foreign investment, sector regulator), third-party consents, sometimes no material adverse change. Sellers want few; each one is an exit for the buyer.
  • Long-stop date: if conditions are not met by then, either side may terminate.
  • Gap covenants: seller runs the business in the ordinary course and asks consent for major steps. Gun-jumping: the buyer must not take control before clearance.
  • Material adverse change / effect: lets the buyer walk if the target suffers a serious, lasting deterioration. Heavily carved out, rarely invoked successfully. CMS 2026: 14% of European deals; about 96% in the US.
  • Who bears gap risk: locked box → buyer (economic risk passes at the locked-box date). Completion accounts → seller until closing. Warranty repetition and MAC clauses shift it back toward the seller.

12. Other clauses that decide disputes

  • Restrictive covenants: non-compete and non-solicit protect the goodwill paid for. Must be reasonable in scope, area and time (commonly 1 to 3 years) or a court may refuse to enforce them.
  • Entire agreement / non-reliance: only what is in the contract counts; pre-contract statements cannot be sued on (fraud aside).
  • Exclusive remedy: the indemnification or damages regime is the buyer's only route.
  • Notices: claims must be notified in the stated form and time. Many claims fail here.
  • Assignment: can the buyer pass warranty rights to a lender or a later purchaser?
  • Disputes: courts or arbitration; accounting disputes go to an independent expert.
  • Ancillary documents: disclosure letter, tax deed, transitional services agreement, shareholders' agreement (if under 100%), employment agreements, escrow agreement, releases of security, resolutions and transfer forms.

In practice, post-closing disputes arise far more often from price adjustments and earn-outs than from warranties.

14. Negotiation dynamics

  • Who holds the pen sets the starting point: seller in auctions, often buyer in bilateral deals.
  • Cycle: draft → markup (redline) → issues list → call → next turn. Three to five turns is normal.
  • Every issue has three positions: opening, fallback, walk-away. Openings are padded ("ask for ten to get three").
  • Client data vs market: one deal's number is an anecdote; the range across deals is the lawyer's knowledge.
  • Thermostat: the client sets how aggressive the lawyer is.
  • Carve-outs answer objections ("no liability for X, unless caused by Y"). The same protection is often stated in several places so that one survives.
  • Trading: concede what is cheap for you and valuable to them. That requires pricing each issue.
  • Escalate the last few issues to principals; record the decision and the reason.
  • What the other side did not change in a markup is information.

15. The landing model

  1. Unit: convert the term to % of price, months, or a category. Note which direction favours the buyer.
  2. Comparables: filter past deals by insurance (first), seller type, size, legal culture.
  3. Band: P25 / median / P75, with n. If n < 4, widen and say so. Never blend insured and uninsured deals.
  4. Leverage: score eight factors +1 / 0 / −1 from the buyer's side; sum; clip to ±2.
  5. Landing = median + (|score| ÷ 2) × (band edge on the stronger side − median); clamp between the two openings.

16. Kept risk and the verdict

kept risk = Σ probability × (loss − what will actually be recovered)
effective price = headline price + kept risk + deal costs
compare with: value to the buyer = standalone value + synergies − integration cost
ResultVerdictAdvice must include
Effective price ≤ value on the predicted landing; no trigger hitPursueWhat to concede cheaply
Only if certain protections are obtainedPursue with conditionsThe protections, each with a price fallback
Effective price > value on any realistic landing, or a trigger is hitWalk or re-priceThe reduction that restores the deal

Walk-away triggers (set before negotiating): a known material risk the seller will neither indemnify nor price; a loss that could ruin the buyer; an approval or consent that cannot come in time; protection with no money behind it; dishonest disclosure.

Rules: a cap above the worst credible loss is worth nothing. Risks that go to the reason for the deal belong in conditions, not indemnities. Sunk costs are irrelevant. Always state the break-even: how far an estimate must move to flip the verdict.

Deal landing memo: bottom line → leverage → issues and landings → kept risk → conditions, trades, triggers → sensitivities → sources.

17. Using AI on deal documents

  1. Structure: "Acting as a junior M&A associate for the BUYER, summarise this SPA in a table: clause number, topic, one-sentence effect, who it favours. Quote clause numbers exactly. If something is absent say 'not found'. Do not infer."
  2. Limits: "Extract every limitation on the seller's liability: de minimis, basket (tipping or deductible), caps by claim type, survival periods, exclusions, sandbagging, materiality scrape. Quote the exact text for each."
  3. Issues list: "For the buyer, list issues: clause, problem, risk rating, proposed position, fallback. Do not cite market statistics unless I provide them."
  4. Verification: "List every assertion not directly quoted from the document and mark it 'from general knowledge: verify'."

Five standard AI errors: wrong mechanism, misread qualifier (tipping vs deductible), wrong number, invented market statistic, dropped carve-out. Check each number against its clause. Never accept a statement of UAE law without a named source. Client documents only in enterprise tools with no data retention.

18. Questions worth asking a senior M&A lawyer

  1. For this clause, what would you ask for as buyer, what would the seller say, and where does it usually land?
  2. What moves it: which facts about a deal make you expect a better or worse result?
  3. Is that general, or specific to the UAE, KSA or this sector?
  4. Which parts of the other side's draft did you accept without comment, and why?
  5. How do local private deals differ from the public precedents: cap, basket type, time limits, escrow?
  6. How often do you see W&I on deals of this size here, and what do the exclusions look like now?
  7. Where do your deals actually end up in dispute?
  8. What do you check first when a new draft arrives?
  9. Which clauses do you treat differently when the target is onshore rather than DIFC or ADGM?
  10. What would make you tell a client to walk?
