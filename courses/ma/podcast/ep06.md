# Episode 6: Due diligence: routing every finding

**Sam:** You are buying a complex system where you have zero visibility into the actual state, the logs have been scrubbed by the outgoing admin, and the default SLA is 'not my problem'.

**Nadia:** That is exactly the common-law starting point for a sale of shares. Caveat emptor. Buyer beware. The seller has run the business for years, you have seen it for weeks, and that gap is pure information asymmetry.

**Sam:** Which means any unpatched vulnerability you do not find, and do not get a contractual promise about, is entirely your loss. Welcome to episode six: Due diligence, routing every finding.

**Nadia:** The seller has no general duty to volunteer their problems. They must not lie, but they are perfectly entitled to stay silent. Due diligence is the buyer's investigation of the target before committing. It is not an audit, and it does not aim at completeness. Its output is a list of decisions.

**Sam:** But if we are demanding warranties in the contract anyway, why are we spending millions on diligence fees? Just make the seller promise the system works and sue them if it fails.

**Nadia:** Because a warranty claim pays out in damages years later, after a fight, subject to financial caps, and only if the seller still has money. Finding the problem today and cutting the price is worth far more. Some problems are fatal to the deal thesis, and no payment fixes them.

**Sam:** Plus, warranties are negotiated. You need to know where the bodies are buried to know which promises to fight for. Diligence handles what you can find. Warranties handle what you cannot.

**Nadia:** Exactly. They are complements. The machinery that feeds this process starts with the data room. It is a secure folder structure where the seller uploads documents. They control what goes in, when it goes in, and who sees it. Sensitive data might be restricted to a clean team of outside advisers.

**Sam:** And we submit written questions through the Q and A process, which sellers often cap in auctions. But in an auction, the seller usually tries to frame the narrative first by providing a Vendor Due Diligence report, a VDD, prepared by their own advisers.

**Nadia:** Which speeds up the process but creates a structural problem regarding reliance. The adviser's duty is owed to its client, the seller.

**Sam:** Let's test the failure mode on that. I am in an auction. I get a legal VDD report from the seller's law firm. It explicitly says there is no material litigation. I win the bid, we close, and I instantly discover a massive pending lawsuit. I never received a reliance letter from that law firm. Who am I suing?

**Nadia:** Likely nobody, which is caveat emptor operating exactly as designed.

**Sam:** Wait, the law firm wrote a negligent report that I read.

**Nadia:** They wrote it for the seller. Without a reliance letter, they owe you no duty. The SPA will say you did not rely on anything outside the contract, which cuts off a misrepresentation claim against the seller, absent fraud. A VDD report without reliance is just information. It is not protection.

**Sam:** So my only route is if the SPA contains a litigation warranty and the seller did not formally tell me of the claim as an exception to it.

**Nadia:** Correct. Which is why your own diligence matters. But a mid-sized target has thousands of documents. Reviewing all of them equally is slow, expensive, and buries the actual problems. We cut scope using materiality thresholds.

**Sam:** This is where I see buyers make administrative decisions instead of risk decisions. If you set the threshold to only review contracts above one million dirhams a year, whatever falls below that is unreviewed risk you have to cover some other way.

**Nadia:** Let's run a scoping scenario. You are buying a SaaS company. They have four thousand customers on standard online terms, twelve enterprise customers on negotiated contracts making up fifty-five percent of revenue, and one reseller agreement that has produced almost zero revenue so far.

**Sam:** And the catch on the reseller agreement?

**Nadia:** It grants the reseller exclusivity in the Middle East for all current and future products of the company and its Affiliates. Your budget allows detailed review of exactly fifteen contracts. What is the scope?

**Sam:** If I sort by revenue, I read the top fifteen and miss the reseller entirely because it earns nothing. But that reseller contract is a fatal contagion. 'Affiliates' means after closing, it binds my entire corporate group. It blocks my own existing products in the region.

**Nadia:** So you do not scope by revenue alone. You read the twelve enterprise contracts, the standard template to cover the four thousand normal users in one read, and that reseller agreement. You scope by what can hurt the deal thesis, then by size.

**Sam:** The output of that scoping is the red flag report. I don't want a descriptive report summarizing every document, costing three times as much just to tell me the target has a normal lease. A red flag report is an exceptions-only filter tuned for decisions.

**Nadia:** Which brings us to the core skill of this module. A finding with no consequence in the contract or the price was a waste of fees. You must route every finding to exactly one of five outcomes. Price reduction, specific indemnity, pre-closing condition, warranty, or walk away.

**Sam:** Let's run the decision rule in order. First: is it fatal to the reason for the deal, or unlawful to take on?

**Nadia:** Walk away. No price compensates. Second: can it be fixed before closing?

**Sam:** Make it a condition precedent. I do not close until it is fixed. Third: is it known, and can you put a number on it?

**Nadia:** Price reduction. Deduct it as a debt-like item or a lower EBITDA. Fourth: is it known, but the amount or occurrence is uncertain?

**Sam:** Specific indemnity. The seller promises to reimburse that identified loss, one for one, if it arises. And finally: nothing was found, but the risk cannot be ruled out?

**Nadia:** Warranty. The seller states the facts are true and pays damages if they are not. It forces the seller to reveal what they know.

**Sam:** Let's put numbers to the price bucket. I agreed to buy a business at eight times EBITDA. Financial diligence shows the largest customer renegotiated last month, permanently cutting the target's annual profit by one point five million dirhams. Tax diligence finds two million dirhams of unpaid tax for a prior year, undisputed. What total price reduction am I demanding?

**Nadia:** Both are known and quantifiable, so both route to price. The only question is which one goes through the multiple.

**Sam:** The contract repricing is recurring, so it reduces the EBITDA. We multiply that: eight times one point five million is twelve million. The tax is a one-off historical failure. It does not multiply. It is a debt-like item. So we deduct the straight two million. Total price reduction is fourteen million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Fourteen million. Certain, immediate, and no later claim needed. Now change the facts to test the indemnity bucket. A former distributor is suing the target for ten million dirhams. The seller's lawyers rate the claim as weak. The seller offers a warranty that there is no litigation, but they formally disclose the claim to you. How are you protected?

**Sam:** Not at all on this claim. The warranty is true: there is no litigation other than what is disclosed. Disclosure removes it from the warranty's reach. And warranty insurance excludes what diligence found.

**Nadia:** Correct. A warranty does not protect against a known problem.

**Sam:** But a ten million dirham price cut overpays for a weak claim. The seller would walk. This is known, but unquantified. So I need a specific indemnity. If the lawsuit succeeds, they pay.

**Nadia:** Yes, but an indemnity is only as good as the person giving it. Say diligence quantifies an under-accrual of employee benefits at one point three million dirhams, undisputed. Your seller is a private equity fund in its final year. They will distribute the proceeds and dissolve. They offer no price change, but an uncapped specific indemnity. Do you accept?

**Sam:** I decline for two reasons. First, the rule: it is known and quantified, so it belongs in price. Second, an uncapped indemnity from an entity about to distribute its cash and dissolve is an SLA backed by a bankrupt vendor. It is worth zero.

**Nadia:** Exactly. 'Uncapped' adds nothing to an empty pocket. If the seller insists on an indemnity, you need the amount in escrow. Economically, an escrow is a price deduction held in suspense.

**Sam:** Let's route a fixable problem. The target's core algorithm was written four years ago by a contractor. There is no written IP assignment. The contractor is friendly, still does occasional work, and lives locally.

**Nadia:** The temptation here is to ask for a price reduction equal to the cost of rewriting the algorithm, or rely on a warranty that the company owns its IP.

**Sam:** Both are terrible trades. A warranty is the wrong tool for a known defect, and damages arrive too late if a competitor has already licensed the code. A rewrite-cost price cut leaves me owning a product I have no right to. The fix is a condition precedent: one signed assignment from the contractor before closing removes the risk entirely.

**Nadia:** Fixing beats pricing whenever it is available. But tell me, as a former CTO, why is 'the company owns its code' a legal question and not a technical one?

**Sam:** Because authorship and ownership are different state machines. Under the default rule in many legal systems, a contractor, unlike an employee, owns what they create unless they assign it in writing. The code can be in the company's repository, written to spec, fully paid for, and still legally belong to the contractor. Technical diligence tells you the code works. Legal diligence tells you we can stop others from using it.

**Nadia:** Now let's push that scenario to the edge. The contractor emigrated, cannot be traced, and the algorithm is the entire reason you want this company. The seller offers an uncapped indemnity for any IP claim by the contractor.

**Sam:** I treat it as potentially fatal. Money cannot give me ownership. The risk here is not a financial cost, it is the loss of exclusive rights to the core asset. If they can stop me using it, the deal thesis is dead. You cannot indemnify a fatal flaw. I walk, unless the seller can establish title another way.

**Nadia:** Let's look at the true unknown. The target's factory has operated on the same site for thirty years. The data room contains no environmental reports. Management says there has never been a problem. A full soil survey takes three months, and the seller will not allow it in an auction.

**Sam:** I have nothing found, so nothing to quantify, fix, or indemnify specifically. This goes to environmental warranties. It shifts the risk to the party with better information and prompts them to disclose anything they do know.

**Nadia:** And because environmental problems surface slowly, you press for a longer claim period for this warranty. But what if you actually knew there was contamination, the seller didn't disclose it, and you signed anyway? Can you claim?

**Sam:** That is sandbagging. Claiming despite having knowledge before signing. The ABA 2025 study on US deals showed sixty-eight percent were silent on it, twenty-eight percent expressly allowed it, and four percent forbade it.

**Nadia:** So if the contract is silent, the default varies by jurisdiction. The practical consequence is that what your diligence team knows can be held against you. Findings must be routed, not just filed.

**Sam:** Which explains why the seller's team always says, 'Everything is in the data room.' That is an attack vector. If the SPA deems the whole data room generally disclosed against the warranties, every document my team did not read is a risk I just accepted.

**Nadia:** Exactly. A large, disorganized data room uploaded late becomes a liability shield for the seller. Your response is to insist on specific disclosure only. Each exception must be stated against the warranty it qualifies. And disclosure must be 'fair'—detailed enough for a reasonable buyer to identify the nature and scope of the matter.

**Sam:** Let's route a compound finding. Compliance diligence finds six percent of the target's revenue comes from a distributor reselling into a sanctioned country. The seller says, 'Just terminate the distributor after closing.' I am a listed group with US investors.

**Nadia:** This finding has three parts, taking three different routes. First, the ongoing conduct is fixable, so it becomes a pre-closing condition. You must not own the business while it continues, since liability can attach to the new owner.

**Sam:** Second, the historic exposure is known but unquantified. We keep a specific indemnity for past breaches.

**Nadia:** Third, the open question: is it only this distributor? You require the exposure assessed before closing. If it shows the problem is wider, it is fatal, and you walk. Notice we did not reduce the price by six percent.

**Sam:** Because a price cut only prices the lost revenue and completely ignores the liability risk. Let's talk about the toolset finding these issues. CMS 2026 reports AI tools were used in sixty percent of deals involving legal technology, double the previous year, with seventy-two percent reporting efficiency gains.

**Nadia:** AI is excellent at extracting defined clause types—change of control, assignment, exclusivity—from thousands of contracts. But the failure modes are specific.

**Sam:** I run an AI tool across two thousand customer contracts. It reports: 'No change of control clauses found.' The tempting response is to accept it because clause extraction is what these tools do well. What is the most likely failure?

**Nadia:** Cross-references. The tool looks for a labeled clause and misses an 'assignment' definition that deems a change in ownership to be an assignment. The anti-assignment clause does the work, and the tool misses it. Re-running the prompt or asking for a confidence score just tests consistency, not correctness.

**Sam:** So the follow-up check is manually verifying how 'assignment' is defined and looking for termination rights triggered elsewhere. We also have a confidentiality failure mode if client documents are put into consumer tools.

**Nadia:** And responsibility does not move. The UAE Ministry of Justice's 2026 guiding principles and the DIFC Courts' practice guidance both call for human review of AI output. AI is strong at extraction, where the answer is in the document, and weak at routing, where the answer depends on the deal thesis.

**Sam:** Since we are in the UAE context, what are the specific diligence items here? AI still struggles with Arabic originals.

**Nadia:** Arabic is a recognized gap. Constitutional documents, court papers, and trade licences are in Arabic, and onshore, the Arabic text generally prevails. You must budget for a lawyer who reads the original. You also check licences. Every company holds a trade licence listing permitted activities; check they only do those.

**Sam:** We also check foreign ownership history. The current companies law made one hundred percent foreign ownership the default outside strategic activities from January second, 2022. But before that, mainland companies needed a majority UAE-national shareholder.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Many used nominee arrangements, where a local shareholder held shares for a foreign owner under side agreements. You trace how title was held and whether the unwinding was done properly. You also check Emiratisation quotas for private employers with fifty or more staff.

**Sam:** And gratuity. End-of-service benefits are commonly under-provided. Let's run the math on this. A UAE target has fifty employees. Each has exactly seven years of service, and a basic pay of twelve thousand dirhams a month. The books accrue forty thousand dirhams per employee. What is the total under-accrual?

**Nadia:** Gratuity is twenty-one days of basic pay per year for the first five years, and thirty days per year thereafter.

**Sam:** Okay, so five years times twenty-one days is one hundred and five days. Plus two years at thirty days is sixty days. Total entitlement is one hundred and sixty-five days. Daily pay is twelve thousand divided by thirty, which is four hundred dirhams.

**Nadia:** So one hundred and sixty-five days times four hundred dirhams.

**Sam:** Is sixty-six thousand dirhams per employee. The books only accrue forty thousand. The shortfall is twenty-six thousand per employee. Times fifty employees, that is one point three million dirhams. It is known and quantified, so it goes to price. The full three point three million is the debt-like item, but the one point three million is the hole in their books.

**Nadia:** Spot on. You also check VAT and federal corporate tax. Both are recent, so filing histories are short and positions are untested. Let's wrap up with three takeaways. One: diligence without routing is just reading. Every finding must become a price reduction, a specific indemnity, a condition precedent, a warranty, or a walk away.

**Sam:** Two: warranties do not cover known problems, and indemnities from empty entities do not pay out. If you know the number, cut the price.

**Nadia:** Three: scope your diligence by what can destroy your deal thesis, not just by administrative thresholds.

**Sam:** Next time, we look at how the SPA actually works, starting with the economic engine of the deal: the price mechanics.
