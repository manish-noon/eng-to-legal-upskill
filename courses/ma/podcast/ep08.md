# Episode 8: Warranties and disclosure: the information game

**Sam:** The default rule in a share sale is caveat emptor. Buyer beware. The seller has no general duty to volunteer problems, and you take the target company exactly as you find it. But the seller has run the business for years. You have had six weeks in a data room.

**Nadia:** That is a textbook information asymmetry. If you leave it unfixed, a rational buyer has to assume the worst and discount the price, which ultimately hurts honest sellers the most. This is episode eight, Warranties and disclosure: the information game.

**Sam:** So if a seller knows the company is clean, they need a credible way to signal that to the buyer to avoid the discount. The classic market for lemons result.

**Nadia:** Exactly. And warranties are a credible signal precisely because they cost a dishonest seller more than an honest one. A warranty is a contractual statement of fact about the target, made by the seller, with damages payable if it turns out to be untrue.

**Sam:** It does two separate jobs, right? Before signing, it forces disclosure. Warranties are drafted as absolutes. "There is no litigation." The seller only escapes liability by listing the exceptions. So the warranty schedule is basically a questionnaire with a financial penalty for wrong answers.

**Nadia:** Yes, most of its value is delivered before you sign, because whatever comes out changes the deal or the price. The second job is adjusting the price after closing. For anything untrue that was not disclosed, you get money back.

**Sam:** Even if the seller was honest and careful?

**Nadia:** Even then. A warranty allocates the risk of the unknown. It is not a statement about the seller's good faith.

**Sam:** Let's categorize these statements. Title and capacity warranties say the seller actually owns the shares, free of third-party rights, and has the power to sell them. If those are false, you didn't receive the thing you paid for.

**Nadia:** Which is why they are fundamental warranties. They carry higher financial limits. Then you have the business warranties. Accounts, tax, contracts, employees, IP, litigation.

**Sam:** The drafting varies by category. A title warranty is absolute. "The Seller is the sole legal and beneficial owner of the Shares, free from all Encumbrances." An encumbrance being any third-party right, like a pledge or an option.

**Nadia:** Compare that to litigation. "No Group Company is engaged in any litigation, and so far as the Seller is aware none is threatened." It is absolute for existing claims, but limited to the seller's awareness for threatened ones.

**Sam:** And the accounts warranty imports a totally different standard. "The Accounts give a true and fair view of the assets, liabilities and financial position." That brings in an accounting standard. If there's a breach, I have to prove it with accountants, not lawyers.

**Nadia:** Correct. The next structural issue is who actually gives these warranties.

**Sam:** Suppose I buy all the shares. A private equity fund holds eighty-five percent and three managers hold the other fifteen. Who stands behind the warranties?

**Nadia:** Private equity funds refuse business warranties. They didn't run the company, and they have to distribute proceeds to their investors. They will give title and capacity only. The business warranties have to come from the management team.

**Sam:** But the managers only have a fraction of the proceeds. Their liability will be capped at a small percentage of their payout. If six months later the accounts turn out to be materially wrong, what is my position?

**Nadia:** You can only claim against the managers, up to their low cap, and most of the loss stays with you. Liability follows who gave the warranty, not who got the money.

**Sam:** The fund warranted ownership of the shares, which was true. A title warranty says nothing about what the shares are worth. That sounds like a massive structural gap in private equity exits.

**Nadia:** It is. That gap is exactly what warranty insurance was built to fill, which we cover in module nine.

**Sam:** What if there is no private equity fund? Just three founders selling the business. Say they hold sixty, thirty, and ten percent of the shares.

**Nadia:** Then you are negotiating how liability is shared among them. It will either be several liability, or joint and several liability.

**Sam:** Several liability means each seller is liable only for its own share, usually its percentage of the price. Let's say a warranty breach causes a loss of five million dirhams. But the sixty percent seller is insolvent. What is the most I can recover?

**Nadia:** Thirty percent of five million is one point five million. Ten percent of five million is half a million. You recover a total of two million dirhams from the solvent sellers. The insolvent seller's three million is your loss.

**Sam:** That is a poor allocation of risk. I don't want to carry their counterparty risk. If I push for joint and several liability, I can recover a hundred percent from any one seller, right?

**Nadia:** Yes. You could take the full five million from either solvent seller. They must then chase the others for contribution. Joint and several moves the collection risk from you to the sellers. You usually see several liability for unconnected sellers, and joint and several within a family or a single corporate group.

**Sam:** Suppose a warranted asset turns out to need replacement. Can the buyer simply send the seller the invoice, or does it have to show how the defect affected the value of the shares?

**Nadia:** For an ordinary English-law share warranty claim, the usual starting point is the reduction in the value of the shares caused by the breach. The repair bill can matter as evidence. You still need to connect it to what the buyer lost.

**Sam:** The value of the shares as warranted, minus their actual value, normally assessed at the date of the sale.

**Nadia:** Exactly. And that has two consequences that point in opposite directions, depending on the type of problem.

**Sam:** Let's run the math on recurring profit. I pay eighty million dirhams for a business. That price was built from an enterprise value of eight times an EBITDA of twelve million, less net debt of sixteen million.

**Nadia:** And the accounts warranty is unqualified. After closing, you find that zero point seven five million a year of recurring revenue, at a hundred percent margin, never existed.

**Sam:** So true EBITDA is twelve million minus zero point seven five million, which is eleven point two five million. At the same eight-times multiple, the enterprise value drops to ninety million.

**Nadia:** Keep going. Net debt is unchanged.

**Sam:** Net debt is unchanged at sixteen million. The shares as warranted were ninety-six million minus sixteen, so eighty million. The shares as they actually are, are ninety million minus sixteen, which is seventy-four million. My loss is six million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Which is zero point seven five million multiplied by eight. The whole enterprise value drop falls on the shares. You paid a multiple for every dirham of profit, so you lose a multiple for every dirham missing.

**Sam:** Which is far more than the nominal repair cost of the accounting error. But what if it's a one-off liability? Say an unqualified litigation warranty, and nothing was disclosed. At the sale date, a customer claim of four million dirhams was pending.

**Nadia:** A one-off item is never multiplied, and a contingent item is discounted. If an informed buyer would have given that claim a thirty percent chance of success, they would have deducted the expected value from the price.

**Sam:** Thirty percent of four million is one point two million dirhams. So my loss is one point two million. But what if, eighteen months later, the company actually loses and pays the full four million?

**Nadia:** On the standard measure, valued at the sale date without hindsight, your warranty loss remains one point two million. You are two point eight million short. Courts sometimes look at later events when valuing a contingency, so the seller cannot rely on that being an absolute ceiling, but the planning point stands.

**Sam:** For a known, lumpy risk, a warranty is the wrong tool. An indemnity would pay the full four million.

**Nadia:** Yes. US-style agreements don't rely on this common-law measure. The seller agrees to indemnify the buyer for "Losses" from any inaccuracy in a representation, dollar for dollar. Whether a multiple can be claimed then depends on drafting, as sellers try to exclude diminution in value and multiples of earnings.

**Sam:** That explains why English sellers refuse the word "represents" and only "warrant".

**Nadia:** Precisely. Under English law, a false representation is a statement of fact that induced the other party to contract. It opens up the law of misrepresentation.

**Sam:** And the remedies for misrepresentation include rescission. Unwinding the whole sale so the parties are returned to their positions before it was made.

**Nadia:** Or damages on the tort measure, putting you back where you were before the deal, which can exceed the contract measure if you overpaid. Sellers want no part of that. They "warrant" only, and add a clause stating you relied on nothing outside the contract.

**Sam:** While US-style agreements say "represents and warrants" throughout because the remedy is the contractual indemnification article anyway, and is usually stated to be exclusive.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Now, an unqualified warranty puts the whole risk of the unknown on the seller. Let's look at how sellers hand part of that risk back to the buyer using qualifiers. Starting with knowledge, or awareness.

**Sam:** This is a massive filter. Take an IP warranty. "So far as the Seller is aware, the Company does not infringe third-party IP." Awareness is defined as the actual knowledge of the CEO and CFO, with no enquiry clause.

**Nadia:** In a fictional sale, the head of product receives an infringement letter before signing. Neither the chief executive nor the finance director hears about it. After closing, the claim costs three million dirhams. Has the seller broken that warranty?

**Sam:** On those facts the two named people were unaware of the claim. The definition doesn't attribute the head of product's knowledge to them. The letter alone doesn't establish a breach of this qualified warranty.

**Nadia:** I'd ask which person would actually receive a complaint about the product. Add that person to the knowledge group or require the named officers to make reasonable enquiries of them. Then check what happened before signing.

**Sam:** So if that enquiry would have uncovered the letter, the seller might be in breach even though the named officers didn't personally see it. The wording of the enquiry obligation matters.

**Nadia:** The ABA 2025 study of US deals found ninety percent used a constructive knowledge standard. Sellers accept knowledge qualifiers most readily for things outside their control, like threatened claims, but buyers resist them on matters the company itself should know.

**Sam:** The other major qualifier is materiality. "The Company has complied in all material respects with employment laws." The seller argues no company complies perfectly. I argue that "material" is undefined, so we are just agreeing to have an argument later.

**Nadia:** The buyer's counter to that qualifier is the materiality scrape. A clause telling the reader to ignore materiality qualifiers for stated purposes.

**Sam:** Let's test a single scrape versus a double scrape. The warranty says the company has complied in all material respects with employment laws. Price is eighty million. After closing, I find two unrelated underpayments costing one hundred and fifty thousand, and one hundred thousand dirhams. Neither is material alone or together.

**Nadia:** Under which drafting is there a breach, and what loss enters the claim?

**Sam:** With no scrape, there is no breach. With a single scrape, the qualifier is ignored only when calculating loss. But I never reach the loss stage because the qualifier stays in place for deciding if there's a breach. So still no breach.

**Nadia:** Correct. And with a double scrape?

**Sam:** A double scrape ignores "material" for both establishing breach and calculating loss. The warranty reads as strict compliance. Any failure is a breach. So I have a breach, and the full two hundred and fifty thousand dirhams enters the claim.

**Nadia:** Assuming it passes the financial thresholds in module eight, yes. The seller will argue that a double scrape deletes the exact qualifier they just spent days negotiating.

**Sam:** And my counter is that the financial thresholds already filter small claims. A materiality word plus a threshold filters twice.

**Nadia:** Let's change the numbers. Same deal, single scrape for loss only. You find three failures costing nine hundred thousand, one hundred and fifty thousand, and one hundred thousand dirhams. The parties agree the nine hundred thousand failure is material.

**Sam:** Step one is breach, decided with the qualifier in place. The nine hundred thousand failure is material, so the warranty is breached.

**Nadia:** And step two is loss. The single scrape removes the word "material", so loss is measured against full compliance. Nine hundred thousand, plus one hundred and fifty, plus one hundred. Total claimable loss is one point one five million dirhams.

**Sam:** Without the scrape, the seller would argue only the nine hundred thousand counts. With a double scrape, the answer is also one point one five million. The double scrape only changes the outcome when no single failure is material.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Let's move to disclosure. Warranties are given "except as Disclosed". In English-style deals, the exceptions sit in a separate disclosure letter from the seller to the buyer, delivered at signing.

**Sam:** This is the most important mechanism in the module. A warranty protects you only against what you were not told. Once an issue is disclosed, the warranty is useless for it.

**Nadia:** If the seller specifically discloses, against the litigation warranty, a customs dispute with a stated maximum exposure of three million dirhams, and you sign without further protection...

**Sam:** When the company later loses and pays three million, I recover nothing. The matter was fairly disclosed, so it is not a breach. The seller's expectations of winning or losing are irrelevant.

**Nadia:** A known issue must go to price, a condition, or a specific indemnity. Disclosure is the moment risk moves to you. It has two parts: specific disclosures against numbered warranties, and general disclosures, which are whole categories deemed known to you.

**Sam:** Specific disclosures are fine. They are a list of known defects I can price one by one. What I reject is general disclosures. The seller offers to disclose "all matters contained in the data room".

**Nadia:** Why is that worse for you than two hundred specific disclosures?

**Sam:** Because it's a transfer of search cost and error risk. Any document among thousands that reveals a problem defeats my claim, whether or not anyone on my team understood it. The seller no longer has to think about which facts contradict which warranty.

**Nadia:** If you must accept a data-room disclosure, you insist on a frozen copy of the data room so there is no argument later, and you insist on the fair disclosure standard.

**Sam:** "Disclosed" must mean fairly disclosed, with sufficient detail to enable a reasonable buyer to identify the nature and scope of the matter.

**Nadia:** Let's test that standard. The SPA uses fair disclosure and deems the data room disclosed. Among four thousand documents is a board minute with one line: "The board noted recent correspondence with the regulator."

**Sam:** Nothing else refers to it. The correspondence was actually a threat to revoke the company's main licence. After closing, the licence is suspended. Does the disclosure defeat my claim under the compliance warranty?

**Nadia:** No. Under fair disclosure, the test is what the disclosed material tells a reasonable buyer, not whether a clue existed. One line requiring further enquiry reveals neither the nature, a revocation threat, nor the scope, the main licence.

**Sam:** A hint is not enough. If the SPA had deemed me to know everything "contained in or referred to in" the data room with no fairness test, the seller's argument would be much stronger. Definitions dictate the outcome.

**Nadia:** Note that US-style deals usually avoid the data room dump entirely. They use disclosure schedules, where exceptions are listed section by section, forming part of the agreement, and a disclosure normally qualifies only its own section.

**Sam:** What happens if a problem isn't disclosed, but I find it anyway? Say my own diligence report flags that a key licence has lapsed. The seller didn't disclose it. I say nothing, sign at full price, and claim under the unqualified licences warranty later.

**Nadia:** That is sandbagging. Claiming for a breach you already knew about when you signed. Three drafting positions exist. Pro-sandbagging, where your rights are not affected by any investigation or knowledge you have.

**Sam:** My view as a buyer. I paid for the warranty. My diligence shouldn't reduce my protection.

**Nadia:** The seller's view is anti-sandbagging. You may not claim to the extent your deal team had actual knowledge of the facts at signing. You knew, you priced it, you cannot be paid twice.

**Sam:** And the third position is silent, leaving it to the governing law. The ABA 2025 study found sixty-eight percent of US deals silent, twenty-eight percent pro-sandbagging, and four percent anti-sandbagging.

**Nadia:** If an English-law SPA is silent, there is a tension. The warranty was untrue and not disclosed, so there is a breach. Contract liability is strict. But English damages compensate loss. A seller will argue that a buyer who knew the truth priced it in and suffered no loss.

**Sam:** So there is a breach, but recovery is uncertain. Which means staying silent was a bad trade. Raising the point before signing would have earned a price reduction or an indemnity, both of which are certain.

**Nadia:** Yes. Do not treat a pro-sandbagging clause in an English-law SPA as fully reliable for things you actually knew. UAE deals of any size are usually written under English, DIFC or ADGM law, so this analysis applies. If onshore UAE law governs, ask counsel how courts treat buyer knowledge.

**Sam:** One final timing issue. Warranties speak at signing. If closing is weeks later, I want them repeated at closing, so that events in the gap are the seller's risk.

**Nadia:** The seller will reply that they cannot warrant the future, and will ask for the right to update the disclosure letter at closing.

**Sam:** If updated disclosure qualifies the repeated warranties, repetition gives me nothing. Say warranties are repeated, the seller can deliver an updated disclosure letter, and there is no termination right. In the gap, the largest customer gives notice to terminate. The seller discloses it in the update.

**Nadia:** You must close, and you have no warranty claim. The gap risk is yours. Repetition creates a claim only for what is not disclosed, and the update disclosed it.

**Sam:** Nothing in a share purchase agreement adjusts the price automatically without a mechanism. That combination looks like protection and delivers none. I need either updated disclosure for information only, or a walk right.

**Nadia:** US-style deals handle this with a bring-down condition. The buyer need not close unless the representations are still true at closing, usually tested subject to a material adverse effect standard. That is a walk right, separate from the damages claim.

**Sam:** Let's pull this together. The seller offers me a choice on a software target. Option one: an unqualified IP warranty, with general data-room disclosure. Option two: a knowledge-qualified IP warranty, with specific disclosure only.

**Nadia:** The method is to list the fact patterns where you end up uncompensated for each package. Option one leaves you the risk of anything in the data room you failed to understand. But the seller bears everything truly unknown.

**Sam:** Option two leaves me the risk of everything the seller's named people didn't know. For a software target, IP infringement by third-party code is most of the real risk. The unknown-unknowns. So option one is better, provided I insist on fair disclosure and my technical team reads the IP folders properly.

**Nadia:** Correct. Time to wrap up. Three takeaways. One, warranties force information out before you sign. A known issue must be priced or indemnified, because once disclosed, the warranty cannot protect you.

**Sam:** Two, English warranty damages pay the diminution in value, not the repair cost. A recurring profit misstatement gets multiplied; a contingent lawsuit gets discounted.

**Nadia:** Three, definitions decide the claim. The exact wording of the knowledge qualifier, the materiality scrape, and the fair disclosure standard will dictate whether you recover a dirham.

**Sam:** Next time, we look at the financial limits of those claims, and how warranty insurance bridges the gap.
