# Episode 7: Reading an 80,000-word SPA in an hour

**Sam:** You get an eighty-thousand-word Share Purchase Agreement at ten p.m. The partner wants a summary by eleven. If you sit down and read it front to back, you fail.

**Nadia:** You do. Because you are reading a contract like a novel. This is 'Reading an eighty-thousand-word SPA in an hour'.

**Sam:** As an engineer, I look at an SPA as a state machine. It defines states: signed but not closed, closed, closed with a problem, and never closed. It defines the transitions between those states, and who pays when a transition fails.

**Nadia:** That is exactly how a senior M&A lawyer sees it. Most of those eighty thousand words are just machinery to handle edge cases. The actual money sits in only four places.

**Sam:** Price, warranties, limitations, and indemnities.

**Nadia:** Plus the definitions those four rely on. Conditions and termination provisions decide whether there is a deal at all. The rest is plumbing.

**Sam:** Which brings us to the reading protocol. The rule is you don't read eighty thousand words. You read about eight thousand, chosen in strict dependency order. And you explicitly say warranties come last.

**Nadia:** Yes. Warranties are just the seller's statements of fact about the target business. They often take up thirty pages. But their value is entirely controlled by the limitations on liability.

**Sam:** So a broad warranty that says 'the software doesn't infringe any patents' looks great. But if it has a tiny financial cap, a narrow definition of recoverable losses, and a twelve-month time limit...

**Nadia:** It is worth far less than a narrow warranty with no limits. Reading thirty pages of warranties first tells you what could go wrong, but it tells you nothing about what you could actually recover. Read the limits, then read only the warranties that matter to your diligence findings.

**Sam:** So we start with the parties and recitals. Two minutes. Who is selling what to whom, and is there a guarantor standing behind either side.

**Nadia:** Then you jump to the price definitions and the price clause. Twelve minutes. You trace the purchase price, the adjustment mechanism, the escrow, and any earn-out. You follow each defined term once.

**Sam:** Then conditions and termination. Ten minutes. What can stop closing, who controls it, the long-stop date, and what is payable if the deal dies. That's deal certainty.

**Nadia:** Finally, you spend fifteen minutes on indemnification or limitations. The basket type and size, the cap, the time limits, exclusive remedy clauses, fraud carve-outs, and insurance.

**Sam:** Let's pause on indemnification. Our source module makes a sharp distinction between US-style and English-style deals. Most of the public filings we train on are US-style.

**Nadia:** The concepts map across, but the mechanics differ. In an English-style SPA, which is common in UAE, DIFC, and ADGM practice, a warranty claim is a claim for damages for breach of contract.

**Sam:** Meaning the buyer has to prove loss. Broadly, the fall in the value of the shares.

**Nadia:** Correct. If a warranty says 'all employees have written contracts', and it turns out forty staff do not, but the business runs fine and the shares are worth what the buyer paid...

**Sam:** Then what can the buyer recover? The cost of putting forty contracts in place as a debt?

**Nadia:** Little or nothing. Breach is established, but damages require proof of loss. No loss, no recovery. That is why buyers push for indemnities for specific risks they care about. An indemnity is a one-for-one reimbursement.

**Sam:** And in US-style?

**Nadia:** US documents use an indemnification article as the general remedy for any breach of a representation. The buyer still has to show 'Losses', but those are defined by the contract and commonly include costs incurred.

**Sam:** If I have ten minutes with a US-style SPA, and the partner asks, 'If we find a breach after closing, what is our realistic recovery from the seller?', what searches answer that fastest?

**Nadia:** You search 'R and W Insurance Policy', 'Cap', 'exclusive remedy', and 'Fraud'.

**Sam:** Not 'represents and warrants'?

**Nadia:** No. Recovery from the seller is set by the limits. If there is a Warranty and Indemnity insurance policy—RWI in the US—the seller's cap is often a small fraction of the price, or even nil.

**Sam:** And the exclusive remedy clause makes that the whole answer, except for fraud.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The structure often says the indemnification provisions are the sole and exclusive remedy, and the sole source of recovery for ordinary breaches is the insurance policy.

**Sam:** So if a non-fundamental representation is false, and the insurer denies the claim under a policy exclusion, what is the buyer's position against the seller?

**Nadia:** The buyer has no recourse against the seller unless they can prove Fraud as defined. The policy exclusions fall entirely on the buyer. That structure strongly favours the seller, and it is very common.

**Sam:** Let's get into the definitions. You say negotiators who lose an argument in the operative clause often win it back in the definitions, where fewer people are looking.

**Nadia:** Because a definition is a function called from many places. Conceding the visible cap amount while quietly adjusting the definition of 'Losses' can produce a better result for the seller.

**Sam:** Let's run the multiple of earnings example from the module. The buyer paid eight times EBITDA. A warranty breach permanently reduces the target's annual profit by one point five million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Before any limits, the buyer's real loss is that they paid twelve million dirhams too much.

**Sam:** Right. But the definition of 'Losses' in the SPA says: 'all losses, liabilities, costs and expenses, but excluding any punitive damages, loss of profit, and any damages calculated by reference to a multiple of earnings or diminution in value'.

**Nadia:** So what is the buyer's claim worth?

**Sam:** Far less than twelve million dirhams. Only direct costs actually incurred. The lost profit is excluded, and the multiple is excluded.

**Nadia:** Exactly. That single definition removes most of the value of the warranties for earnings-related breaches. It heavily favours the seller. It should be one of the first definitions a buyer's lawyer reads.

**Sam:** Another huge one is the Knowledge definition. A warranty reads: 'To the Knowledge of the Seller, the Company is not in breach of any Data Protection Law.'

**Nadia:** The warranty is qualified. You must search for how Knowledge is defined.

**Sam:** The SPA defines it as the quote 'actual knowledge, without any duty of inquiry or investigation, of Karim Haddad and Lina Farouk.'

**Nadia:** Two named people. No duty to enquire.

**Sam:** So after closing, it emerges that the target's IT manager knew of an unreported data breach a year before signing, and never escalated it. Is the warranty breached?

**Nadia:** No. Neither of the two named individuals actually knew, and they had no duty to investigate. The warranty wasn't 'the Company complies'. It was 'these two people do not actually know of non-compliance'. That statement was true.

**Sam:** So an employee's knowledge isn't the Company's knowledge here. The definition decided the outcome, and it favours the seller on both axes: a narrow group, and no duty to enquire.

**Nadia:** A buyer would ask for constructive knowledge. What a wider group, including relevant function heads, knew or should have known after reasonable enquiry. ABA twenty-twenty-five reports that ninety percent of US deals in its sample used a constructive knowledge standard.

**Sam:** Let's look at Material Adverse Effect. MAE. It's used in conditions, meaning the buyer can refuse to close, and as a qualifier on warranties. CMS twenty-twenty-six reports MAC clauses—Material Adverse Change, same thing—are in fourteen percent of European deals against ninety-six percent in the US.

**Nadia:** It is a highly negotiated definition. It defines a serious deterioration in the business. Buyers want any material adverse change included. Sellers want a long list of exclusions.

**Sam:** Market, industry, law, war, pandemics. Let's test the carve-out from the carve-out. The MAE definition excludes changes affecting the industry, and changes in Law.

**Nadia:** But then it adds a carve-out from those exclusions: quote, 'except to the extent such changes have a disproportionate adverse effect on the Company relative to other participants in its industry.'

**Sam:** So a new regulation cuts margins across the target's industry. Competitors' profits fall about ten percent. The target's profits fall forty percent because of its product mix. 'No MAE' is a closing condition. What can the buyer argue?

**Nadia:** Layer one: materially adverse changes count. That favours the buyer. Layer two: changes in Law do not. That favours the seller. Layer three: except to the extent disproportionate. Back to the buyer.

**Sam:** So the full forty percent decline is an MAE?

**Nadia:** No. 'To the extent' means only the excess over the industry effect is counted. The disproportionate part counts. The question is whether that part alone is materially adverse. Stopping at the change in Law exclusion is the skim-reader's error.

**Sam:** Let's hit 'Indebtedness'. Every dollar defined as debt is a dollar deducted from the price. The buyer wants a long list: tax, leases, bonuses, deal costs.

**Nadia:** And the seller wants it restricted to borrowed money only.

**Sam:** The module's definition includes borrowed money, capitalized leases, unpaid pre-closing taxes, transaction expenses, and deferred revenue. It ends with 'without duplication'. Who does this favour?

**Nadia:** The buyer. Every limb beyond borrowed money is a deduction from price.

**Sam:** And what's the most contestable limb?

**Nadia:** Deferred revenue. Cash received for services not yet delivered. The buyer argues they must perform the service without being paid, so it is a debt. The seller argues the cost of performing is a fraction of the revenue, and it is just an ordinary working capital item.

**Sam:** Treating all of it as debt overstates the burden. Does the phrase 'without duplication' solve it?

**Nadia:** No. It is neutral. It prevents an item being counted twice, but it does not decide which bucket it belongs in.

**Sam:** Let's do the math on the limitations. The basket and the cap. I want to read the module's exact wording for a basket. 'The Seller shall not be liable for any Claim unless A, the Losses in respect of that Claim exceed fifty thousand dollars, and B, the aggregate of all Losses for Claims not excluded by A exceeds seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars, in which case the Seller shall be liable for the full amount of such Losses and not merely the excess.'

**Nadia:** Limb A is a de minimis. A per-claim threshold. Limb B is a tipping basket.

**Sam:** We have three claims arise. Losses of forty thousand, three hundred thousand, and six hundred thousand dollars. How much is the seller liable for?

**Nadia:** The forty-thousand-dollar claim is ignored completely because it falls below the fifty-thousand-dollar de minimis.

**Sam:** So the qualifying claims are three hundred thousand plus six hundred thousand. That equals nine hundred thousand dollars.

**Nadia:** Which exceeds the seven-hundred-and-fifty-thousand-dollar basket threshold. Because the clause says 'the full amount and not merely the excess', it tips. The seller pays nine hundred thousand dollars.

**Sam:** First dollar recovery. If the words had been 'only to the extent of the excess', it would be a deductible.

**Nadia:** And the seller would only pay one hundred and fifty thousand dollars. A few words change the outcome by seven hundred and fifty thousand dollars.

**Sam:** CMS twenty-twenty-six found eighty-seven percent of European baskets were tipping. Deductibles are reported at forty-nine percent of US baskets against thirteen percent in Europe.

**Nadia:** Market practice dictates where you start the argument, but the words dictate where you finish it.

**Sam:** Let's run another math example on caps. An SPA provides a deductible of one point five million dollars. The seller is liable only for losses in excess of the deductible. There is a cap of twelve million dollars on claims for breach of representations.

**Nadia:** Ordinary representations.

**Sam:** Right. Then it says 'the Deductible and the Cap shall not apply to breaches of Fundamental Representations, for which the Seller's liability shall not exceed the Purchase Price'. The price is one hundred and fifty million dollars.

**Nadia:** Fundamental warranties are typically title to the shares, capacity, and authority.

**Sam:** The buyer proves losses of fifteen million dollars from breaches of ordinary representations, and three million dollars from a breach of a Fundamental Representation. What is the total recovery?

**Nadia:** You calculate them separately. For the ordinary claims, you have fifteen million. Subtract the one point five million deductible. That leaves thirteen point five million.

**Sam:** But limited by the cap to twelve million.

**Nadia:** Correct. For the fundamental claim, there is no deductible and no twelve-million-dollar cap. It is well under the purchase price, so the buyer gets the three million in full.

**Sam:** Total recovery is twelve million plus three million. Fifteen million dollars.

**Nadia:** The common slips are applying the twelve-million cap to everything, resulting in twelve. Or forgetting the cap on the ordinary claims, resulting in sixteen point five.

**Sam:** Let's talk about time limits. In US documents, this is the survival clause. Section nine point one says representations survive until the date that is fifteen months after closing.

**Nadia:** And it usually contains a saver mechanism for claims that are already in progress.

**Sam:** Yes. 'Any claim asserted in writing with reasonable specificity prior to the expiration of the applicable survival period shall survive until finally resolved.'

**Nadia:** So if the buyer discovers a breach in month thirteen, sends a detailed written claim in month fourteen, and starts arbitration in month twenty. Who wins if the seller says the claim is out of time?

**Sam:** The buyer. The saver sentence says what must happen inside the fifteen-month period is a written assertion with reasonable specificity. Not the commencement of proceedings.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The claim survives until resolved. But without that last sentence, the answer could differ. Some agreements require proceedings to be started within a further period. You have to read the survival clause to its end. And note 'reasonable specificity'. A vague protective notice might not count.

**Sam:** Let's talk about the gap between signing and closing. US documents usually have a bring-down condition.

**Nadia:** Yes. The buyer need not close unless the representations are still true at closing. They are brought down to the closing date.

**Sam:** Often qualified by 'except where the failure to be true would not have a Material Adverse Effect'.

**Nadia:** Correct. English-style deals more often give warranties at signing only and resist repetition at closing. Walk rights for deterioration are uncommon in Europe.

**Sam:** And the exceptions to the statements?

**Nadia:** In English-style, it is a separate disclosure letter, and general disclosure of data rooms is common. In US-style, it is disclosure schedules, numbered to match each representation, and specific disclosure is the norm.

**Sam:** Let's hit the traps. The things that defeat a skim-reader. Cross-references are one. 'Subject to Section nine point four' means you haven't finished reading the sentence.

**Nadia:** And you must check that the reference points where it should. Numbering drifts between drafts.

**Sam:** Then there is the override. 'Notwithstanding anything to the contrary in this Agreement'.

**Nadia:** That phrase makes its clause defeat everything else. If you search for it and find two such clauses in conflict, it is a drafting failure that will end in dispute.

**Sam:** Here is how an override gets weaponised. Section nine point three says the aggregate liability of the seller shall not exceed twelve million dollars. Section nine point six says, 'Notwithstanding anything to the contrary, nothing shall limit any Person's liability for Fraud.'

**Nadia:** The eye is drawn to the override. But you have to read the definition of Fraud.

**Sam:** The definition says: 'actual and intentional common law fraud by the Seller with respect to the making of the representations and warranties in Article III'.

**Nadia:** Very narrow.

**Sam:** The target's CFO, who is not the seller, recklessly gives false figures in a management presentation. Does the cap apply?

**Nadia:** The cap very likely applies. The conduct falls outside the defined term of Fraud on three counts. It requires intent, so recklessness fails. It must be by the Seller, and the CFO is not the seller. And it must be in the making of Article III representations, which a presentation is not.

**Sam:** So the override is real, but it is only as wide as the capitalised term. The definition quietly takes the substance back. It favours the seller.

**Nadia:** Always read the definition of Fraud.

**Sam:** What about when we're reading a publicly filed SPA? The ones filed with the US securities regulator.

**Nadia:** You have to watch for unfiled schedules and redactions. Public filings usually omit the disclosure schedules.

**Sam:** Meaning you can read every representation and still not know what exceptions were listed against them.

**Nadia:** Exactly. And commercially sensitive text is often marked with brackets and asterisks.

**Sam:** So if the SPA says 'The aggregate amount recoverable shall not exceed bracket, three asterisks, bracket.' And a junior on my team suggests writing 'cap approximately ten percent of price, consistent with market' in the summary.

**Nadia:** That is a terrible idea. A summary reports what the document says. The document says a cap exists and withholds the number. Guessing a market figure invents a fact and a statistic at once.

**Sam:** So I just write 'No cap'?

**Nadia:** No, that is false. You write 'Cap exists; amount redacted in the filed version; not determinable from this document'. Omitting it entirely hides something the reader needs to know, including that it is unknown.

**Sam:** What if I search a US-style SPA for 'Cap' and find absolutely nothing?

**Nadia:** A failed search is a question, not a finding. It could be a different label: 'maximum', 'aggregate liability', or 'Indemnity Escrow Amount'. Or, there is no seller liability at all because of RWI insurance. Or the limits sit in an unfiled schedule.

**Sam:** So I record 'not found under these terms' and list what I searched. I don't just assume it's uncapped.

**Nadia:** Genuinely uncapped deals are rare and very buyer-friendly. Assume you haven't found it yet.

**Sam:** Which brings us to how to write a clause summary that a partner will actually accept. You have a four-field structure.

**Nadia:** Clause number, what it says, who it favours, and a quote.

**Sam:** The rule is state the effect, not a paraphrase. And if the answer turns on a definition, cite the definition as part of the entry.

**Nadia:** Yes. Never summarize just the heading. A section headed 'Indemnification by Seller', when read with its definitions and overrides, might provide almost no indemnification at all. Quote the operative words so the reader can verify without opening the document.

**Sam:** Like quoting 'actual and intentional common law fraud by the Seller' for the fraud carve-out. It shows exactly why it favours the seller.

**Nadia:** Exactly.

**Sam:** We're at the hour. Give me the three-point takeaway for surviving an eighty-thousand-word SPA.

**Nadia:** One, read in dependency order. Price, conditions, limits, then definitions. Warranties come last, because their value is determined elsewhere. Two, definitions are where deals are won. Re-read the definition every time a money clause uses one. Three, track the overrides and carve-outs. A narrow definition of Fraud or a 'notwithstanding' clause can erase what you thought you negotiated.
