# Episode 16: Kept risk and the pursue or walk call

**Sam:** The lawyer comes back from the negotiation and says, 'Great news, I got them to agree to a sixteen percent cap.' And the client looks at them and asks, 'Is that good?'

**Nadia:** And if the lawyer says, 'Yes, sixteen is higher than ten,' they have failed. A client does not want to know the cap is sixteen percent. They want to know: on the terms I can realistically get, should I do this deal? Welcome to episode sixteen: Kept risk and the pursue or walk call.

**Sam:** We spent module fourteen predicting where the cap, the basket, and the time limit will land. But prediction without valuation just tells you what the terms are. It doesn't tell you if they matter. To answer the client's question, you have to convert those legal terms into money.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The link between the terms in the SPA and actual money is the list of things that could go wrong. We call it the risk register. It is a table of the specific bad outcomes you know about or reasonably fear, taken mostly from due diligence.

**Sam:** It is essentially a failure mode matrix. And every row in that matrix needs four data points. First, 'p', the probability the risk turns into an actual loss. Rough estimates are fine here—ten percent, twenty-five percent, fifty percent. You get these from the advisers.

**Nadia:** Second, 'L', the loss to the buyer if it does happen. This comes from financial and legal diligence. And remember from module seven: if it is a recurring profit hit, it is worth the hit times the price multiple.

**Sam:** Third is the month it surfaces after closing. You look at audit cycles, tax assessment windows, contract renewal dates. It's basically the latency of the failure. And fourth is the protection type. Which part of the SPA answers for this risk? General warranty, fundamental warranty, specific indemnity, insurance, or nothing.

**Nadia:** There is one mandatory addition. You must add one row for unknown unknowns. A general warranty breach nobody has spotted. Without it, the general cap and basket protect against nothing in your model and will look mathematically worthless, which is wrong.

**Sam:** Okay, so we have our matrix. How do we calculate the money? You use a concept called kept risk. For each risk, the SPA at the landed terms lets the buyer recover some amount, 'R'. The rest stays with the buyer.

**Nadia:** The formula is kept risk equals the probability, 'p', multiplied by the difference between the loss, 'L', and the recovery, 'R'. So, 'p' times 'L' minus 'R'.

**Sam:** And 'R' is computed by passing the loss through the limits exactly as we did in module eight. Is it in time? Is it above the de minimis? Does the total clear the basket? Does the cap bite? Then you add what the insurer pays. You sum that over the whole register to get total expected kept risk. Let's work an example from the source.

**Nadia:** Go ahead. Price is seventy-six million dirhams.

**Sam:** Landed terms. We have a tipping basket of one percent, which is seven hundred and sixty thousand dirhams. A cap of twenty percent. A time limit of eighteen months. We have one risk on the register: a VAT reassessment. Probability is thirty percent. Loss is one point two million dirhams. And it will surface at month twenty.

**Nadia:** First scenario: we treat it as a general warranty claim. Month twenty is after the eighteen-month time limit. So recovery, 'R', is zero. Kept risk is thirty percent of the one point two million dirham loss. That is three hundred and sixty thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** Second scenario. We model it with a negotiated time limit of twenty-four months. The claim is now in time. The one point two million loss clears the seven hundred and sixty thousand tipping basket. It is well under the twenty percent cap. So recovery is the full one point two million. Kept risk is zero.

**Nadia:** Third scenario. We get a specific indemnity for VAT. Recovery is one point two million regardless of the general limits. Kept risk is zero.

**Sam:** So for this specific buyer, fighting for six more months on the time limit is worth exactly three hundred and sixty thousand dirhams in expected value. And so is fighting for a VAT indemnity. Those aren't just legal concepts anymore; they are tradeable quantities.

**Nadia:** Exactly. But you cannot compute recovery for each risk alone and just add them up. Risks interact through the basket.

**Sam:** Right, because the basket acts like a state machine. It sits in a 'no recovery' state until claims push it over the threshold. One claim of five hundred thousand dirhams does not clear a seven hundred and sixty thousand dirham basket. Two of them do. The state flips, and you recover.

**Nadia:** Which is why the simulator handles this by weighing every combination of risks occurring together. If you have ten risks on your register, that is one thousand and twenty-four combinations. Trivial for a computer, impossible by intuition.

**Sam:** Let's test that intuition with another example. Price is fifty million dirhams. Landed terms: deductible basket of one percent, cap of ten percent, time limit eighteen months, no de minimis. One risk only on the register. Probability forty percent. Loss one point one million dirhams, surfacing at month six, covered by general warranties. What is the expected kept risk?

**Nadia:** One percent of fifty million is a basket of five hundred thousand dirhams. It is a deductible, so the buyer only recovers the excess. Loss is one point one million minus the half-million basket. Recovery is six hundred thousand. That is far below the five million dirham cap.

**Sam:** So if the risk occurs, the kept amount is the one point one million loss minus the six hundred thousand recovery. That leaves five hundred thousand dirhams. Which is exactly the basket amount. Because with a deductible that has been cleared, you always eat the basket.

**Nadia:** Correct. So expected kept risk is the forty percent probability multiplied by the five hundred thousand dirhams. Two hundred thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** Okay, let's look at a trade on that exact same deal. The buyer's lawyer comes back and says, 'I won a move from a ten percent cap to a thirty percent cap. But I had to concede six months of time limit to get it.' Was that a good trade?

**Nadia:** No. It is a terrible trade. The only modelled loss on the register is one point one million dirhams against a cap of five million. The higher cap of fifteen million adds absolutely nothing, while the shorter time limit could put a later-surfacing claim out of time.

**Sam:** Because a cap only has value in the scenarios where losses exceed it. Here nothing comes close to five million, so the thirty percent cap protects against events that aren't on the register. The buyer paid with something real—time—for something with zero expected value.

**Nadia:** The only exception is if you believe there is a tail of unknown large losses. If so, you add that row to the register with its probability and then test the cap again. Otherwise, you are negotiating for vanity.

**Sam:** Alright, so kept risk is fundamentally a cost of doing the deal. Which brings us to the effective price.

**Nadia:** Yes. Effective price is the headline price, plus the expected kept risk, plus the deal costs. Deal costs include advisers' fees, any warranty and indemnity insurance premium the buyer pays, filing fees, and the cost of delay if approvals hold up closing.

**Sam:** And you compare that effective price with the value to the buyer. Value being what the business is worth standing alone, plus the synergies the combination creates, minus the cost of integrating it.

**Nadia:** And remember, value is the client's number and the client's responsibility. Your job is to make sure it is compared with the right price.

**Sam:** Let's run the numbers on that comparison. Headline price is one hundred and twenty million dollars. Expected kept risk at the predicted landing is three point four million. Deal costs are two point two million. The buyer values the business at one hundred and thirty-one million dollars. What is the surplus?

**Nadia:** Effective price is one hundred and twenty, plus three point four, plus two point two. That is one hundred and twenty-five point six million dollars. The surplus is the value, one hundred and thirty-one, minus the effective price. That leaves five point four million dollars.

**Sam:** That surplus is basically the buyer's remaining room. They could concede terms or price worth up to five point four million in expected value and still be no worse off than walking away.

**Nadia:** Which leads directly to the three verdicts you give a board. Pursue, pursue with conditions, or walk. If effective price is at or below value on the predicted landing, and no walk-away trigger is hit, the verdict is pursue. The advice must state which terms to concede cheaply, so capital is spent only where kept risk is large.

**Sam:** If effective price is below value only if certain protections are obtained, the verdict is pursue with conditions. You name the two or three protections, each with a fallback of equal value, which is usually a price reduction.

**Nadia:** And if effective price exceeds value on any realistic landing, or a trigger is hit, the verdict is walk, or re-price. You state the price reduction that would restore the deal, and the facts that would change the answer.

**Sam:** You mentioned a walk-away trigger. That is a hard-coded abort condition fixed in advance. Under that condition, the buyer will not sign, whatever else is offered. You have to set these before negotiating to defend against the sunk-cost effect.

**Nadia:** Typical triggers include a known, material risk that the seller will cover by neither indemnity nor price. Or a required approval that cannot realistically be obtained before the long-stop date. Or any sign of dishonesty in disclosure, because warranties do not fix a counterparty you cannot trust.

**Sam:** Let's talk about the counterparty risk trigger. The empty shell. The seller is a special-purpose holding company that will distribute the sale proceeds to its owners the day after closing. It offers the buyer a one hundred percent cap, a tipping basket, and a thirty-six-month time limit, with no escrow and no guarantee. What should recovery, 'R', be for general warranty claims in our model?

**Nadia:** Close to zero. Because there will be nothing to recover from. A legal right against an empty company is worth what the company has. The generous terms cost this seller nothing, which is why it offered them. Module nine's question, 'where does the money come from?', comes before any reading of caps and baskets.

**Sam:** If they had a ten percent escrow, recovery would be capped at ten percent, regardless of the stated one hundred percent cap. The incentives dictate the reality.

**Nadia:** Another trigger is a loss that would threaten the buyer itself, whatever its probability. Expected value is the wrong tool when one outcome is ruin.

**Sam:** Right. Two risks are on the register. Risk X is a fifty percent chance of an eight hundred thousand dirham loss. Risk Y is a two percent chance of a sixty million dirham loss, which is more than the buyer's entire equity. The seller will protect only one. Which should the buyer insist on?

**Nadia:** Risk Y. Expected loss already favours Y anyway—one point two million against four hundred thousand. But more importantly, a single occurrence would be ruinous, which expected value understates. A buyer is not indifferent between a certain small cost and a small chance of ruin. Losses that threaten survival are trigger territory: cover them, insure them, or do not sign.

**Sam:** Frequency is just the wrong metric there. What about when the protection offered doesn't actually fix the failure mode? A target earns sixty percent of its revenue from one contract that the customer may end when ownership changes. The seller offers an uncapped indemnity for any loss if that happens. Why is that the wrong protection?

**Nadia:** Three reasons. First, the loss is the collapse of the business you bought, which is incredibly hard to prove and quantify as 'loss' years later. Second, the seller would owe a sum close to the whole price and may not have it.

**Sam:** And third, we didn't want compensation. We wanted the business. If the core revenue engine fails, the deal makes no sense. Risks that go to whether the deal makes sense at all belong in a condition, like customer consent before closing. Indemnities are for risks you can live with if paid.

**Nadia:** Let's look at trading. Once each term has a value in money, the negotiation becomes a set of trades. A trade is good for the buyer when what it gets is worth more to it than what it gives. The best trades are those where the two sides value the item differently.

**Sam:** Because they disagree on probabilities. A seller who is sure there is no VAT problem gives a VAT indemnity at no perceived cost. A buyer who thinks the chance is thirty percent receives something worth three hundred and sixty thousand dirhams.

**Nadia:** Here is a swap. The seller offers to change the basket of six hundred thousand dirhams from tipping to deductible. In return, the time limit is extended by six months. You estimate a thirty percent chance that claims will clear the basket. The extension brings one risk into time: twenty-five percent probability, two million dirham loss, fully recoverable if in time. What is the net expected value of the swap?

**Sam:** Cost of the deductible first. In the thirty percent of cases where the basket is cleared, the buyer loses the basket amount. Zero point three times six hundred thousand is one hundred and eighty thousand dirhams. That is what we give.

**Nadia:** And the value of the extension?

**Sam:** The extension covers a risk with a twenty-five percent chance of a two million dirham loss. Zero point two five times two million is five hundred thousand dirhams. That is what we get. Net value is five hundred thousand minus one hundred and eighty thousand. Plus three hundred and twenty thousand dirhams. Take the swap.

**Nadia:** Note the simplification there. If the newly covered claim is itself what clears the basket, the two effects interact. Which, again, is why the simulator weighs combinations.

**Sam:** Right. But every probability in this register is an estimate. How do we know our advice is robust? What if our estimate is wrong?

**Nadia:** You test sensitivity. You find the break-even: the value at which effective price equals value. If the verdict flips only when a probability moves from thirty percent to eighty percent, the advice is robust. If it flips between thirty percent and thirty-five percent, the honest advice is 'this is finely balanced and depends on this specific risk'.

**Sam:** Let's run a break-even. Price one hundred million dirhams, deal costs two million, value to the buyer one hundred and eight million. There is one uncovered risk with a twenty million dirham loss. At what probability does effective price equal value?

**Nadia:** The room is the value minus price and costs. One hundred and eight, minus one hundred, minus two. Six million dirhams. Break-even happens when the probability times the twenty million loss equals six million. So, probability is thirty percent.

**Sam:** If advisers put the probability at ten percent, we pursue. If they say 'somewhere between twenty and forty percent', the entire deal turns on that one risk. The right next step is to investigate it or cover it, not argue about the basket.

**Nadia:** What if the numbers show a shortfall? Price ninety-five million. Expected kept risk eighteen point five million. Deal costs two million. Value to the buyer one hundred and four million. What is the minimum price reduction that makes the deal break even?

**Sam:** Effective price is ninety-five, plus eighteen point five, plus two. That is one hundred and fifteen point five million. The shortfall against the one hundred and four million value is eleven point five million dirhams. So we need a price reduction of at least eleven point five million, or protections that cut kept risk by the same amount.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Offering the seller both routes—price down by eleven point five million, or these two specific protections—is how you turn a likely walk into a counter-proposal.

**Sam:** But walking is hard. Four months into a deal, diligence finds a risk that takes effective price above value. The client says, 'We have spent two million on advisers, we cannot walk now.' How do you handle that?

**Nadia:** You tell them the two million is spent either way and is irrelevant. You only compare future costs and value. Then you re-price, cover the risk, or walk. Costs already incurred do not change with the decision, so they belong on neither side. That is the sunk-cost effect.

**Sam:** What if the margin is just razor-thin? The model says pursue, with a surplus of half a million dirhams on a one hundred and eighty million dirham deal. Do you advise them to pursue because the numbers support it, or walk because the margin is too thin?

**Nadia:** Neither. A surplus of zero point three percent of price is inside the error of any probability estimate. Calling it a clear pursue or a clear walk claims a precision you do not have. The accurate advice is: 'The deal is at break-even within the accuracy of our estimates. It depends on two inputs, which are these, and here is what would firm each one up.'

**Sam:** So how do we package all this up for the board? They aren't going to read the simulator logs.

**Nadia:** You write a deal landing memo. One or two pages. The bottom line comes first: Verdict, effective price against value, and the two or three points that decide it. Three lines maximum. Then leverage: the eight factors scored.

**Sam:** Then issues and landings. For each disputed term, you show the precedent band, the predicted landing, and whether to hold, trade, or concede. Followed by the kept risk register and the total.

**Nadia:** Then conditions, trades, and triggers. What must be obtained, what to give for it, and when to stop. Followed by the sensitivities. And crucially, every single number must be traced to a clause, a precedent row, or a named study.

**Sam:** So to tie this all together. Which statement best describes how modules fourteen and fifteen fit together?

**Nadia:** Module fourteen predicts what terms are obtainable. Module fifteen values those terms against the deal's actual risks, which tells you which ones to fight for and whether the whole deal is worth doing.

**Sam:** Prediction without valuation tells you where terms will land but not whether that is acceptable. Valuation without prediction tells you what you would like but not what is achievable. Together, they give the advice.

**Sam:** Three takeaways. One, kept risk translates legal limits into expected monetary loss so you can calculate an effective price. Two, value caps and baskets based only on the risks actually in your register, plus one row for unknown unknowns. Three, if a risk threatens the survival of the business, expected value is the wrong tool—make it a walk-away trigger.
