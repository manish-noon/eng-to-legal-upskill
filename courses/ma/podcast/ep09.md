# Episode 9: Limits on claims: de minimis, basket, cap, time

**Sam:** You are two months away from the warranty time limit. You hold seven hundred thousand dirhams in counting claims. The basket is seven hundred and sixty thousand dirhams, and it is a tipping basket. What does that structure push you to do?

**Nadia:** It pushes you to audit the company aggressively. Finding one more claim of eighty thousand dirhams releases the full seven hundred and eighty thousand. The marginal value of finding that one claim is almost ten times its size.

**Sam:** Which gives me a reason to notify arguable claims in time, even small ones that might add up later. This episode is about limits on claims. We will follow one from its first notice through to the amount the seller has to pay.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Module seven gave you a right to damages. In this module, the seller's lawyers wrap that right in a schedule of limitations. These are contractual filters that remove or shrink your claims.

**Sam:** I want to know why a perfectly good warranty claim might pay nothing. Is it too late, too small, or the wrong kind of claim for the clause I'm reading?

**Nadia:** And the seller has a legitimate case for insisting on that sequence. They sold the business to stop owning its risks. They want to distribute the purchase price to their funds or spend it. Unlimited exposure for years defeats the entire purpose of selling.

**Sam:** From the buyer's side the first question is which kind of claim we have. A title claim, an ordinary business warranty and a specific indemnity may each follow different rules in the same agreement.

**Nadia:** Read the limits that apply to that category. The seller is well placed to stand behind its own title and authority to sell. Buyers will often ask for those claims to have separate protection.

**Sam:** Meaning you either own the shares or you do not. There is no noisy unknown there.

**Nadia:** So you may see fundamental warranties outside the ordinary basket and subject to a separate cap equal to the price. Business warranties about accounts, contracts and employees commonly face tighter limits. Check the actual exceptions in this SPA.

**Sam:** Stage two is time. The survival period. Without a clause, English law gives you six years to sue on an ordinary contract, or twelve if it is signed as a deed. But the SPA replaces that with a much shorter limit.

**Nadia:** Usually twelve to twenty-four months for business warranties. The duration logic is tied to the audit cycle. Hidden problems surface when the buyer closes a financial year and has it audited. One full audit cycle under the buyer's ownership takes roughly twelve to eighteen months from closing.

**Sam:** So buyers ask for two cycles, which is twenty-four months, and sellers offer one cycle, which is twelve to eighteen. But there are two clocks in the notice clause, and I have seen buyers fail because they only tracked the first one.

**Nadia:** Let us test that. Closing was on January first of year one. The SPA says the buyer must notify a claim within eighteen months, and then issue and serve proceedings within six months of that notice.

**Sam:** Okay. I send a compliant notice of a two million dirham warranty claim on June first of year two. That is month seventeen.

**Nadia:** That notice is perfectly in time. The first clock is satisfied. Now settlement talks begin.

**Sam:** The talks drag on. I finally issue proceedings on February first of year three, which is month twenty-five. The statutory limitation period has years left to run. What is the result?

**Nadia:** The claim is lost. The notice was in time at month seventeen, but the second clock expired six months later, at month twenty-three. Proceedings at month twenty-five are too late. The claim is deemed withdrawn.

**Sam:** The contract can require action well before the statutory deadline. If settlement talks are continuing, check whether you need to start and serve proceedings or obtain an agreed extension in writing before time expires.

**Nadia:** Exactly. English courts apply notice clauses strictly. A late notice, or one without the required detail and good-faith estimate of the amount, loses the claim. Also, a quick warning on jurisdiction: if onshore UAE law governs, short contractual notification periods may not operate as drafted.

**Sam:** Which is why parties choose English, DIFC, or ADGM law. Alright, stage three is the de minimis, or mini-basket. It excludes any single claim below a set amount. Stage four is the basket, an aggregate hurdle.

**Nadia:** Before we run the math, why does a seller insist on both? The basket already blocks small totals.

**Sam:** Because they filter different things. Without a de minimis, I could fill the basket with hundreds of trivial items. A late filing fee here, a miscounted pallet there. Each one costs more to investigate than it is worth, but together they tip the basket.

**Nadia:** Correct. The de minimis removes noise at the per-claim level. Excluded claims vanish entirely. They are not paid, and they do not count towards the basket. But that creates a risk for the buyer when a single defect causes many small losses.

**Sam:** Which brings us to aggregation. Let us run a scenario. Price is fifty million dirhams. The de minimis is zero point two percent of the price. The tipping basket is zero point eight percent. The general cap is twenty-five percent.

**Nadia:** So the de minimis is one hundred thousand dirhams, and the basket is four hundred thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** Right. We have timely claims. Thirty customer refunds of eight thousand dirhams each, all caused by the exact same product defect. Plus one unrelated claim of ninety-five thousand dirhams, and another unrelated claim of one hundred and eighty thousand dirhams. How much does the seller pay?

**Nadia:** It depends entirely on two words in the drafting: related claims. If the SPA says claims arising from the same or related facts are treated as one claim, the thirty refunds aggregate to two hundred and forty thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** Which clears the one hundred thousand dirham de minimis. So they count.

**Nadia:** Yes. The ninety-five thousand dirham claim is below the de minimis, so it is excluded. The one hundred and eighty thousand dirham claim counts. Your counting total is four hundred and twenty thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** The basket is four hundred thousand. We exceeded it, and it is a tipping basket, so the seller pays the whole four hundred and twenty thousand dirhams, back to the first dirham.

**Nadia:** But without that aggregation wording, each eight thousand dirham refund faces the de minimis alone and vanishes. Your counting total would only be one hundred and eighty thousand dirhams. You would miss the basket entirely and recover nothing.

**Sam:** That is a massive swing from a minor drafting point. It shows how the de minimis can accidentally, or intentionally, erase real systematic problems if they manifest as small identical instances.

**Nadia:** Let us talk about the basket types. Tipping versus deductible. A tipping basket, or first dollar basket, pays the whole total once the threshold is crossed. A deductible basket, or excess only, pays only the amount above the basket.

**Sam:** The rationale is that the purchase price already assumes a normal level of imperfection. The deductible treats that basket amount as a permanent loss to the buyer, like an insurance excess.

**Nadia:** While the tipping type treats the basket as a trigger for serious trouble. As you pointed out at the start, the tipping basket has a cliff effect. Sellers point to that perverse incentive when they ask for a deductible.

**Sam:** If I am negotiating this, how do I price the concession of moving from a tipping basket to a deductible?

**Nadia:** The maximum cost of conceding a deductible is exactly the basket amount. If the basket is one point two million dirhams, moving from tipping to deductible costs you at most one point two million. You can price that concession in a trade.

**Sam:** What does the market data say about where this lands?

**Nadia:** The CMS twenty-twenty-six study of European deals from twenty-twenty-five shows baskets present in sixty-six percent of deals. In Europe, eighty-seven percent of those are tipping baskets. But the US is split. The US figure reported alongside the CMS data shows forty-nine percent of US baskets are deductibles.

**Sam:** Why the difference?

**Nadia:** One hypothesis lawyers offer is that US agreements more often scrape materiality, leaving the basket as the only small-claims filter, so sellers insist it be a true deductible. But treat that as a hypothesis. Just know practice differs.

**Sam:** Stage five is caps. This is the term with the largest money effect. With a cap of twenty percent, the seller is certain to keep eighty percent of the price, whatever is found.

**Nadia:** But you must check the base of every percentage. Let us say a draft SPA sets the general cap at twenty percent of the Enterprise Value. Enterprise Value is one hundred million dirhams, but the purchase price for the shares is seventy-six million.

**Sam:** Okay. My precedent table records caps as a percentage of purchase price. What number do I enter for this deal?

**Nadia:** The cap in money is twenty percent of one hundred million, which is twenty million dirhams. As a share of the purchase price, twenty million divided by seventy-six million is twenty-six point three percent.

**Sam:** If I entered twenty percent into my precedent tracker, I would understate the cap by a quarter and skew every comparison I make. Normalising the base is critical.

**Nadia:** Exactly. And remember that caps are cumulative. Every amount paid uses up room under the cap for later claims. Let us run a cumulative cap scenario.

**Sam:** Price is two hundred million dirhams. De minimis is zero point one percent. Deductible basket is zero point five percent. General cap is ten percent.

**Nadia:** So the general cap is twenty million dirhams.

**Sam:** In year one, the seller paid six million dirhams on a business warranty claim, after the deductible had been fully absorbed. Now, a second business warranty claim of eighteen million dirhams is notified in time. How much does the seller pay on it?

**Nadia:** The cap is twenty million and it is cumulative. Six million is already used, leaving fourteen million in headroom. The new claim passes the de minimis of two hundred thousand. The deductible was used up by the first claim, so nothing more is deducted.

**Sam:** The new claim is eighteen million, which exceeds the remaining fourteen million. So the seller pays fourteen million dirhams, and the buyer bears the four million dirham loss. When a cap is binding, the basket terms are irrelevant.

**Nadia:** Correct. Now let us test your categorization at stage one. Price is ninety million dirhams. Business warranties have a de minimis of zero point one percent, a tipping basket of one percent, and a general cap of twenty percent.

**Sam:** Fundamental warranties have no de minimis or basket, and a cap of one hundred percent of the price. The overall cap is the price. Standard.

**Nadia:** You have timely claims. A business warranty claim of seventy thousand dirhams, another of five hundred thousand, another of twenty-two million. And a title claim of nine million dirhams. How much does the seller pay in total?

**Sam:** Categorize them first. For the business claims, the de minimis is ninety thousand, so the seventy thousand claim is excluded. The counting total is twenty-two point five million dirhams. That is way above the nine hundred thousand dirham basket.

**Nadia:** It is a tipping basket, so the whole twenty-two point five million passes through. But then it hits the general cap.

**Sam:** Twenty percent of ninety million is eighteen million. So the business claims max out at eighteen million dirhams. Now the title claim. It is fundamental, so no thresholds. It has its own cap of ninety million. It pays the nine million in full.

**Nadia:** Total recovery is eighteen million plus nine million, which is twenty-seven million dirhams. The common error is to put the title claim under the general cap and answer eighteen million. Always categorize first.

**Sam:** Suppose our main exposure is one large loss. A single production line generates much of the target's revenue. A covered breach affecting it could cost tens of millions. The seller offers us a choice of basket and cap.

**Nadia:** Package one or package two.

**Sam:** Package one is a tipping basket of zero point five percent and a cap of ten percent. Package two is a deductible basket of one percent and a cap of thirty percent. Price is one hundred million dirhams. Which fits my risk?

**Nadia:** You take package two. Your exposure is one large loss. For a forty million dirham failure, package one pays ten million. Package two pays thirty million. The deductible costs you at most one million dirhams. Conceding the basket to win the cap is the cheap trade when the risk is fat-tailed.

**Sam:** If the production line fails and the loss is large enough to exhaust the cap, could I simply call it fraud?

**Nadia:** The size of the loss doesn't establish fraud. You'd need evidence of the elements of fraud under the applicable law, including who acted dishonestly. Read whose fraud the exception covers and what limits it disapplies.

**Sam:** Why is fraud uncapped even though the seller negotiated a cap in good faith?

**Nadia:** Limitations price the risk of honest error. Both sides accept the seller may be wrong without knowing it. But if caps applied to fraud, a seller could profit from lying by any amount above the cap. Removing it restores the incentive to disclose.

**Sam:** But whose fraud counts? The limitations schedule says: 'Nothing in this Schedule limits the Seller's liability for fraud by the Seller.' After closing, I find the target's finance manager falsified stock records.

**Nadia:** Who is the seller?

**Sam:** A holding company. They knew nothing. The loss is thirty million dirhams, and the general cap is fifteen million. What do I recover?

**Nadia:** Fifteen million dirhams. The accounts warranty is breached, and warranties are strict, so you have a valid claim. But the carve-out is for fraud 'by the Seller'. An innocent holding company is not fraudulent because someone inside the target was.

**Sam:** So the cap applies. That means buyers need to negotiate whose fraud counts, expanding it to include fraud by the seller, its directors, and named target managers.

**Nadia:** And sellers will resist, because they cannot monitor everyone. Another bypass is a specific indemnity. If the SPA contains a specific indemnity for a named pending lawsuit, it is usually drafted outside the limitations schedule with its own cap.

**Sam:** Say that indemnity has a cap of five million dirhams. The lawsuit settles for sixty thousand dirhams. The warranty de minimis is seventy-six thousand dirhams. Can I recover the sixty thousand?

**Nadia:** Yes. The indemnity never enters the threshold stages. Thresholds exist to filter unknown noise. This item was known, named, and priced as a seller risk, so there is nothing to filter.

**Sam:** Before we finish, we need to talk about insurance. If we buy W and I insurance, or RWI in the US, the buyer claims for unknown warranty breaches from an insurer instead of the seller.

**Nadia:** Which changes the cap entirely. The seller's general cap collapses to a nominal amount, often one dirham. The limits that matter move into the policy. Its excess plays the role of the deductible basket, and its limit is the cap.

**Sam:** But what happens if the insurer says no? The SPA caps the seller's liability for business warranties at one dirham. A breach causes a five million dirham loss. The insurer declines it because it falls under a policy exclusion. What do I recover from the seller?

**Nadia:** You recover one dirham. The cap is a term of the SPA and does not depend on the insurer's decision. The risk of policy exclusions sits entirely with the buyer, unless the SPA specifically says the seller backs them.

**Sam:** Which sellers rarely accept. So before signing, I need to read the exclusions in the policy as carefully as I read the cap in the SPA.

**Nadia:** Exactly. We also skipped over reducers at stage six, but the logic is simple: they prevent you from being paid for something you haven't really lost. No double recovery, a duty to mitigate, and no claims for things already provided for in the completion accounts.

**Sam:** First, identify the claim. Read which deadlines, thresholds and caps apply to ordinary business warranties. Then check whether the agreement makes separate rules for title claims and specific indemnities.

**Nadia:** Second, watch the wording on thresholds. A de minimis without aggregation wording can erase systematic problems made of small identical losses. And a tipping basket creates a cliff effect that forces buyers to audit aggressively.

**Sam:** Third, value the cap against the losses you could actually suffer. A higher cap can matter greatly for a single large covered loss. If there is insurance, check which part of that loss the policy would pay before agreeing to reduce the seller's liability.

**Nadia:** Next time we'll look at specific indemnities, warranty insurance, and who actually has money available when a claim falls due.
