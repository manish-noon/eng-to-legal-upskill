# Episode 5: Price: from enterprise value to the cheque

**Sam:** You sign a letter of intent to sell your company for two hundred million dirhams. Six months later, you sit at the closing table, and the wire hits your bank account. It is one hundred and thirty-seven point seven million dirhams.

**Nadia:** And you want to know who stole your sixty-two million.

**Sam:** Exactly. I did not renegotiate the headline price. The business did not tank. So where did it go?

**Nadia:** It was eaten by the definitions. Welcome to episode five, Price: from enterprise value to the cheque.

**Sam:** Let us start with that headline number. If comparable businesses sell for eight times EBITDA, which is earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and we earn twenty-five million dirhams, the buyer offers an enterprise value of two hundred million. But that is just a parameter in a valuation formula.

**Nadia:** Correct. A buyer values the business. A seller is paid for the shares. Enterprise value is the value of the operating business regardless of how it is financed. Equity value is what the shares are worth: the business, minus what the company owes its lenders, plus the spare cash it holds.

**Sam:** So the house analogy holds up. A house worth two million with a one point five million mortgage has an enterprise value of two million and an equity value of half a million. Whoever buys the company's shares inherits the mortgage.

**Nadia:** The analogy is exact. And why a lawyer cares about a valuation formula is that every input is a defined term in the Sales and Purchase Agreement, the SPA. Every definition is price. The multiple turns every recurring item into a lever.

**Sam:** Because if I persuade the buyer that a two million dirham cost was a one-off and should be added back to EBITDA, the price rises by eight times two, which is sixteen million.

**Nadia:** Let us test that. A buyer has agreed eight times EBITDA. In diligence, they find two things. First, the founder pays himself one million dirhams a year below the market salary for his role. Second, the company owes one million dirhams in unpaid tax from last year. Sam, what is the combined effect on a rational buyer's price?

**Sam:** They are two totally different failure modes. The underpaid salary is a recurring state. A replacement manager costs one million more every year, so the true EBITDA is one million lower. You multiply that by eight. The price falls by eight million dirhams.

**Nadia:** And the tax?

**Sam:** The tax is a one-off balance sheet debt. It reduces the price exactly once, by one million. So the combined effect is nine million dirhams lower. The mistake would be treating both as recurring, which would lower the price by sixteen million, or both as one-offs, which would only cost two million.

**Nadia:** Precisely. First ask of any finding: is it a recurring earnings item, so you multiply it, or a one-off balance sheet item, so you count it once. The walk from enterprise value to equity value is called the bridge. Equity value equals enterprise value, minus debt, plus cash, plus or minus the working capital adjustment.

**Sam:** Offers are usually made on a cash-free, debt-free basis. The headline price assumes the business is handed over with no debt, no spare cash, and a normal level of working capital.

**Nadia:** Nobody literally strips the company. The price is simply reduced for each unit of debt left in it and increased for each unit of cash. The reason for the convention is that debt and cash levels are the seller's financing choices and can change daily. The buyer prices the operations and lets the bridge handle whatever balance sheet arrives on the day.

**Sam:** Net debt is debt minus cash. Bank loans are obvious. But the real fight is over debt-like items, right? Liabilities that are not borrowings, but that the buyer will have to pay in cash for something that benefited the seller's period of ownership.

**Nadia:** Yes. The buyer's test is: is this a claim on the company's cash that relates to the past and that I did not price into EBITDA? If yes, it should come off the price. Unpaid tax on pre-closing profits is debt-like. Finance leases are economically a loan to buy equipment, so they are debt-like. Deal costs borne by the target are debt-like.

**Sam:** And on the other side, cash. Sellers want every dirham counted. Buyers only want free cash. Trapped cash, like customer deposits or cash securing bank guarantees, is excluded or discounted because the buyer cannot use it.

**Nadia:** Let us look at a regional specific. In the UAE, employees are entitled on leaving to an end-of-service gratuity. Twenty-one days basic pay per year of service for the first five years, and thirty days thereafter, capped at two years pay. Most companies do not fund it, so it is a book accrual with no cash set aside. Buyers treat the accrual as debt-like.

**Sam:** And I assume sellers reply that staff leave gradually, so the liability is paid out over many years as a running operating cost. Expect that argument on every UAE deal.

**Nadia:** Always. Let us say the target has an accrued gratuity liability of six million dirhams, stable all year. The buyer wants it as debt. The seller proposes to treat it as part of working capital, with the working capital target calculated on the same basis. How much better off is the seller under its own proposal?

**Sam:** Six million dirhams better off. If it is debt, the six million is deducted from the price in full. But if it is working capital, it reduces actual working capital by six million, and it reduces the target working capital by the exact same six million. The adjustment, actual minus target, is zero. The liability passes to the buyer for free.

**Nadia:** That is the general rule. Anything inside working capital at its normal level has no price effect. Classification between debt and working capital is therefore a price negotiation disguised as accounting.

**Sam:** Let us define net working capital. Receivables plus inventory minus payables. It is the money tied up in running the business day to day. As an engineer, I view it as the oil in the engine. The buyer paid for an engine that runs, so it expects a normal level of oil to be delivered.

**Nadia:** We call that normal level the peg, or target working capital. If actual net working capital at closing is above the peg, the price goes up by the difference. If below, it goes down. The peg is usually an average of the last twelve months of normalised working capital, stripped of one-offs, because month-end balances swing with the seasons.

**Sam:** Without the peg, a cash-free, debt-free deal can be completely gamed. If the SPA has no working capital adjustment, in the month before closing, the seller could just stop paying suppliers. They build four million dirhams of extra cash and four million of overdue payables. What happens?

**Nadia:** The buyer overpays by four million. They offset inside the company, but not in the price. The price rises four million for the extra cash. The overdue payables are not debt, and there is no peg to catch them, so nothing comes off. The buyer then pays the suppliers four million out of pocket. The cash the buyer received is cash it already paid for.

**Sam:** Which proves the three definitions, debt, cash, and working capital, must be read as one system. Every balance sheet item should fall into exactly one of them.

**Nadia:** Let us look at how the peg is manipulated. A toy retailer's net working capital peaks in October because stock is built for the holidays. It is lowest in January, ten million dirhams below the twelve-month average. Closing is the thirty-first of January. The seller proposes a peg equal to last January's level, arguing that is what working capital normally is at closing. Who benefits?

**Sam:** The seller, by about ten million dirhams. In January, the stock has been sold and turned into cash, and cash is paid for one for one. With an average peg, the ten million of extra cash is matched by a ten million working capital shortfall, making the price neutral.

**Nadia:** Exactly.

**Sam:** But with a January peg, there is no shortfall. The seller is paid for the cash, and the buyer must find ten million out of pocket to rebuild stock next autumn. The enterprise value priced a business that carries its average working capital across the whole cycle.

**Nadia:** Which is why lawyers ask the accountants to model the whole bridge, cash and working capital together, on the expected closing date before agreeing any peg. Now, the bridge needs balance sheet figures at a date. There are two ways to choose it: completion accounts or a locked box.

**Sam:** Completion accounts, which the US market calls a purchase price adjustment. The price is calculated from a balance sheet drawn up as at closing. Since that cannot exist on the day, the buyer pays an estimate at closing, and there is a true-up later.

**Nadia:** Yes. Afterwards, one side prepares the accounts within sixty to ninety days. The other side reviews and objects. The SPA fixes the accounting rules in a hierarchy. SRS Acquiom 2026 reports that thirty-nine percent of working capital adjustments use an agreed worksheet methodology, not a generic reference to accounting standards. Parties are closing the room for argument.

**Sam:** Let us run the math on a completion accounts true-up. At closing, the buyer paid on the seller's estimates: net debt of fifty million dirhams, and working capital equal to the peg of thirty-five million. The final completion accounts show net debt of fifty-three point two million, and working capital of thirty-six point five million. Who pays whom?

**Nadia:** Net debt is three point two million higher than estimated, so the price falls by three point two. Working capital is one point five million above the peg, where the estimate assumed zero excess. Price rises by one point five. Net result: three point two minus one point five is one point seven million, payable by the seller to the buyer. Each component is computed separately and then netted.

**Sam:** And the alternative is a locked box. The price is fixed at signing from a historical balance sheet at a date before signing. There is no true-up. Economic risk and reward pass to the buyer from that locked box date.

**Nadia:** CMS 2026 reports a purchase price adjustment in forty-eight percent of European deals against ninety-two percent in the US. Of the European deals without one, fifty-four percent used a locked box. But the danger is obvious: the seller still controls a company whose value now belongs to the buyer.

**Sam:** So the seller promises there will be no leakage. No value moving from the target to the seller or its associates. Dividends, management fees, bonuses tied to the sale. If there is leakage, it is repaid one for one, usually under a claim period of six to twelve months after closing.

**Nadia:** And because the buyer gets the profits earned since the locked box date, the seller asks to be paid for them through a ticker. A daily amount, or interest on the price, from the locked box date to closing. Let us run a scenario. Equity price is two hundred million dirhams. The ticker is five percent a year on the equity price, accruing daily on a three-hundred-and-sixty-five-day basis, for the one hundred and forty-six days from the locked box date to closing.

**Sam:** Okay, let me do the ticker first. Two hundred million times five percent is ten million for a full year. We prorate that for one hundred and forty-six days out of three hundred and sixty-five. That is exactly forty percent of the year. Forty percent of ten million is four million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Before closing, the buyer learns the target paid the seller a dividend of three million, paid a zero point six million management fee to the seller's parent, and paid the seller's nominee director his normal salary of zero point four million. Only ordinary-course salaries are permitted leakage. What does the buyer pay at closing?

**Sam:** The dividend of three million and the management fee of zero point six million are leakage. That is three point six million. The salary is permitted leakage, so we ignore it. Payment is the two hundred million base, plus the four million ticker, minus the three point six million leakage. Two hundred point four million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Correct. But what if, between a locked box date of thirty-first December and a closing on thirtieth June, the target loses a major contract and makes a trading loss of five million dirhams? The seller extracted nothing. What can the buyer recover under the leakage clause?

**Sam:** Nothing. Leakage is value transferred to the seller or its associates. A trading loss is value lost to the outside world. In a locked box, the buyer took trading risk and reward from the locked box date. And worse, the buyer still pays the ticker.

**Nadia:** That is the hidden cost of a locked box for buyers: you own the downside before you own the company. Private equity sellers love the locked box because the fund can distribute the proceeds to its investors on day one with no risk of paying some back. But buyers should resist when the target is being carved out of a larger group and has no standalone accounts, when working capital is volatile, or when the gap to closing is long.

**Sam:** Because a locked box is only as good as the accounts it locks. Let us move to earn-outs. Bridging a valuation gap by making part of the price depend on the target's performance after closing.

**Nadia:** CMS 2026 found earn-outs in a record twenty-seven percent of European deals, with an EBIT or EBITDA metric in fifty-five percent of them. ABA 2025 shows eighteen percent in the US, and SRS Acquiom 2026 shows twenty-four percent excluding life sciences. The structural problem is that after closing, the buyer controls the thing being measured.

**Sam:** The buyer decides costs, pricing, staffing, and what gets booked where. Let us test the drafting. The buyer pays three times the amount by which Year One EBITDA exceeds twenty million dirhams, capped at fifteen million. The SPA says earn-out EBITDA is calculated before any management charges levied by the buyer's group. The buyer's accounts show Year One EBITDA of twenty-two million after a two million group management charge. What is the payment?

**Nadia:** You add back the charge. Twenty-two million plus two million is twenty-four million. The excess over twenty is four million. The payment is three times four million, which is twelve million dirhams, safely below the fifteen million cap. Without that add-back, the payment would be three times two million, which is six million. One line in the definition is worth six million dirhams.

**Sam:** This is why buyers hate EBITDA earn-outs just as much as sellers do. It constrains integration. To keep the metric measurable, the buyer must keep the target separate, forgoing the synergies it paid for.

**Nadia:** If a founder sells her software company with an earn-out, and six months later the buyer moves the target's sales team into its own group sales force, bundling the products at a discount, standalone EBITDA can no longer be measured. The metric itself is destroyed.

**Sam:** If the SPA lets the buyer run the business in its sole discretion, an audit right does not help. You are verifying a number that no longer means anything. The best protection for the seller is acceleration of the maximum earn-out if the business ceases to be run as a separate unit with separate accounts.

**Nadia:** Because it converts an unmeasurable contingent price into a fixed one and makes the buyer price the integration decision. Finally, let us talk about how the money is held. Escrow versus retention.

**Sam:** A seller is offered fifteen million dirhams paid into escrow with a bank for eighteen months, or retained by the buyer, a newly formed company funded with bank debt, for eighteen months. Headline terms are identical. But under the retention, the seller is just an unsecured creditor of a leveraged company that also decides whether to pay.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Under escrow, the money sits outside both parties and is released under agreed instructions. Identical headline terms, totally different risk. Per DealLawyers dot com's summary of the SRS Acquiom 2026 study, eighty-eight percent of 2025 deals in that sample had an escrow or holdback, with a median of ten point zero percent of deal value where there was no warranty insurance, and two point eight percent where there was.

**Sam:** Time to wrap up. Point one: treat the bridge definitions as a closed system. Every balance sheet item must sit in exactly one of debt, cash, or working capital. If it is in two, the seller pays twice. If it is in none, it is given away free.

**Nadia:** Point two: the working capital peg must match the cash-free, debt-free mechanic. If you agree to a seasonal low point for the peg, you will hand the buyer cash they did not pay for.

**Sam:** Point three: a locked box shifts trading risk to the buyer before closing. If the business is volatile or lacks standalone audited accounts, stick to completion accounts.

**Nadia:** Next time, we look at the product that drops that escrow requirement from ten percent to under three: warranty insurance.
