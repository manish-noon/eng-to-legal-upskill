# Episode 15: Predicting the landing: precedent plus leverage

**Sam:** If a founder asks three different M&A partners what a standard liability cap is, they get three different numbers, and every partner claims their number is 'market'.

**Nadia:** Because 'market' is just a lawyer's word for the precedent they personally remember and consider comparable. It is treated as a dark art. But you can actually calculate it. I am Nadia.

**Sam:** And I am Sam. This is Calling the Deal. Episode 15: Predicting the landing: precedent plus leverage.

**Nadia:** Every negotiated term in an SPA starts with two opening positions. That is what each side first asks for. It ends at a landing, which is what is finally signed. A senior M&A lawyer can usually tell you the landing on day one. That judgment rests on two inputs only.

**Sam:** Precedent and leverage. Precedent is where this term landed in comparable deals. That gives you the range. Leverage is which side has more power in this specific deal. That tells you where you sit inside the range.

**Nadia:** Exactly. A lawyer does this from memory of perhaps a hundred deals. You have no such memory, so you do it explicitly, from a table. The method is the same, but doing it explicitly has one advantage. Every step can be checked and challenged.

**Sam:** Before we calculate anything, we have to isolate the variables. We can't compare raw dollar amounts because deals differ in size. We have to define every term in a unit that travels.

**Nadia:** Yes. A general cap is measured as a percent of the purchase price. A higher number favors the buyer. A de minimis is also a percent of the price, but a higher number favors the seller. A time limit on survival is measured in months, favoring the buyer.

**Sam:** Escrow or holdback is measured in two dimensions: percent of price and months held. Both favor the buyer. Baskets are a percent of price, plus a category: tipping or deductible.

**Nadia:** Always note the direction. A leverage score that pushes the cap up must push the basket down.

**Sam:** Right, so step one is defining the unit. Step two is choosing comparables to read off the precedent band. What filters do we use to decide if a past deal is comparable?

**Nadia:** You filter in the order of explanatory power in your data. The first filter is warranty insurance. Yes or no. This splits the population in two. In our twenty-two-deal set, with insurance, the seller's cap is near zero. The median is about zero-point-three percent of price. Several deals have warranties that do not survive closing at all.

**Sam:** And without insurance?

**Nadia:** Without it, the cap sits around ten to seventeen percent.

**Sam:** So if I am predicting the cap for an uninsured founder sale at forty million dollars, and my table has nine uninsured deals and eight insured deals, a colleague might suggest using all seventeen for a bigger sample. But that is a trap.

**Nadia:** It is a trap. What is your reply to that colleague?

**Sam:** I refuse. Insurance changes the state machine that sets the cap. The seller is no longer the source of recovery. So those eight insured deals are a completely different population. Including them would drag the median toward a value that no uninsured deal actually shows.

**Nadia:** Correct. Never average across the insured and uninsured groups. The average of zero-point-three percent and fifteen percent describes no deal that exists. Sample size only helps when the extra deals come from the same process.

**Sam:** What are the other filters, after insurance?

**Nadia:** Seller type. Private equity sellers and carve-out sellers behave differently from founders. Then deal size. Larger deals tend to have lower caps as a percentage, because the absolute amounts get large. CMS 2026 reports forty-three percent of large deals have caps below ten percent.

**Sam:** That makes sense. Ten percent of a billion dollars is a hundred million. That is plenty of absolute protection. And the final filter?

**Nadia:** Legal culture. English-style or US-style paper. This matters most for categories such as basket type. Once you apply these filters, you take your comparable deals and find the twenty-fifth percentile, the median, and the seventy-fifth percentile. That is your precedent band.

**Sam:** So half of comparable deals landed inside that band. And I should always state 'n', the number of deals behind it.

**Nadia:** Always. A band from three deals is just three anecdotes. If your 'n' is below four, relax the least important filter, which is usually size, and state that you did.

**Sam:** Let me test a conflict. My filtered set gives an uninsured cap band of ten, fifteen, and seventeen percent. But the CMS 2026 study says fifty-eight percent of European deals have caps below fifty percent of price. Do these conflict?

**Nadia:** No. 'Below fifty percent' includes everything from one percent to forty-nine percent, so your band sits comfortably inside it. Published studies report coarse buckets across all deal types. Use them to check that your band is not absurd, or to quote a named source in a negotiation. But use your own table for the point estimate.

**Sam:** Okay, so the precedent band gives us the playing field. Step three is scoring the leverage to find out where we land on that field. Leverage comes down to one thing: BATNA. Best alternative to a negotiated agreement.

**Nadia:** Exactly. How much worse off is each side if this deal does not happen. The side with the better alternative has the leverage. We score eight observable factors from the buyer's side. Plus-one if the fact favors the buyer, minus-one if it favors the seller, and zero if neutral.

**Sam:** Let's run through them. Factor one: Competition. A bilateral deal is plus-one. An auction with several bidders is minus-one, because the seller compares markups and picks the cleanest.

**Nadia:** Factor two: Seller's need. Distress, a deadline, or a fund at end of life is plus-one. A seller who can just keep the business is minus-one. Time pressure is the strongest single source of leverage.

**Sam:** Factor three: Warranty insurance. None is plus-one. Policy in place is minus-one. Factor four: Diligence findings. Red flags justify protection, so that's plus-one. Clean diligence is minus-one.

**Nadia:** Factor five: Seller continuity. Sellers leaving with the cash is plus-one. Sellers rolling over or staying as managers is minus-one. A buyer rarely sues the people running its new business, so it accepts less.

**Sam:** Factor six: Buyer type. Financial buyer with alternatives is plus-one. Strategic buyer that needs this asset is minus-one. Factor seven: Market cycle. Few buyers and cheap targets is plus-one. Capital chasing deals is minus-one. The cycle shifts everyone's alternatives at once.

**Nadia:** And factor eight: Regulatory and timing risk. A quick, clean close is plus-one. Approvals needed is minus-one, because a seller facing delay risk demands certainty elsewhere.

**Sam:** We add those eight scores, and then we clip the total to the range of minus-two to plus-two. Why do we clip it?

**Nadia:** Because beyond a point, more leverage does not move a term further. The weaker side simply walks away, or the term hits the edge of what anyone will sign.

**Sam:** It's a boundary condition. A saturation curve. The system maxes out.

**Nadia:** If you like. Let's test your scoring. A private equity fund in its final year is auctioning a business to five bidders. Warranty insurance is arranged. Management keep fifteen percent and stay. Diligence is clean. The buyer is a trade competitor that needs this asset to stay in the market. Treat market cycle and regulatory risk as neutral. What is the raw leverage score?

**Sam:** Okay. Auction is minus-one. Fund deadline is plus-one. Insurance is minus-one. Clean diligence, minus-one. Continuity, minus-one, because management stays. Strategic buyer that needs it, minus-one. That is a raw score of minus-four. Clipped to minus-two.

**Nadia:** Correct. The tempting error there is to score the fund's deadline as decisive. It is real, but an auction with five bidders means the deadline will be met by someone else if you are difficult.

**Sam:** Are all these factors weighted equally?

**Nadia:** No, but equal weights are a deliberate simplification. With eight factors and a clipped total, the answer is not sensitive to the weights unless one factor entirely dominates.

**Sam:** Like a seller who truly does not need to sell.

**Nadia:** Exactly. When a single fact outweighs the arithmetic of the others, you state it as a dominant factor and override the model.

**Sam:** Alright, step four: compute the landing. The formula starts at the median. We move toward the side that has leverage, in proportion to the clipped score. The formula is: landing equals median, plus the absolute score divided by two, multiplied by the difference between the edge of the band on the stronger side and the median.

**Nadia:** Let's work an example aloud. Cap band for your comparables: twenty-fifth percentile is ten percent, median is fifteen percent, seventy-fifth percentile is twenty percent. Your raw leverage score is minus-three. The buyer opened at one hundred percent and the seller opened at twelve percent. What cap does the model predict, in percent of price?

**Sam:** First, clip minus-three to minus-two. The score is negative, so the seller is stronger. For a cap, a lower number favors the seller, so we move toward the twenty-fifth percentile, which is ten percent. The math is fifteen, plus two divided by two, times the difference between ten and fifteen. That gives us ten percent.

**Nadia:** But does it land at ten percent?

**Sam:** No, because the seller itself offered twelve percent. In control theory, we call that a clamping function. The system cannot output a value outside its input constraints. We clamp the prediction to twelve percent.

**Nadia:** In law, we just say no seller lands below its own opening offer. The lesson is to read the opening positions before you quote the band. An opening inside the band tells you the other side knows the market and has left itself little room.

**Sam:** Let's do a basket band. Uninsured, percent of price. Twenty-fifth percentile is zero-point-five. Median is zero-point-seven-five. Seventy-fifth percentile is one-point-one-six. Leverage score is plus-one for the buyer. Buyer opened at zero-point-two-five percent, seller at two percent. What is the predicted basket?

**Nadia:** A lower basket favors the buyer, so a positive score moves toward the twenty-fifth percentile. Landing equals zero-point-seven-five, plus one over two, times the difference between zero-point-five and zero-point-seven-five. That is zero-point-six-two-five percent. It lies between the openings, so no clamp.

**Sam:** The common slip there is to move toward the seventy-fifth percentile just because the score is plus-one, forgetting that a higher basket hurts the buyer. Directionality matters.

**Nadia:** Now step five: categories, and terms that do not move. Some terms are not numbers. You predict them by frequency in the comparables. Basket type, for example. Buyers want tipping, sellers want deductible. Tipping recovers the whole amount once the threshold is passed.

**Sam:** And that follows legal culture. CMS 2026 says tipping is eighty-seven percent of European baskets, but deductible is forty-nine percent in the US compared to thirteen percent in Europe.

**Nadia:** Sandbagging is another. Does diligence knowledge kill a claim? ABA 2025 says sixty-eight percent are silent, twenty-eight percent pro-sandbagging, and four percent anti. In our twenty-two-deal set, it is pro in most founder deals, and anti only in two carve-outs.

**Sam:** What about fundamental warranties and fraud? The buyer wants a one hundred percent cap for fundamental warranties, and an uncapped liability for fraud.

**Nadia:** Those do not move. Fundamental warranties go to whether the buyer got what it paid for, so they cap at one hundred percent. And no one can contract out of their own fraud, so it is uncapped. Recognizing terms that do not move saves negotiating capital. If a buyer opens by demanding a one hundred percent cap for title, they just demanded what the seller's first draft already gave.

**Sam:** I noticed in the precedent set that the cap equals the escrow in several deals. Why is that pairing stable? It feels like an equilibrium state.

**Nadia:** It is, because it gives each side what it most wants. The seller knows its worst case exactly and can spend the rest of the money. The buyer knows the money is there and never has to sue or chase anyone. Once both see this, the argument is only about one number. When you predict a cap for founders who are leaving, predict the escrow at the same time. They often turn out to be the same number.

**Sam:** Step six is drivers. A prediction without its drivers is a guess. We have to name the facts that would shift the landing. Let's say we predicted a sixteen percent cap and a five percent escrow for an uninsured founder sale. The founders now offer to take thirty percent of the price in buyer shares locked for two years. What happens?

**Nadia:** The buyer can accept a lower cash escrow, because locked shares give it something to claim against. And the founders will argue for a lower cap because they now share the downside.

**Sam:** So consideration structure and risk terms are one system. The locked shares are a form of security, and they also align the founders with the buyer, which moves the continuity factor toward minus-one.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Let's look at another driver. A partner tells you that on an insured deal, the cap discussion is over in five minutes. Why?

**Sam:** Because insurance removes the buyer's need, which is what the cap exists to serve. Once a policy pays for unknown warranty breaches, the seller has a principled reason to give a nominal cap, and the buyer has little reason to resist.

**Nadia:** Right. The two insured deals in our set with high caps of twenty-five and thirty-seven-point-five percent had special facts. Insurers do not forbid seller caps, and insured buyers must still do thorough diligence, or the policy excludes the area.

**Sam:** What happens when the model fails? Say I filter down to uninsured carve-out sellers and I only have two deals, with caps of ten percent and thirty-five percent.

**Nadia:** You do not report a median of twenty-two-point-five percent. Two deals do not make a band, and their midpoint is an artefact. You report that 'n' equals two is too thin. You widen the filter to all uninsured deals, which gives you 'n' equals nine and a band of ten, fifteen, and sixteen-point-seven. Report that, and note the two carve-outs as individual data points.

**Sam:** What about geography? The twenty-two deals in our set are US filings. Private UAE deals are not public. If I'm building a prediction model for a deal in Dubai, my dataset is empty.

**Nadia:** Until you have local data, present the band as 'public precedent' and ask a practicing lawyer how local deals differ. That question, asked clause by clause, is the knowledge-capture exercise from the Gulfstream demo.

**Sam:** Let's say I run the model, and it predicts eighteen months for the time limit. The seller's lawyer says: 'CMS shows over twenty-four months in only thirty-two percent of deals, so twelve months is market.' How do I respond?

**Nadia:** You use their own source. You say, 'The same study shows eighteen to twenty-four months in thirty-three percent, and over twenty-four in thirty-two percent. So about two-thirds of deals are at eighteen months or longer. Twelve is the minority position.'

**Sam:** That's beautiful. The other side quoted a true number that does not support its conclusion. Answering with the full distribution wins the point on their own source. Citing my model as authority persuades no one.

**Nadia:** Never cite the model as authority. The model does not produce the legally correct value of each term. It produces a transparent first estimate of where each term will settle, with the evidence and the drivers stated, so an experienced lawyer can correct a specific step.

**Sam:** If they disagree, they have to say 'your comparables are wrong' or 'you under-scored the seller's alternatives'. It moves the argument from a vague appeal to 'market' down to a specific variable.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Three takeaways. One, predict the landing by building a precedent band, scoring the leverage, and moving from the median. Two, filter comparables by insurance and seller type, but never average across those populations. Three, clamp your prediction between the opening positions.

**Sam:** Next time, we look at irrelevant terms, and what happens when the model predicts a cap that protects against nothing that matters.
