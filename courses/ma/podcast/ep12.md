# Episode 12: The clauses that decide disputes, and life after closing

**Sam:** You buy a company. The earn-out promises the seller five times any EBITDA above twenty million dirhams in year one. The day after closing, you move the target's best sales team into your own subsidiary. The target's EBITDA comes in at nineteen million. The seller sues. Who wins?

**Nadia:** Assuming you drafted it right, you do. This is episode twelve of Calling the Deal: the clauses that decide disputes, and life after closing.

**Sam:** That outcome sounds like a massive failure mode for the seller. They were on track, and the buyer just ripped out the engine to avoid the payout.

**Nadia:** It is a failure mode, but a contractual one. If the SPA has an entire agreement clause, no earn-out protection covenants, and expressly gives the buyer full discretion to operate the business, the seller has almost nothing to stand on. Warranty limits protect the seller, not the buyer. Restrictive covenants bind the seller. The seller's protection has to be written into the earn-out: ring-fencing the business, no diversion of revenue, an adjustment if the buyer restructures.

**Sam:** What if they sue on general principles? That pulling the sales team was obvious bad faith.

**Nadia:** Some legal systems imply good-faith limits on a discretion, which is a point you ask local counsel about. But relying on an implied duty is a weak position. If you want protection, you draft it. Let us rewind to the immediate post-closing risks. Restrictive covenants. Much of the price of a business is often goodwill.

**Sam:** Goodwill lives in people's heads. Customer relationships, reputation, the workforce. It is the value above the hard assets. If I pay for that, I need a lock on the seller so they do not take my money and open a competing shop next door.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Restrictive covenants are the seller's promises not to do that. Non-compete: the seller will not carry on or invest in a competing business. Non-solicit of customers: they will not approach the target's customers. Non-solicit of employees, or non-poach: they will not hire away key staff. And confidentiality: they will not use or disclose confidential information.

**Sam:** As a buyer, my incentive is to max out the parameters. Ten years, worldwide, binding the seller's entire corporate group.

**Nadia:** And the consequence of asking for too much is that you are left with nothing. The enforceability logic matters more than your wish list. Courts dislike contracts that stop people trading. The common-law starting point is that a restraint is void unless it protects a legitimate interest and goes no further than reasonably necessary.

**Sam:** So we are testing three dials: scope, geography, and duration. What is the benchmark for reasonable?

**Nadia:** The business actually sold, at the time of sale. Courts are more generous to covenants given by a seller of a business, because they were paid for the goodwill and negotiated as an equal, compared to covenants given by an employee. But they are still tested strictly. Let us test one. A buyer acquires a catering company that operates only in Dubai and Abu Dhabi. The SPA non-compete is a single sentence: the seller shall not engage in any food or hospitality business anywhere in the world for ten years.

**Sam:** Two years later, the seller opens a rival caterer in Dubai. That conduct is plainly within a reasonable restraint. It is exactly what the clause should forbid.

**Nadia:** Which is what makes this painful for the buyer. Reasonableness is tested on the clause as drafted, not on the breach that happened. Worldwide, ten years, and all hospitality far exceed the business actually sold. Under a strict common-law approach, where courts strike down rather than rewrite, the buyer is at serious risk of losing.

**Sam:** The court just drops the entire request because it fails validation. A single-sentence covenant gives them nothing to sever.

**Nadia:** Precisely. Some laws would cut it down, but you cannot bank on it. This is why the drafting uses separate, independent sub-clauses. The clause will define the restricted business, the territory, and a duration of, say, twenty-four months. Then it adds a severability device: 'Each undertaking in this clause is a separate undertaking, and if any is held unenforceable the others remain in force.'

**Sam:** So if the twenty-four months is struck down, the non-solicit and confidentiality arrays survive. Where do these dials usually land in a negotiation?

**Nadia:** For a non-compete, the buyer wants three years, wide scope, binding the seller's whole group. The seller wants one year, narrow scope, with carve-outs for existing businesses and small passive shareholdings. It typically lands at one to three years, with scope and territory strictly matched to the target's actual business.

**Sam:** What about a private equity fund? If a fund is selling, they will refuse a non-compete almost regardless of price.

**Nadia:** They have to. A fund's business is owning companies, and its internal documents may stop it restricting future investments. A non-compete binding the seller's group could bar the fund's other portfolio companies and its next acquisition. The buyer normally settles for non-solicits of customers and staff plus confidentiality from the fund, and takes real non-competes from the individual managers.

**Sam:** That makes sense. The managers carry the relationships anyway. Let us move to guarantees. An SPA is only as good as the party who signed it. I have seen holding companies sell a business, distribute the cash the next day, and become an empty shell.

**Nadia:** Which is why you need a seller's guarantee. The seller's parent or ultimate owner guarantees the seller's obligations. Warranty claims, indemnities, restrictive covenants. It is a promise by a third party with real assets to perform or pay if the contracting party does not. The alternatives you already know do the same job: escrow, retention, W and I insurance.

**Sam:** Buyers play the same game. We buy through newly formed vehicles with zero assets. So the seller demands a buyer's guarantee to secure payment of the price, deferred consideration, and earn-outs. But if I am a fund-backed buyer, my fund will not sign a direct guarantee.

**Nadia:** In that case, the fund usually offers an equity commitment letter. It is the fund's written promise to put the money into the buying vehicle. The buyer wants the guarantor to be liable as a primary debtor so the guarantee survives changes to the SPA. The seller wants the guarantor's liability capped at the exact same limits as the seller's. It usually lands there.

**Sam:** Let us talk boilerplate. The standard clauses at the back of the contract that everyone ignores until everything breaks. Entire agreement.

**Nadia:** They decide a large share of real disputes because they answer the procedural questions before anyone reaches the merits. The entire agreement clause states that the written contract is the whole deal. Nothing said or sent before signing is a term. The purpose is certainty.

**Sam:** It is a state reset. Without it, every management presentation and late-night email is a candidate promise. And non-reliance?

**Nadia:** The buyer confirms it has not relied on any statement outside the contract. This is aimed at a different claim. Not 'that was a term', but 'you induced me to sign by a false statement', which is misrepresentation. A misrepresentation claim could otherwise escape the SPA's limits. The two clauses work as a pair.

**Sam:** Let us test them. During a management presentation, the seller's CEO says next year's revenue will be at least ninety million dirhams. It comes in at sixty million. The SPA contains no warranty about forecasts. It has an entire agreement clause, a non-reliance clause, and an exclusive remedy clause. The buyer sues for the shortfall. There is no evidence the CEO disbelieved the forecast.

**Nadia:** The entire agreement and non-reliance clauses win the case for the seller. The statement is outside the contract, so the entire agreement clause stops it being a term. The non-reliance clause blocks the misrepresentation claim.

**Sam:** Does the warranty cap protect the seller here?

**Nadia:** The cap never comes into play because there is no warranty to breach. And the exclusive remedy clause limits the buyer to damages under the SPA, subject to its limits. No rescission, which means unwinding the deal, and no claims in tort.

**Sam:** What if the CEO knew the forecast was entirely fabricated?

**Nadia:** Then you have fraud. Neither clause protects against fraud. The law does not allow a party to exclude liability for its own fraud. But the practical lesson for a buyer is absolute: if a statement matters to your price, get it into the warranties.

**Sam:** Further assurance. Each party will sign whatever further documents are needed to give effect to the deal. If six months after closing I discover a key software license is still held by the seller's founder personally, the IP warranty is breached. But the loss is hard to quantify and falls under the basket. I do not want damages, I want the license.

**Nadia:** Then you use the further assurance clause. It obliges the seller to procure the transfer of the license so that the buyer gets what was sold. It rescues transfers that turn out incomplete. But it cannot be used to add new commercial obligations.

**Sam:** Assignment. Transferring the right to sue to someone else. The default prohibits assignment without consent. The buyer needs two exceptions. First, the lender will want the warranty rights assigned as security. Sellers accept that. Second, the buyer might later sell the target and want to pass the warranties to the next owner.

**Nadia:** Sellers strongly resist that second exception. And they always add that an assignee can recover no more than the original buyer could have. There is a trap here. Warranties belong to the buyer as a contracting party. They are not attached to the target's shares.

**Sam:** Let us map the trap. Buyer A buys Target from Seller. The SPA prohibits assignment without consent, except to lenders. Two years later, Buyer A sells Target to Buyer B. There is no assignment of the first SPA. Buyer B discovers a breach of the Seller's tax warranties, still within the time limit. Who can sue the original Seller?

**Nadia:** Only Buyer A, or its lender if the security assignment was enforced. Buyer B has no rights under a contract it is not party to, and that was not assigned to it. This is the rule of privity. Target cannot sue either, because it was never a party to the SPA. Buyer B's only remedy is against Buyer A, under the warranties in the second SPA.

**Sam:** Notice mechanics. I look at notice clauses as strict API gateways. If the payload does not match the schema exactly, it drops. The limitation regime has two clocks: a deadline to notify a claim, and usually a second deadline to issue and serve court or arbitration proceedings. Fail either, and the claim is withdrawn.

**Nadia:** Courts enforce these clauses strictly because the parties chose a bright line. Let us look at a standard clause. The Seller shall not be liable unless the Buyer gives written notice within thirty days of becoming aware of it, and in any event within eighteen months after Completion. Any claim so notified shall be deemed withdrawn unless proceedings are issued and served within six months of the notice.

**Sam:** Three gates. Awareness plus thirty days. Completion plus eighteen months. Notice plus six months. Let us run the math. Completion was the fifteenth of January, twenty twenty-six. The buyer's finance team becomes aware of a warranty breach on the first of June, twenty twenty-seven. The buyer sends a compliant notice on the tenth of July, twenty twenty-seven. Counting day one as the second of June, are they in time?

**Nadia:** Two notice deadlines apply. The eighteen-month backstop expires on the fifteenth of July, twenty twenty-seven. They met that, with five days to spare. But the awareness deadline is thirty days from the first of June. June has thirty days, so it expires on the first of July, twenty twenty-seven.

**Sam:** And the notice went on the tenth of July. Nine days late.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Because the clause says the seller shall not be liable unless both are met, the claim is barred. People check the backstop and completely forget the awareness clock.

**Sam:** What about the second clock? General warranty claims must be notified within twenty-four months of completion, and proceedings issued and served within nine months of the notice. The buyer notifies a claim exactly twenty months after completion. What is the absolute latest point, in months after completion, they can issue proceedings?

**Nadia:** Twenty-nine months. The second clock runs from the notice, not from the end of the warranty period. Twenty plus nine. The tempting answer is thirty-three, adding the nine to the twenty-four.

**Sam:** So notifying early starts my litigation clock early.

**Nadia:** Which is exactly why buyers sometimes hold a notice until the claim is well prepared, so long as no awareness deadline forces their hand.

**Sam:** What about format? The notices clause says delivery by hand or courier to the registered office, marked for the General Counsel. On the last day, the buyer's lawyer emails a detailed claim to the seller's CEO, who reads it that day. Is it notified in time?

**Nadia:** Probably not. The contract defines what counts as notice. Email is not a permitted method here, and actual knowledge does not cure a defective notice. Purposive arguments, like 'he read it', are weak against a clear clause. Serve early, by every permitted method, exactly as the clause says.

**Sam:** Money mechanics. Gross-up. If a payment under the SPA is taxed or subject to withholding, the payer increases it so the recipient keeps the intended amount. Let us run the numbers. The seller owes one million dirhams under an indemnity. Assume the receipt is taxed at twenty percent in the buyer's hands. What is the payment?

**Nadia:** The common error is adding twenty percent to get one million, two hundred thousand. But if you receive that and pay twenty percent tax, you lose two hundred and forty thousand. You are left with nine hundred and sixty thousand. You missed the target.

**Sam:** You have to solve for the principal. Principal times one minus zero point two zero equals one million. One million divided by zero point eight is one million, two hundred and fifty thousand dirhams.

**Nadia:** Correct. Whether the receipt is taxed at all depends on the tax law in question, but the clause allocates the risk whichever way it falls. Now, set-off. Deducting what the other side owes you from what you owe them.

**Sam:** Sellers hate it. The SPA says all payments shall be made without set-off or counterclaim. The buyer owes ten million dirhams of deferred consideration on the first of March. In February, the buyer notifies a four million dirham warranty claim, which the seller disputes. The buyer pays six million. What happens?

**Nadia:** The buyer is in breach for four million dirhams. They owe default interest on it, and must pursue their warranty claim separately. The no set-off clause exists for exactly this moment. It keeps the seller's cash certain. If a buyer wants to withhold, they must negotiate an express right to set off, or an escrow. Nothing is held back automatically.

**Sam:** Governing law and dispute resolution. Governing law is the legal system whose rules interpret the contract. Dispute resolution is who decides and where. Courts are public, with appeals. But judgments are only enforced abroad if a treaty allows it.

**Nadia:** Which is why cross-border deals use arbitration. A private tribunal, usually confidential, with almost no appeal. Crucially, its decision, the award, is enforceable in most countries under a widely adopted international convention. You must fix the seat of arbitration, whose courts supervise it and whose law governs the procedure.

**Sam:** And expert determination. For accounting disagreements like completion accounts or earn-outs. An independent accountant decides quickly as an expert. Final except for manifest error. It is the tool for 'what is the number', not 'what does the contract mean'.

**Nadia:** Split clauses are dangerous. If the SPA says arbitration and the escrow agreement says courts, one dispute can run in two forums with inconsistent results. Take a completion accounts dispute. The buyer values inventory three million dirhams lower. The seller says the buyer used a provisioning method not found in the accounting policies schedule.

**Sam:** The SPA sends disputes on Completion Accounts to the expert, and all other disputes to arbitration. The buyer files for arbitration. Three million feels like a tribunal issue.

**Nadia:** It belongs with the expert. The clause routes by subject, not size. Inventory valuation is the expert's core job. But the seller will argue that interpreting the policies schedule is a legal question. Good clauses explicitly say who decides that boundary.

**Sam:** Any traps in the UAE?

**Nadia:** Two. Asymmetric dispute clauses, where one side can choose between forums but the other cannot, have been held invalid onshore. Second, the DIFC-LCIA arbitration centre was abolished in September twenty twenty-one, but old clauses naming it still turn up in due diligence. Treat any dispute clause touching the UAE as a point to ask counsel about.

**Sam:** Ancillary documents. The SPA is the hub, but there is a whole bundle. Disclosure letter for warranty exceptions. Tax deed for pre-closing tax cover. Transitional services agreement, or TSA. In a carve-out, the seller's group keeps providing IT, payroll, or premises to the sold business for a fixed period.

**Nadia:** Until the buyer can stand it up alone. Watch scope, service levels, price, duration, and exit help.

**Sam:** If I am the CTO, and the seller offers a TSA for six months, but I know migrating off their legacy ERP takes ten months. I ask for twelve months, with an option to extend, and migration assistance.

**Nadia:** The seller will say it is not a service provider and wants out. It will price extensions at a rising premium to push you off. The usual landing is a term near your realistic estimate, a short extension right at a stepped-up price, and a service level set to the same standard as the prior twelve months.

**Sam:** The rest of the bundle. Shareholders' agreement if the buyer takes less than one hundred percent. Employment agreements. Management incentive plans. Escrow agreements. IP assignments. Powers of attorney.

**Nadia:** And the share transfer instrument. The short form that actually transfers title. For an onshore UAE LLC, that is a notarised Arabic transfer document, which we covered in module twelve.

**Sam:** After closing, where do deals actually end up in dispute? Price mechanics.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Completion accounts and earn-outs generate far more post-closing disputes than warranty claims. A price adjustment happens on every deal that has one. The amounts are certain to be computed, both sides have accountants ready, and there is no cap or basket in the way.

**Sam:** A warranty claim requires an unknown problem to surface, survive disclosure, clear the financial limits, and pass those three strict notice gates. It has too many filters.

**Nadia:** Which is why buyers prefer a dispute over completion accounts to a warranty claim for the same two million dirhams. It is pound for pound, with no need to prove breach or loss. SRS Acquiom twenty twenty-six reports that thirty-nine percent of working-capital adjustments use an agreed worksheet methodology in place of a generic reference to accounting standards. Specificity is your best defense.

**Sam:** We are out of time. Three takeaways. One: Notice clauses are strict API gateways. Serve early, by every permitted method, exactly as the clause says, or your claim is worth zero. Two: Gross-up math means solving for the principal, not just adding the tax rate. Three: Price mechanics cause the most disputes because they are uncapped, unavoidable, and pound-for-pound.

**Nadia:** And that concludes the written course. Thank you for listening.
