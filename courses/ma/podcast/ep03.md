# Episode 3: Companies, structures and who is in the room

**Sam:** I have a U A E logistics company. Twelve warehouses, three large customer contracts with no transfer restrictions, and forty staff. But there is a historic customs under-payment investigation, and the size of the liability is unknown. I want the warehouses and the customers. How do we structure this?

**Nadia:** Asset purchase. The liability is unquantifiable, so you cannot price it. The things you want are few and transferable. You leave the history in the seller's company.

**Sam:** This is Companies, structures and who is in the room.

**Nadia:** To understand deal structures, you have to understand what a company actually is. It is a legal person, separate from the people who own it. It signs contracts, employs staff, sues and is sued in its own name. That drives two consequences: limited liability and continuity.

**Sam:** Limited liability means the failure mode is contained. The company's debts belong to the company. An owner can lose what it invested and no more, unless a court pierces the corporate veil for something rare, like fraud.

**Nadia:** And continuity means the outside world does not notice who owns the company. The contracts, licences, staff and liabilities stay with the entity when its shareholders change.

**Sam:** So ownership is just an abstraction layer. A shareholder owns a bundle of rights: votes, a share of any dividend, and a share of whatever is left in a wind-up. They don't manage. They appoint directors who run the company and owe their duties to the company itself.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The rulebook governing that relationship is the constitution. In English-style systems, we call them the articles of association. They set share classes, voting thresholds, and transfer limits. And businesses are rarely just one company. They are groups.

**Sam:** Groups are just trees of companies. Holdco at the top, subsidiaries below, and an operating company, or Opco, holding the actual business. Let's map the difference between control and economic interest down that chain. Holdco owns eighty percent of Midco. Midco owns sixty percent of Opco. Each share is one vote. Opco pays a dividend of one hundred dirhams. What percentage ultimately belongs to Holdco?

**Nadia:** Economic interest multiplies down the chain. Zero point eight zero times zero point six zero is zero point four eight. So Holdco gets forty-eight percent of the economics.

**Sam:** But control does not multiply. Holdco controls Midco because eighty percent is more than half. Midco controls Opco because sixty percent is more than half. So Holdco controls Opco's board absolutely, while owning under half of its money. That asymmetry is a dangerous wedge if you are a minority investor.

**Nadia:** Which is why groups use separate companies to ring-fence liabilities, so a claim against one Opco cannot reach its sister. They also use them to fit local licensing rules, and to create clean units that can be sold one at a time.

**Sam:** How does that map to the U A E specifically?

**Nadia:** Three flavours matter. An onshore mainland limited liability company is licensed by an emirate's economic department and trades anywhere in the U A E. Foreigners can own one hundred percent by default, except in strategic activities. Then you have free zone companies, licensed by specific authorities with their own registries.

**Sam:** And the D I F C or A D G M companies. Those sit in the financial free zones under common-law statutes and English-language courts.

**Nadia:** Right. Many groups put a D I F C or A D G M Holdco on top of onshore Opcos so investors can deal with each other under familiar common-law rules. The mechanics of moving shares differ completely. Transferring an onshore L L C needs a notarised document and an updated licence. A D I F C transfer is just a signed form and a register entry.

**Sam:** Let's talk about how we actually buy these businesses. M and A means transferring ownership or control of a target. The source gives us three structures: share purchase, asset purchase, and statutory merger.

**Nadia:** In a share purchase, the buyer buys the target company's shares. The company is untouched, it just has a new owner. In an asset purchase, the buyer buys chosen assets and assumes chosen liabilities. The seller is the company itself, which stays behind with its old owners and whatever wasn't sold.

**Sam:** And a statutory merger is two companies combining into one by a legal procedure. All assets and liabilities pass automatically, and a required majority of shareholders can bind those who voted against. It's the standard tool for U S listed companies where you can't get thousands of shareholders to sign a contract, but they are uncommon in U A E private M and A.

**Nadia:** Correct. In private M and A, the seller generally prefers a share sale. It leaves with cash and no company to clean up. The buyer accepts the inherited liabilities and manages them by investigation, contract protection, and price. So share purchases dominate.

**Sam:** I want to push back on that. If an asset deal leaves the liabilities behind, why doesn't every buyer insist on one? Surely a share purchase is a bad trade because you inherit the history, known and unknown.

**Nadia:** Because the friction is real, and the seller's price goes up. In an asset deal, every customer contract needs consent, which gives every customer a free option to renegotiate. Licences may need fresh applications. The seller is left with a shell holding liabilities and possibly two layers of tax, so it demands more money.

**Sam:** And employees. In the U K and E U, they transfer automatically. But in many places, including the U A E, they do not. Each one is terminated by the seller and rehired by the buyer. That triggers payment of their accrued end-of-service gratuity, a statutory lump sum owed when employment ends.

**Nadia:** Whereas in a share purchase, the employer does not change. The accrued gratuity stays inside the target as a liability the buyer inherits, but no cash goes out the door on day one.

**Sam:** Let's test the friction. A software company has nine hundred customer contracts. Most forbid transfer without the customer's consent but say nothing about a change of owner. It holds a regulatory licence that cannot be transferred. Its history is clean. Asset or share?

**Nadia:** Share purchase. The contracting party does not change, so a ban on transfer is not triggered, and the licence stays where it is. An asset purchase would need nine hundred consents and a new licence. Even seeking consent from just the fifty largest customers leaves eight hundred and fifty contracts not validly transferred. The structures are never equivalent because they differ in what moves automatically.

**Sam:** Let's put a price on that choice. I am choosing a structure. Share route: I estimate a ten percent chance of an inherited claim costing thirty million dirhams, with no protection from the seller. Asset route: no inherited claim, but I pay one point eight million dirhams of gratuity on rehiring staff, seven hundred thousand dirhams of transfer costs, and face a twenty percent chance that a key customer refuses consent, costing four million.

**Nadia:** Let's run the expected cost. The share route is zero point one zero times thirty million, which is three million dirhams. The asset route is one point eight million plus seven hundred thousand, which is two point five million. Then add the customer risk: zero point two zero times four million is eight hundred thousand. Total for the asset route is three point three million dirhams.

**Sam:** So the expected cost of the asset route is three hundred thousand dirhams higher. But look at the failure modes. The share route's worst case is thirty million. The asset route's worst case is six point five million.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The expected values are close, but the tails are not. That is why the real answer is usually a share deal, which avoids the operational friction, paired with contractual protection against that specific thirty million claim. Asset deals are chosen when the history is the problem, but they were only twenty-one percent of the sample in the A B A twenty twenty-five study of U S private deals.

**Sam:** Let's move to why we are buying at all. A buyer only pays more than standalone value if they expect to make the difference back. The source lists synergy, scale, capability, financial return, and exit.

**Nadia:** Synergies are the extra value created when two businesses combine. Lower costs, like one head office or one tech stack. Or higher revenue, like selling each side's products to the other's customers. Scale gives you more volume over the same fixed costs. Capability is buying a team or a technology that would take years to build.

**Sam:** Cost synergies are highly reliable because the buyer controls its own systems. Revenue synergies are mostly fiction because you do not control your customers. So how do we price this? A target is worth one hundred million dirhams on its own. We estimate synergies with a present value of thirty million, and one-off integration costs of eight million. What is the ceiling price before I destroy value?

**Nadia:** Value to you is one hundred, plus thirty, minus eight, which equals one hundred and twenty-two million dirhams. At one hundred and twenty-two, the seller has captured the entire gain, and you break even. If you pay one hundred and twenty-five, you destroy three million.

**Sam:** And that ceiling is fragile. If only half the synergies arrive, the ceiling drops to one hundred, plus fifteen, minus eight, which is one hundred and seven million. Also, any risk I am forced to keep under the contract, like an inherited liability, comes straight off that ceiling. If the value case is thin, a small unprotected risk is enough to say walk.

**Nadia:** Which brings us to who is sitting across the table. A party's type predicts its negotiating behaviour better than anything its lawyers say. On the buyer side, you have strategic buyers, financial buyers, and sovereign wealth funds.

**Sam:** Strategic is an operating company buying a related business. They have synergies, hold for the long term, and care about integration. A financial buyer is usually a private equity fund. A fixed-term pool of money, typically ten years, managed by a sponsor. They use debt, improve the company, and sell. Zero synergies, so they are brutal on price and intensely focused on terms.

**Nadia:** And sovereign wealth funds have very long horizons, massive capital, and often take minority positions. Their involvement can trigger regulatory and political review. The PwC TransAct twenty twenty-six report notes Middle East sovereign funds invested one hundred and twenty-seven billion U S dollars globally in twenty twenty-five.

**Sam:** The seller side is where the incentives really diverge, based on what happens to the money. Let's compare a founder with a corporate carve-out.

**Nadia:** A founder knows the business intimately. Much of their wealth is tied up in this one sale, and they often stay on as a manager. They can give detailed promises because they have the knowledge, but they resist personal exposure. A corporate carve-out is a large company selling a division. They are solvent repeat players who negotiate from standard positions and concede little.

**Sam:** The hardest part of a carve-out is that the division shares systems and staff with the parent. You have to physically separate the codebases and get the parent to supply shared services for a period. Then you have distressed sellers, running out of cash. They sell fast and as is. You get almost no promises, and any you get are worthless because there is no money behind them. The price reflects that.

**Nadia:** Let's look closely at the private equity fund exiting. They didn't run the business day to day, and they must distribute proceeds to their investors and wind the fund up. They demand a clean exit: fixed price, no holdbacks, and minimal liability after the sale.

**Sam:** I have a P E fund selling a company for two hundred million dirhams. I ask for twenty million to be held back for two years to cover claims. Why do they fight this so much harder than a founder would?

**Nadia:** Because a fund's performance is measured by cash returned and speed. Money held back is cash they cannot distribute. A contingent liability means they cannot close the fund. The managers might even have to return part of their own profit share if a claim succeeds later. A founder just has less money in the bank for two years. Same clause, completely different cost.

**Sam:** So the fund will trade price or buy insurance to avoid it. Warranty and indemnity insurance, W and I. The buyer gets a policy, the insurer pays for unknown breaches of the seller's warranties, and the P E seller walks away clean. Who is actually using this?

**Nadia:** It is standard. The C M S European M and A Study twenty twenty-six found W and I insurance in twenty-three percent of its deals, and fifty-nine percent of large deals. In the U S, it is called representations and warranties insurance, or R W I. The A B A twenty twenty-five study found it in sixty-three percent of its sample.

**Sam:** Let's look at the premium. The buyer drops the holdback and buys a W and I policy with a limit of twenty million dirhams. Marsh's Q one twenty twenty-six data puts Middle East premiums at zero point seven percent to one point eight percent of the policy limit. Say the quote is one point five percent. What is the cost?

**Nadia:** One point five percent of twenty million is three hundred thousand dirhams. Compare that to the seller locking up twenty million for two years. The insurance is cheap relative to the friction it removes, which is why the negotiation is usually just about who pays the premium.

**Sam:** Let's test how much a clean exit is worth. A P E fund in its ninth year receives two bids. Bid A is two hundred and ten million dirhams, ten percent held back for twenty-four months, and the seller liable for warranty claims up to thirty percent of the price. Bid B is two hundred and four million, nothing held back, seller's liability limited to one dirham, and the buyer takes out W and I insurance.

**Nadia:** They take Bid B. Certainty and immediate distribution of all proceeds are worth more to a late-life fund than a three percent price difference. Bid A gives them an extra six million, but locks up twenty-one million and leaves up to sixty-three million of exposure hanging over them for two years.

**Sam:** The mistake is thinking the holdback will probably be returned anyway. Probably is not certainly, and a fund cannot distribute on probably. A bidder who understands the seller's type can win with a lower headline price.

**Nadia:** Now flip it. You are buying from a founder who keeps twenty percent of the shares and stays as C E O for three years. She can give fuller warranties than a fund, but you will be reluctant to sue your own C E O. It is self-harm. So you want protection that does not depend on a lawsuit, like money held back or adjustments to her deferred payments. Her twenty percent stake aligns her on the future, not on problems that already existed.

**Sam:** Let's talk about the sale process itself. Private M and A is buying a company not traded on an exchange. Free negotiation, deep investigation. Within private deals, we have auctions and bilateral deals. Leverage is simply the other side's belief that you have an alternative.

**Nadia:** In an auction, the seller's financial adviser invites multiple buyers and collects bids against deadlines. Competition creates leverage for the seller. In a bilateral deal, you negotiate one on one. The buyer normally demands exclusivity: a binding promise by the seller not to talk to other buyers for a fixed period. Exclusivity removes the seller's alternative.

**Sam:** Let's look at the first draft of the S P A, the share purchase agreement. Whoever drafts it sets the baseline, because every change the other side asks for looks like a concession. The seller drafts in an auction, the buyer often drafts in a bilateral deal.

**Nadia:** In an auction with four bidders, you submit the highest price along with sixty changes to the seller's draft contract. A rival bids four percent less with only eight changes. The seller chooses the rival. Why?

**Sam:** Because they price the bid as a package. Sixty changes signal massive risk coming back to the seller and a lower probability of actually signing on time. That expected cost outweighs a four percent premium. The skill is choosing the few changes that carry real money value, which requires knowing where the terms land anyway.

**Nadia:** And in a bilateral deal, exclusivity is a decaying asset. Say another bidder has told the seller they are still interested. Ten days into your thirty-day exclusivity, you find a problem in diligence and ask for a ten percent price cut. Or, twenty-five days in, on the same facts, you ask for the cut. When are they more likely to agree?

**Sam:** Day ten. The seller's best alternative is twenty days of doing nothing, so they might concede. At day twenty-five, their alternative is waiting five days and calling the other bidder. The deadline pressures the party with no alternative, which at day twenty-five is the buyer. Leverage is a function of alternatives at that exact moment, not the facts alone.

**Nadia:** Not every deal is for control. Minority investments, venture rounds for newly issued shares, and joint ventures all involve buying less than fifty percent. Because you cannot outvote the others, your protection has to be contractual, written into the shareholders' agreement, or S H A.

**Sam:** Four mechanisms recur. Reserved matters: a list of decisions like new debt or selling the company that need the minority's consent. It's a veto, not a power to act. Pre-emption: a right of first refusal so stakes aren't diluted and strangers can't arrive. Drag-along, and tag-along.

**Nadia:** Drag-along protects the majority. If holders above a stated threshold agree to sell the company, they can force the rest to sell on the same terms. It ensures they can deliver one hundred percent to a buyer. Tag-along protects the minority. If the majority sells, the minority can require the buyer to take its shares too, on the same terms, so they aren't left behind with a new controller.

**Sam:** Let's test the drag-along. The clause says: if shareholders holding at least seventy-five percent of the shares wish to transfer all their shares to a bona fide third-party purchaser, they may require all other shareholders to transfer all their shares on the same terms and at the same price.

**Nadia:** A company has three shareholders: A with fifty-five percent, B with twenty-five percent, and C with twenty percent. A buyer offers a good price for one hundred percent. A and C want to sell. B refuses. Can the sale happen?

**Sam:** A has fifty-five, C has twenty. Fifty-five plus twenty is seventy-five percent. They meet the threshold exactly. So yes, they can drag B on the same terms. A's fifty-five percent controls the board, but control of a company is not ownership of other people's shares. You need the drag right to force the sale.

**Nadia:** And as a fifteen percent investor, what does the S H A not give you? Reserved matters protect you while you are in, but none of these four mechanisms gives you a way to force a sale of your own stake. Tag-along only helps if the majority chooses to sell. A stake you cannot exit is worth far less than its percentage suggests, which is why minority investors negotiate explicit exit rights, like forcing a sale or listing after a set number of years.

**Sam:** Finally, let's map the room. The cast of characters. Principals bear the outcome. Local counsel handle country-specific formalities. Accountants test whether reported profit is real and check tax exposure. Lenders finance the price and demand certainty. Escrow agents hold the money and take no side.

**Nadia:** The key is knowing who pays who, and how. Investment bankers run the sale process and advise on value. They are mostly paid a success fee, a percentage of the price paid only if the deal closes. Lawyers structure the deal and negotiate contracts. They are paid by time, fixed or capped, and their name is on the advice.

**Sam:** So if the seller's investment banker calls me and says, 'Honestly, the lawyers are overcomplicating this. Sign the seller's draft as it stands and you will win.' How do I weigh that?

**Nadia:** You treat it as a statement from someone paid on closing, whose fee does not fall if you carry more risk after the deal. The banker might be right that a clean markup wins, but they work for the seller. You use the signal about what the seller values, but you let your own lawyers decide which risks you can afford to accept.

**Sam:** Due diligence is the buyer's tool to shrink that information gap. It feeds the price and the contract across seven phases: strategy, N D A, letter of intent, diligence, S P A negotiation, signing to closing, and post-closing.

**Nadia:** Time for takeaways.

**Sam:** One: The company is a separate entity. In a share deal, you buy the history. In an asset deal, you buy specific pieces, but the friction and cost of moving contracts and staff usually make the share route cheaper in expected value.

**Nadia:** Two: A party's type predicts its behaviour. A private equity fund needs a clean exit to close its fund, so it will trade price for certainty and rely on W and I insurance. A founder is tied to the future, so you need mechanisms that don't rely on litigation.

**Sam:** Three: Leverage is just alternatives. Sixty S P A changes in an auction will lose to a clean markup, and exclusivity decays every day you hold it. If you are a minority, reserved matters are a shield, but you need explicit exit rights to get your money out.

**Nadia:** Next time, we look at non-disclosure agreements, letters of intent, and how those first documents lock in the leverage.
