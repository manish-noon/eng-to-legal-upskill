# Episode 4: The deal timeline and the documents before the SPA

**Sam:** You could spend three months negotiating a hundred-page share purchase agreement. By the time the first draft arrives, which bargaining choices have you already made?

**Nadia:** Think of exclusivity. Once the seller agrees to stop talking to other bidders, the buyer has more room to press its terms. This is episode four, The Deal Timeline and the Documents Before the SPA.

**Sam:** I'd want two answers for every document I sign along the way. What information do I now get, and what promises, if any, have I just made?

**Nadia:** And the durations vary wildly. A simple bilateral deal with no regulatory approvals can run from the first call to closing in three months. A cross-border deal in a regulated sector can take a year. But the sequence of those documents is predictable.

**Sam:** Let us start at the very beginning. The price of admission. The NDA, or non-disclosure agreement.

**Nadia:** Right. The problem the NDA solves is simple: the buyer cannot price your business without seeing its secrets. But the most motivated buyer is often a direct competitor who could exploit those secrets if the deal dies.

**Sam:** So we define Confidential Information widely. Everything supplied about the target, the buyer's own analyses derived from it, and even the fact that talks are happening. What are the standard exceptions?

**Nadia:** Information that is already public, already known to the buyer, independently developed, or lawfully received from a third party. But the secrecy promise is only half the battle. The more important piece is the use restriction.

**Sam:** Let us stress-test that. Say a competitor signs an NDA. They review our customer-level pricing, then drop out of the process. Six months later, they undercut us on three tenders by tiny margins. They never showed the data to anyone. Which clause did they breach?

**Nadia:** The use restriction. Nothing was disclosed to a third party, so the secrecy promise is perfectly intact. The breach is using the information for a purpose other than evaluating the deal. The standard wording says they shall use it solely for the purpose of evaluating the proposed transaction.

**Sam:** But proving they used it to reprice their own tenders sounds like a nightmare. They will just claim they guessed.

**Nadia:** It is a nightmare. Proving use is incredibly hard. That practical weakness is exactly why smart sellers stage their disclosure. You hold back the most sensitive pricing or technical data from competitors until very late in the diligence process.

**Sam:** What about the permitted recipients? I assume the buyer cannot just broadcast this to their entire company.

**Nadia:** Correct. The buyer may share it only with its Representatives—directors, employees, advisers, lenders—who strictly need to know. And the crucial mechanism here is strict liability: the buyer is legally liable for any breach of the NDA by its Representatives.

**Sam:** I also see non-solicit clauses in these NDAs. Without them, letting a competitor do diligence is just handing them a recruiting catalogue. We usually ask for two years.

**Nadia:** Buyers will resist two years for all employees. They hire in the same labour market anyway. The typical landing zone is twelve to twenty-four months, restricted only to key employees the buyer actually met during the deal process, with a carve-out for general advertising.

**Sam:** And the standstill clause? The buyer promises not to buy shares in the target directly without board consent. Why does a private company care about that?

**Nadia:** It matters mainly when the target or its parent is listed. It stops a bidder from using inside knowledge to launch a hostile takeover. For a pure private company, it is less relevant, but you still see it in the templates.

**Sam:** Sellers typically want these obligations to last indefinitely, especially for trade secrets. Buyers want a twelve-month term because tracking NDA obligations indefinitely is an administrative burden.

**Nadia:** And the market standard landing is a fixed term of two to three years. Also, note that NDAs almost always state the seller gives no promise that the information is accurate. Promises about accuracy come much later, as warranties in the SPA, and the buyer pays for them.

**Sam:** One legal quirk. Why do sellers insist the NDA says they can seek an injunction? If you breach, I sue you for damages. Why state the obvious about court orders?

**Nadia:** Because the loss from leaked trade secrets is hard to quantify and impossible to reverse. An injunction prevents the harm; damages only price it afterwards, and you have to prove the amount. The clause records the parties' agreement that damages alone are inadequate, which helps persuade a court to actually grant the order.

**Sam:** Let us move to auction documents. If I am selling my company in a competitive process, the bank controls a staged release of information. First is the teaser: one or two pages, anonymous, just enough to generate interest but not enough to identify us. Sent before the NDA.

**Nadia:** Then, after the NDA is signed, bidders get the Information Memorandum, or IM. It is a fifty to one hundred page selling document. But legally, it is pure marketing. It carries a heavy disclaimer that nobody may rely on it.

**Sam:** Let us test that disclaimer. The IM states that the target holds all licences required for its operations. We close the deal. I find out one licence was never obtained. The SPA contains no warranty on licences and says I have not relied on any statement outside the SPA. Under English law, what is my position?

**Nadia:** You have, in practice, no claim unless you can show outright fraud. The IM disclaimer combined with the SPA's non-reliance wording is deliberately designed to make the SPA the only source of enforceable promises.

**Sam:** So any specification in the marketing brochure is dead to me unless I copy-paste it into the binding contract as a warranty. That is a massive lesson for due diligence.

**Nadia:** Exactly. After the IM comes the process letter. These are the rules of the game: deadlines, what an offer must contain, and a statement that the seller may change the rules or stop the process at any time without liability.

**Sam:** Then the bidders submit an indicative offer. Price, funding sources, key assumptions. The seller uses these to shortlist a few bidders for full diligence. Finally, the binding offer. A price, plus a mark-up of the seller's draft SPA.

**Nadia:** And note that binding is loose usage here. It normally means the bidder is ready to sign that specific SPA mark-up, not that a contract already exists. The crucial mechanism is that the seller scores bids on price and contract terms together. A bidder offering one hundred with a heavily marked-up SPA can easily lose to a bidder offering ninety-seven with a clean SPA.

**Sam:** Because the seller discounts the headline price for the risk that the heavy mark-up will allow the buyer to claw the money back later. That makes perfect sense. Next stage: the Letter of Intent. The LOI, term sheet, heads of terms. It is deliberately split into two classes of terms: binding and non-binding.

**Nadia:** Yes. The commercial terms are usually not binding: price, structure, form of payment, conditions, warranty outlines, timetable. The process terms are binding: exclusivity, confidentiality, costs, governing law and dispute forum.

**Sam:** Why the split? If we agree on a price, why not make it binding?

**Nadia:** Because you have not done your diligence yet. A binding price before diligence is something no informed buyer will sign. But the process terms must bind immediately because they protect the massive investment both sides are about to make in lawyers and accountants.

**Sam:** Under English law, the LOI usually says subject to contract. That is the magic formula telling a court we do not intend to be bound until the formal SPA is signed, except for the specific paragraphs we carve out.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Let us try a scenario. An English-law LOI headed subject to contract contains: A, price of one hundred and twenty million dirhams; B, seller will not negotiate with others for forty-five days; C, each party bears its own costs; D, seller will give customary warranties; E, disputes go to DIFC-seated arbitration. Which ones bind?

**Sam:** B, C, and E. Exclusivity, costs, and the dispute forum are process terms and get carved out. Price and warranties are commercial and do not bind. But if the price is non-binding, why do we spend weeks fighting over it in the LOI?

**Nadia:** Because a non-binding LOI still anchors everything. First, moral commitment: the founders who shook hands on a number have to explain any departure to their own boards. Second, the burden of proof flips. Whoever wants to move away from an LOI term must produce a new fact from diligence to justify it.

**Sam:** And third, re-trading—reopening an agreed term—costs credibility. If I re-trade the price, you will respond by reopening the liability caps. It is not legally enforceable, but it is highly expensive to change.

**Nadia:** Precisely. Which is why your leverage as a seller peaks just before you grant exclusivity. Suppose a buyer sends an LOI saying: Price, one hundred million dirhams on a cash-free, debt-free basis, subject to due diligence, and asks for sixty days exclusivity. What is your move?

**Sam:** I do not sign it quickly, because the price is not actually locked. Debt and working capital are undefined. I would say: before we grant exclusivity, we need to define what counts as debt, agree the working capital basis, and set the liability cap. I spend my leverage on the definitions that actually move the money.

**Nadia:** Perfect. One jurisdictional note here. Some US courts will enforce an express promise in an LOI to negotiate in good faith. English law does not enforce a bare agreement to negotiate; that is the rule in Walford versus Miles. Read a US-law LOI carefully before assuming it is toothless.

**Sam:** And in Dubai? UAE onshore law is civil law, which contains general good-faith duties.

**Nadia:** Yes, and whether those duties reach pre-contract negotiations is a complex question for your lawyer, not something to guess. It is a major reason parties often choose English, DIFC or ADGM law for the LOI itself.

**Sam:** Let us talk about Exclusivity. The lock-out. The seller's binding promise not to solicit, negotiate with, or give information to any other buyer for a fixed period. It solves my problem as a buyer: I am not spending millions on diligence if you are just using my offer as a stalking horse.

**Nadia:** Right. Buyers want eight to twelve weeks, with automatic extensions. Sellers want four weeks, ending early if the buyer drops the price, and absolutely no automatic extension. A common landing is four to eight weeks, extended by agreement only.

**Sam:** What is the remedy if the seller breaches and signs with someone else? Can I sue for my lost profit on the deal?

**Nadia:** Rarely. You cannot prove the deal would have definitely signed. The remedy is normally your wasted costs, sometimes fixed in advance as a cost cover. Let us run the math on a cost cover. The LOI says if the seller breaches, they reimburse documented third-party adviser costs up to one point five million dirhams.

**Sam:** Okay. In week five, the seller signs with a rival. I have spent one point two five million on lawyers, four hundred and fifty thousand on accountants, and four hundred thousand of internal staff time.

**Nadia:** Let us calculate. Third-party costs are one point two five million plus four hundred and fifty thousand. That equals one point seven million. Your internal staff time of four hundred thousand is completely excluded because it is not a third-party cost.

**Sam:** So my valid claim is one point seven million. But the cap is one point five million. So I recover exactly one point five million dirhams. I am still out of pocket by six hundred thousand, even with a proven breach.

**Nadia:** Exactly. A cost cover prices the breach; it does not make it unprofitable for a seller who just received a massively higher counter-offer. Now, what if the seller has granted six weeks of exclusivity, and in week five the buyer asks for four more weeks. What does that tell the seller?

**Sam:** It tells them I am not ready. Signing is delayed. And it tells them they briefly have their leverage back, because the extension is theirs to give.

**Nadia:** Yes. A seller who extends for free has given away the one bargaining chip exclusivity leaves them. They should charge for it: ask for confirmation that the price stands, demand a closed list of open points, or tie a shorter extension to strict milestones.

**Sam:** Diligence is done and the parties have agreed the SPA. On closing day the seller doesn't want to release shares before it gets paid, and the buyer doesn't want to pay before it gets title. How do we arrange the exchange?

**Nadia:** Start with the list of closing deliverables. It might call for signed share transfers, board approvals, director resignations and lender releases. Then read the funds flow memo. For each payment it names the payer, recipient, amount and account. Agree who holds each document and what authorises release.

**Sam:** But Signing and Closing are often separate events. There is a gap between them. Why?

**Nadia:** Because of Conditions Precedent, or CPs. Events that must occur before the obligation to close arises. Merger control clearance, sector regulator approvals, key customer consents. Neither side can get these before the contract actually exists to show the regulator.

**Sam:** And how does UAE merger control affect this?

**Nadia:** It is mandatory and suspensory. If a deal meets the thresholds—three hundred million dirhams of relevant UAE sales, or a forty percent combined share, which has been in force since March 31, 2025—you cannot close before clearance. You must file at least ninety days before completion, and silence at the end of the review period is a deemed rejection. That alone forces a gap.

**Sam:** So which deal can actually sign and close on the same day? Let us look at four options. One: A buyer with nine hundred million dirhams of UAE sales buying a competitor with three hundred and fifty million in the same market.

**Nadia:** No, that triggers suspensory merger control. There must be a gap.

**Sam:** Two: A target whose largest contract terminates on a change of owner, and consent has not been requested yet.

**Nadia:** No, the consent is a CP that needs time to satisfy.

**Sam:** Three: A DIFC holding company with no regulated activities, sold to a financial investor with no overlapping business, and customer consents are already in hand.

**Nadia:** Yes. No regulator, no competition overlap, and consents are obtained. That can sign and close simultaneously. Both sides prefer it because it entirely removes gap risk.

**Sam:** And four was a licensed insurer sold to a foreign group, which obviously needs a sector regulator. Let us talk about gap risk. The SPA is signed March first with one CP: merger clearance. Long-stop date is June thirtieth. On May fifteenth, the target loses its second-largest customer. Clearance arrives June first. Can I walk away?

**Nadia:** Does the SPA have an express clause letting you exit for deterioration in the business?

**Sam:** No.

**Nadia:** Then you must close at the agreed price. After signing, the buyer is bound. The only exits are an unsatisfied CP at the long-stop date, or an express termination right. The CP was met, so the obligation to close triggered. Gap risk sits with you.

**Sam:** Ouch. This brings us to leverage. Leverage is simply each side's cost of walking away compared with the other's. Before exclusivity, the seller is strong because multiple bidders are terrified of losing to each other.

**Nadia:** But by late diligence, before signing, the buyer is strong. The rival bidders have redeployed. If the seller restarts, the market asks what the first buyer found to make them walk. Management is tired, and the seller has mentally spent the money.

**Sam:** Let us quantify that late-stage leverage. LOI price is one hundred million dirhams. Late in exclusivity, I demand a price cut. The seller's alternative is to return to the runner-up, who bid ninety-seven million.

**Nadia:** Assume the seller estimates a seventy percent chance the runner-up deal completes. If it fails, the seller keeps the business, valued at eighty-five million. Restarting costs one million in fees either way. Let us calculate the walk-away value for a risk-neutral seller.

**Sam:** Value of walking equals zero point seven times ninety-seven, plus zero point three times eighty-five, minus one. That is sixty-seven point nine, plus twenty-five point five, minus one. Which lands at exactly ninety-two point four million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Correct. The seller should accept any price above ninety-two point four million. So the absolute largest cut the buyer can demand before the seller is better off walking is one hundred minus ninety-two point four, which is seven point six million dirhams.

**Sam:** The buyer's leverage is exactly the gap between the LOI price and the seller's decayed alternative. So if I am the seller, I shrink that gap by keeping the runner-up warm for as long as the exclusivity wording legally permits.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Let us test another scenario. A founder is selling to a single PE buyer. Exclusivity has run ten weeks after two free extensions. The founder told key staff and two customers about the sale. The buyer now asks for fifteen percent of the price to be held back for two years, which was not in the LOI.

**Sam:** The founder is incredibly weak here. There is no alternative buyer. A failed sale damages the business because staff and customers know. And the LOI is silent on holdbacks, giving no protection. The buyer is exploiting the gap the founder left by giving free extensions and a vague LOI.

**Nadia:** Spot on. Now consider leverage during the gap between signing and closing. Suppose a buyer signs an SPA with a single CP: approval by the buyer's own shareholders, with no obligation to actually recommend or pursue that approval. Who holds the leverage?

**Sam:** The buyer. A condition precedent entirely within my control is just a free option. I can let the condition fail if the market worsens, or close if it improves. The seller is locked in and off the market the whole time.

**Nadia:** Which is why sellers refuse CPs the buyer controls, or pair them with an obligation to use best endeavours and a break fee if the condition fails. Finally, leverage after closing. Who holds it?

**Sam:** The seller, unless money was held back. The seller has the cash. The party who has to bring a claim bears the cost, the delay, and the burden of proof. If I want money back, I have to sue.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Possession of the cash decides who has to move. That brings us to the end of the timeline. Three takeaways. One, non-binding LOI terms anchor the deal because the burden of proof flips to whoever wants to change them.

**Sam:** Two, spend your leverage before you grant exclusivity. Detail is the currency the buyer trades for the lock-out. If you are selling, nail down the debt definitions and liability caps before you sign.

**Nadia:** Third, before granting exclusivity, consider how long the next bidder will stay interested. Next time we'll follow the price from the value of the business to the cash the seller receives.
