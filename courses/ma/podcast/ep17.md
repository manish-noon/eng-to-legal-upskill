# Episode 17: Three deals, three verdicts

**Sam:** A client calls you. They are ecstatic. They just bullied a seller into a one hundred percent liability cap on a major acquisition, and they think they have eliminated all their risk. What do you tell them?

**Nadia:** I tell them they have won a theoretical argument and probably lost the deal's actual economics. A general warranty cap is a safety net for unknown unknowns. It does absolutely nothing to protect you if the target's biggest customer walks out the door the day after closing, or if there is a massive, known environmental liability sitting on the site.

**Sam:** Welcome to episode seventeen. Three deals, three verdicts. Today we are looking at the capstone deals from the deal desk lab. We have the facts, the leverage scores, the diligence findings, and the risk registers for three completely different transactions.

**Nadia:** And our job is to predict where the negotiated terms will land based on precedent and leverage, and ultimately, to advise whether each deal is worth pursuing.

**Sam:** Let us start with Project Falcon. We act for the buyer, a Gulf logistics group. The target is a Dubai mainland limited liability company running cold-chain warehouses. The sellers are two founders in their sixties who are retiring. One of them has told our client privately that he wants to be completely done by year end.

**Nadia:** That private admission is a massive leverage point. Time pressure is squarely on the seller.

**Sam:** Exactly. It is a bilateral deal. No competing bidders. Our client approached them directly. The price agreed in the Letter of Intent is one hundred and eighty million dirhams, cash-free and debt-free. No Warranty and Indemnity insurance has been arranged. Our client values the business at two hundred and five million dirhams including synergies, and they have said they would be disappointed, but not damaged, to lose it.

**Nadia:** So the buyer can walk away if the math stops making sense. What is the gap between the seller's first draft of the Sale and Purchase Agreement and our client's instinct?

**Sam:** The seller's first draft SPA offers a liability cap at ten percent of the price, a deductible basket of one point five percent, a de minimis of zero point two five percent, claims must be brought within nine months, no escrow, English law, and DIAC arbitration seated in the DIFC.

**Nadia:** A nine-month survival period is aggressive, even for retiring founders. And a deductible basket means they only pay the excess above the threshold, not back to the first dirham. What does our client want to ask for?

**Sam:** Our client's instinct is to go for the throat. A one hundred percent cap, a tipping basket of zero point five percent, thirty-six months for claims, and a fifteen percent escrow.

**Nadia:** That instinct is exactly what we were just talking about. It is a bad trade. Asking for a one hundred percent cap when the uninsured precedent band normally sits between ten and thirty percent just burns goodwill. Let us look at the leverage score.

**Sam:** The raw leverage score is a plus four in favour of the buyer. Bilateral deal, no competing bidder, that is a plus one. Founder wants out by year end, plus one. No insurance, meaning the sellers are the only source of recovery, plus one. And diligence found real problems, which we will get to, that is another plus one.

**Nadia:** There is also the fact that founders leave with all the cash and will be hard to chase later, giving the buyer a fair right to insist on protection. But we have a merger filing risk. The combined UAE sales of the buyer and target in the relevant market were three hundred and forty million dirhams last year. That is above the three hundred million threshold.

**Sam:** Whether the test is combined or per party is an open point, so we have to plan for a filing. That introduces a delay risk that the sellers will want the buyer to carry, which costs us a leverage point. So the raw score of plus four gets clipped to a plus two.

**Nadia:** A plus two means leverage is strongly with the buyer, but not absolute. The model puts each term at the buyer-friendly edge of the uninsured band. We should expect the cap to land at about seventeen percent, the basket at about zero point five percent, survival at eighteen months, and an escrow of about five percent.

**Sam:** A senior lawyer might push that cap to twenty or twenty-five percent because the founders are leaving, but as you said, the cap is not where the value is. The value is in fixing the failure modes we found in diligence. Let us go through them.

**Nadia:** First finding. The end-of-service gratuity of three point one million dirhams is not provided for in the accounts.

**Sam:** That is known and quantified. It is a price issue. The state machine here is simple: we treat it as a debt-like item and deduct it in the price bridge. There is no need to negotiate an indemnity when you can just count it and subtract it from the cash paid at closing.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Next finding. VAT may have been under-declared in twenty-twenty-three and twenty-twenty-four. Our tax advisers estimate a fifty percent chance of an assessment of about two point four million dirhams, including penalties.

**Sam:** This is known, but uncertain in amount and whether it actually arises. This is the textbook use case for a specific indemnity. We pull it completely outside the general basket and cap, give it a time limit matching the tax authority's audit window, which is usually five years, and back it with the escrow.

**Nadia:** Correct. We size the escrow to cover that two point four million VAT exposure plus a safety margin, rather than just asking for a random percentage habit like fifteen percent.

**Sam:** Third finding. The largest customer, which accounts for twenty-eight percent of revenue, can terminate their contract on a change of control.

**Nadia:** This is fixable before closing. We make the customer's written consent or waiver a condition precedent. If the customer refuses, you have learned something fundamental that changes the price or kills the deal entirely. You never take a warranty for a twenty-eight percent revenue concentration. Money back later does not replace your core business.

**Sam:** Fourth finding. The trade licence does not list one of the activities the company actually carries on.

**Nadia:** Another fixable issue. We make a licence amendment a pre-closing step. It is cheap and easy for the seller to fix now, but incredibly awkward for the buyer to fix after closing when they are already operating in breach.

**Sam:** And the final finding: no litigation has been found, but the litigation search can only cover some emirates' courts. This is an unknown unknown.

**Nadia:** Which is exactly what general warranties are for. We ask for a broad litigation warranty. If there is anything out there, the disclosure letter will flush out whatever the sellers actually know.

**Sam:** Let us run the numbers in the simulator and do the expected value calculation. We have an agreed price of one hundred and eighty million dirhams, and a value of two hundred and five million. Transaction costs are two point five million.

**Nadia:** On the model landing, our expected kept risk is about eleven point four million dirhams. That brings our effective price to about one hundred and ninety-four million, against a value of two hundred and five million. So, on averages, the deal clears.

**Sam:** But wait, look under the hood of that eleven point four million kept risk. Ten point six million of it comes from just two items that the general cap and basket do not touch at all. The customer contract risk is a thirty percent chance of losing twenty-five million dirhams in value, which is seven point five million. And the unfunded gratuity is a guaranteed three point one million loss.

**Nadia:** Exactly. If that customer does leave, the buyer has paid about two hundred and eleven million dirhams for a business only worth two hundred and five million. They are underwater. And the simulator assumes we actually get the VAT indemnity. Without it, the VAT claim surfaces after any realistic time limit and adds another one point two million to the expected loss.

**Sam:** So the verdict is: pursue with conditions. If we get all four fixes, the gratuity deduction, the VAT indemnity, the customer consent, and the licence fix, our kept risk falls to about eight hundred thousand dirhams, and the effective price drops to about one hundred and eighty million.

**Nadia:** The trade here is simple. We accept the seller's pushback on the general limits, say, an eighteen-month limit and a cap under twenty percent, in return for the VAT indemnity and the escrow. But we walk away if the customer will not consent and the sellers will not re-price, or if the sellers refuse both the VAT indemnity and a price reduction for it.

**Sam:** Perfect. Let us switch gears to Project Dune. We act for the buyer again, a listed payments company that has publicly announced it will enter the Gulf this year. The target is a payments processor held through a DIFC holding company, operating in the UAE and Saudi Arabia.

**Nadia:** And the seller is a private equity fund in its final year, meaning it absolutely must return cash to its investors. But they are running an auction with four bidders. Management will keep ten percent of the equity and stay on.

**Sam:** Our bid is one hundred and twenty million US dollars, on a locked box basis. Our client values the business at one hundred and thirty-one million dollars. Transaction costs are two point two million.

**Nadia:** What does the auction draft SPA look like?

**Sam:** It is aggressive. The seller's liability for warranties is capped at exactly one US dollar. They have stapled a Warranty and Indemnity policy to the deal, meaning the buyer has to take it out. The policy has a retention of zero point five percent, a limit of twenty percent of the price, and the premium is about four hundred thousand dollars, which is included in our costs. Title warranties are capped at the purchase price. No escrow. And a twelve-month policy period for general warranties.

**Nadia:** And how is our client reacting to a one dollar liability cap?

**Sam:** They hate it. Their instinct is to demand a real seller cap of twenty percent and an escrow, because, quote, that is what we got on our last deal, unquote.

**Nadia:** Your client's last deal is client data, not market practice. Leverage is heavily with the seller here. The raw score is minus five, clipped to minus two. The fact that the fund must exit is the only factor in our favour, but the four-bidder auction neutralises it completely. Asking for a twenty percent cap against a private equity seller in an insured auction is asking for a term that almost no precedent supports. It costs you credibility and it might cost you the deal.

**Sam:** Right, the state machine for a private equity fund at the end of its life requires them to distribute the cash and shut down. They cannot leave a twenty percent liability hanging open for years. The seller is effectively a null pointer after closing. That is why the W and I insurance is there. The seller has a complete answer to any request for a real cap.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Do not fight the structure. Accept the one dollar cap and rely on the policy. But we need to look at the diligence findings, because W and I insurance does not cover everything.

**Sam:** We have two major findings. First, a customer-data incident eight months ago. Our advisers estimate a thirty-five percent chance it leads to a regulatory fine and claims totalling four million dollars.

**Nadia:** Because that incident is known, the W and I policy will absolutely exclude it. You cannot insure a house that is already on fire. Policy exclusions hand the risk straight back to the buyer. It needs a specific indemnity.

**Sam:** But a PE seller will resist any indemnity, because it traps their cash. So our realistic fallbacks are a very small, tightly defined escrow just for this one item, or we ask for a price reduction equal to the expected value of the loss.

**Nadia:** Right. Thirty-five percent of four million is one point four million dollars. We ask for a price cut of one to one point five million as our fallback.

**Sam:** Second finding. The Saudi licence is up for renewal in four months. Without it, about a third of the target's revenue stops. We model that as a ten percent chance of a fifteen million dollar loss.

**Nadia:** Fifteen million is too large to price in or indemnify, especially since the PE fund will have distributed the money by the time the renewal fails. We either make the renewal a condition to closing, or we accept the risk knowingly.

**Sam:** But in a four-bidder auction, adding a condition precedent massively weakens our bid. The seller wants certainty of execution. This is the real strategic decision for the client: bid safer but weaker, or bid stronger and take a ten percent chance of a massive failure mode.

**Nadia:** There is one more diligence point. The locked-box accounts are nine months old, and management fees to the fund were paid during that period.

**Sam:** Payments to the seller since the locked-box date are leakage. Unless they are explicitly listed as permitted leakage, they come back to us pound for pound. That is a price point, dealt with purely through the leakage clause.

**Nadia:** Let us run the numbers. Expected kept risk is one point four million for the data incident, plus one point five million for the licence risk, plus the zero point five percent retention share of any unknown claims. Our effective price comes out to about one hundred and twenty-five million dollars, against a value of one hundred and thirty-one million.

**Sam:** So the verdict is a zero: pursue, and do not fight the structure. The deal clears with room to spare. We spend our limited negotiation capital on two things: getting a specific indemnity or price cut for the data incident, and tightening the leakage definition. We walk only if the regulator signals the licence will not be renewed, or if the auction bids the price up beyond one hundred and twenty-six million dollars, where effective price meets value.

**Nadia:** Agreed. Now for the final deal, Project Mirage. We act for the buyer, a regional industrial group. A European multinational is carving out its Abu Dhabi industrial services unit. There is no auction, but there is also no urgency. If this sale fails, the seller will just keep running the unit.

**Sam:** That is the dominant leverage factor. The seller does not need to sell. The price is ninety-five million dirhams. Our client values the unit at one hundred and four million. Transaction costs are two million. No W and I insurance.

**Nadia:** The seller's draft is tough. A liability cap of fifteen percent, a tipping basket of one percent, a twelve-month survival period, and no escrow. More importantly, they are selling the site, quote, as is, unquote, with absolutely no environmental warranties or indemnity. And they want a twelve-month transitional services agreement at cost plus ten percent.

**Sam:** On paper, the leverage score nets to about plus two for the buyer. Bilateral deal, uninsured, serious diligence findings. The arithmetic alone would put the general terms at the buyer-friendly edge of the uninsured band, meaning a cap around seventeen percent and eighteen months survival.

**Nadia:** Override the arithmetic. Because the seller can simply walk away and keep the business, expect the general terms to land nearer the uninsured median and the seller's own draft. But honestly, the cap and basket do not matter here.

**Sam:** Why? Because neither of the two massive risks we found in diligence are covered by the general warranties anyway.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Finding one: soil contamination at the main site. Consultants estimate a forty percent chance the authority requires a clean-up within three years. The cost is somewhere between five million and forty million dirhams, with a central estimate of eighteen million.

**Sam:** Because it is disclosed, it is excluded from the general warranties. It needs a specific environmental indemnity, uncapped or with its own high cap and a long time limit. If the seller insists on selling it as is, our alternative is a price reduction of at least the expected cost, which is forty percent of eighteen million, or seven point two million dirhams. And honestly, for a risk-averse buyer, you need a bigger cut because of that forty million dirham tail risk.

**Nadia:** Finding two: sixty percent of the target's revenue comes from one government-entity contract. That contract cannot be transferred without consent, and the entity has hinted it may re-tender the work. Our client puts the chance of losing it at thirty-five percent, with a value loss of thirty million dirhams.

**Sam:** Again, a warranty does not help. If you lose sixty percent of your revenue, the business is broken. Consent must be a condition precedent. Without the contract, there is no business to buy.

**Nadia:** We also have a possible Emiratisation shortfall for past years, unquantified. We assume a fifty percent chance of a one point five million dirham loss. That needs a specific indemnity for pre-closing periods, and a corporate seller that still exists after closing is a perfectly good indemnifier.

**Sam:** And finally, the unit depends on the seller's group IT and payroll for at least a year. That is handled by the transitional services agreement. We need to negotiate the scope, the term, the exit, and frankly, push back on that cost plus ten percent pricing.

**Nadia:** Let us do the math on the kept risk. Forty percent of eighteen million for the clean-up, plus thirty-five percent of thirty million for the contract loss, plus fifty percent of one point five million for the Emiratisation shortfall.

**Sam:** That is seven point two million, plus ten point five million, plus seven hundred and fifty thousand. Expected kept risk is roughly eighteen point five million dirhams. The effective price is the ninety-five million base, plus eighteen point five million risk, plus two million in costs. That equals one hundred and fifteen point five million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Against a value of one hundred and four million. The deal is eleven point five million dirhams underwater, before even allowing for the forty million dirham environmental tail risk.

**Sam:** Verdict two: do not pursue on these terms. Re-price or walk. This is a classic bad trade. You could win the negotiation on the cap and basket, get your fifteen percent down to ten, and still destroy client capital.

**Nadia:** To rescue it, we need the government entity's written consent as a condition, which removes the largest risk. And we need an environmental indemnity, or a price cut of at least seven million dirhams. With those two fixes, the effective price falls to roughly ninety-eight million, and the deal works.

**Sam:** But we walk if the seller holds to the as-is stance and refuses the consent condition. This is where a good adviser saves the client from themselves.

**Nadia:** Let us wrap up with three takeaways. One: general warranty caps are a distraction if your major risks are known and quantified; deduct them from the price or get a specific indemnity.

**Sam:** Two: in a private equity auction with W and I insurance, do not fight the structure by demanding a high seller cap; spend your capital on specific exclusions and leakage.

**Nadia:** Three: always calculate the effective price by adding your expected kept risk to your purchase price. If it exceeds your valuation, re-price or walk away.
