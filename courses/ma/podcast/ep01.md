# Episode 1: What lawyers actually sell

**Sam:** Your team has reviewed a share sale contract and changed two percent of the text. The board still wants a partner at a law firm to sign the advice. What is the board paying for?

**Nadia:** Someone to explain the consequences of the other ninety-eight percent and to stand behind that judgment. There is no general rule that a lawyer has to write the contract.

**Sam:** I'm Sam. And this is Calling the Deal. Episode one: What lawyers actually sell.

**Nadia:** I'm Nadia. Anyone can read a statute or look up a standard clause. What the board needs is an answer about its particular deal.

**Sam:** Right, if I just needed information, I would read the statute. When I look at a law firm, I expect a definitive, straightforward answer. Can the buyer walk away if the target company loses its largest customer before the deal completes? I want a yes or no.

**Nadia:** And the law rarely answers that real question. It depends on the wording, the facts, and how a judge would read both years later. You are paying for judgment under uncertainty. The lawyer turns that ambiguity into a decision you can act on: likely or unlikely, expensive or cheap, fixable or not.

**Sam:** So if the firm gives negligent advice and my company suffers a loss as a result, we may have a claim against the firm?

**Nadia:** Yes. The lawyer has professional duties, and a firm's insurance may respond to a claim. The engagement terms and policy matter. The board wants to know who gave the advice, what they reviewed and whether the company can rely on it.

**Sam:** So judgment and accountability both count. What's the third thing?

**Nadia:** Knowing what is normal. A partner who has closed two hundred deals knows which demands the other side will accept at once, which they will fight, and which make you look like an amateur.

**Sam:** Information asymmetry sold by the hour. Most clients do a handful of deals in a career. You see the whole distribution.

**Nadia:** Precisely. But that signature liability we talked about is strictly bounded by jurisdiction. A jurisdiction is a territory with its own legal system and courts. England and Wales, New York, France, the UAE.

**Sam:** And your licence only covers specific jurisdictions where you passed the exams.

**Nadia:** Yes. If a lawyer admitted in England advises on Saudi law, they are outside the licence and usually outside the firm's insurance cover. This is why on a cross-border deal, the lead firm hires local counsel.

**Sam:** Local counsel being a firm admitted where the target operates, advising on local law and handling local filings. I always assumed having local lawyers on the email chains was just professional politeness.

**Nadia:** It is not politeness. It is how the accountability chain stays unbroken. My insurer will not cover me for guessing how a Saudi court operates.

**Sam:** Let's stress-test that. You are a New York-admitted partner in Dubai. You are leading my purchase of a Saudi company, and the contract is under English law. In a meeting, I ask you whether Saudi law lets the target dismiss forty staff without compensation. What do you say?

**Nadia:** I give a general indication flagged as such, and I get Saudi-qualified counsel to confirm before you rely on it.

**Sam:** Why not just answer from your experience? You've done several Saudi deals.

**Nadia:** Because experience makes the indication useful, but I am not admitted in Saudi Arabia, so I cannot stand behind the answer. If I am wrong, my insurance might not pay.

**Sam:** What if you just answer under English law, since the sale contract is governed by English law?

**Nadia:** That is the trap. The contract between buyer and seller can be governed by English law, but the target's relationship with its Saudi employees is governed by Saudi law regardless.

**Sam:** Got it. Let's talk about the UAE specifically, since I'm sitting in Dubai. How does the licence boundary work here?

**Nadia:** The UAE has three legal systems in one country. The onshore, mainland system, and two financial free zones, the DIFC in Dubai and the ADGM in Abu Dhabi. Each free zone has its own English-language courts.

**Sam:** So international firms here are full of lawyers admitted in England, New York or Paris. They are registered locally as legal consultants.

**Nadia:** Exactly. They draft English-law deal documents every day. But for a point of onshore UAE law, or to appear in the onshore Arabic-language courts, they bring in locally qualified lawyers. When a partner says 'we would need to check that with our onshore team', that is the licence boundary speaking.

**Sam:** Who would we actually instruct in England? What's the difference between a solicitor and a barrister?

**Nadia:** Yes. A solicitor advises clients directly, drafts documents and runs deals. Deal lawyers are solicitors. A barrister is a specialist advocate who argues in court and gives opinions on hard points of law, usually instructed by a solicitor. The United States has one profession, the attorney, admitted state by state.

**Sam:** Let's look at the anatomy of your firm. It's a partnership that sells hours. I want to read it like an org chart. The equity partner owns a share of the firm, wins clients, sets strategy, and signs off the advice.

**Nadia:** And handles the hardest negotiations. They are rewarded with a share of the firm's profit. There is no salary floor, they take full downside risk.

**Sam:** Then salaried partners. They have the title and authority, but they aren't owners. Fixed pay plus bonus. Then counsel, who are senior specialists not on the ownership track, often the deepest technical experts.

**Nadia:** Correct. Below them are the senior associates. They run the deal day to day, draft the main contract, and manage the juniors. They are your daily contact.

**Sam:** Associates draft supporting documents and review contracts. Trainees are apprentices, two years before qualifying. And paralegals handle document management and signature packs. They aren't qualified lawyers.

**Nadia:** The economics depend on staffing. Law firms call the ratio of fee-earning staff to equity partners leverage.

**Sam:** Let's run the numbers on that leverage. These are illustrative numbers, not market data, but let's build the model. You have a team with four associates. Each bills one thousand seven hundred hours a year at two thousand two hundred dirhams per hour.

**Nadia:** But clients actually pay ninety percent of what is billed.

**Sam:** Right. So billed per associate is one thousand seven hundred times two thousand two hundred, which is three million, seven hundred and forty thousand. Collected is that times zero point nine, so three million, three hundred and sixty-six thousand dirhams. If each associate costs the firm one point four million a year in salary and overhead...

**Nadia:** The surplus per associate is one million, nine hundred and sixty-six thousand. For four associates, that is seven million, eight hundred and sixty-four thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** And the partner's own hours are extra. That explains the incentives perfectly. A partner's scarce resource is client relationships, not drafting time. One relationship keeps five people billing.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Which brings us to how you pay for those hours. Three pricing models. The billable hour is time recorded in six-minute units, multiplied by an hourly rate per rank.

**Sam:** The client bears the overrun risk. The firm has no incentive to be quick.

**Nadia:** Then the fixed fee. One price for a defined scope. Here, the firm bears the overrun risk, so we define the scope tightly and list assumptions. For example, 'one round of comments', or 'no regulatory filing'. When an assumption fails, the price reopens.

**Sam:** And the capped fee. Hourly billing up to a ceiling. I keep the upside if the work is quick, you bear the overrun. I'm guessing firms resist caps or price them high.

**Nadia:** We do. Let us test your understanding of a capped fee. A firm agrees a fee cap of one point five million dirhams for a deal. The deal runs long.

**Sam:** Okay, hit me with the time recorded.

**Nadia:** Recorded time is two hundred partner hours at four thousand dirhams, and nine hundred associate hours at two thousand dirhams. How much recorded time does the firm write off?

**Sam:** Partner time is two hundred times four thousand, so eight hundred thousand. Associate time is nine hundred times two thousand, which is one point eight million. Total recorded is two point six million dirhams. I pay the cap, one point five million. So the write-off is two point six minus one point five. One point one million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Correct. The firm recovers about fifty-eight percent of its recorded time. Now, the behavioral question: under that cap, how will the firm's behavior change in the last weeks of the deal?

**Sam:** Every extra hour is now free to me and a pure cost to you. I expect you to push work to cheaper juniors, resist extra negotiation rounds, and point to any failed assumption to reopen the cap.

**Nadia:** That is exactly what happens. A cap protects your budget but reduces the firm's appetite to fight marginal points. Know which model you are on before asking for 'one more turn' of a document.

**Sam:** Let's switch to my side of the table. Large companies have a General Counsel, the GC, reporting to the CEO or board. They know the business and decide how much risk we accept. You supply the specialist capacity and the insured signature.

**Nadia:** Yes. On a deal, the GC is my real client contact and the person who translates legal risk into a business decision. But before my firm starts work, three things must happen. Conflicts check, engagement letter, and confidentiality.

**Sam:** Let's test conflicts. I want your firm to act for me in buying a company from SellerCo. But your firm currently advises SellerCo on an unrelated property lease. Can you just act for both of us to save cost?

**Nadia:** Absolutely not. A lawyer owes a duty of loyalty to each client. Negotiating against a current client is a conflict of interest even if the matters are unrelated.

**Sam:** So you drop SellerCo as a client to take my deal. That solves the conflict.

**Nadia:** No. Dropping a client to take a better mandate is restricted by many professional rules, and the firm still holds SellerCo's confidential information. I cannot act against a current client without their informed consent, and I may have to decline your mandate.

**Sam:** So a conflicts check can literally cost me my first-choice firm, and it takes a few days. Once we clear that, we sign the engagement letter.

**Nadia:** Which fixes the scope, the fee model, which group company is actually the client, and the limit on the firm's own liability. Anything outside the scope is not covered by the firm's signature.

**Sam:** How does a lawyer's duty of confidentiality differ from legal privilege? If something is confidential, can the other side still require us to produce it?

**Nadia:** Much stronger. It is the client's right to refuse to hand over certain lawyer-client communications to a court, a regulator or an opponent. Without it, clients would hide bad facts from their own lawyers, and advice would be worse.

**Sam:** In English-style systems it covers confidential communications made for the purpose of giving or getting legal advice. Let's look at a common mistake. During a deal, I email my CFO: 'The target's supply chain breaches its biggest customer's contract. We should close quickly before they notice.' I cc you, my external lawyer. Two years later the customer sues and demands internal emails. Is my email protected by privilege?

**Nadia:** Probably not. It is a business communication between two non-lawyers, not a request for or giving of legal advice.

**Sam:** But a lawyer was a recipient. And it concerns a legal risk. Plus, all deal communications are confidential under the engagement letter.

**Nadia:** Copying a lawyer on an email does not make it privileged. A cc does not change the purpose of the email. And the engagement letter binds the firm to secrecy, but it does not bind a court.

**Sam:** So what is the correct approach? How do I actually get the protection?

**Nadia:** Ask the lawyer the question directly. 'Please advise whether the supply chain breaches clause X.' And keep commentary out of side emails. Also, remember privilege can be waived if you forward the advice outside the company, like to a banker or co-investor.

**Sam:** Let's move to how a transactional lawyer actually thinks. Contentious work looks backward at disputes. Transactional work looks forward. You write rules for events that haven't happened yet.

**Nadia:** Yes, though the good transactional lawyers draft every clause while picturing the dispute it will have to survive. The core idea is risk allocation. A contract is a table: for each bad outcome that could occur, it names who pays.

**Sam:** If a regulator blocks the deal, who bears the wasted cost? And if the contract is silent, the loss stays where it falls. Usually on the buyer. Caveat emptor. Buyer beware.

**Nadia:** Exactly. A buyer who did not bargain for protection has none. Every clause is a move of one risk from one party's column to the other's, and the price is the balancing item.

**Sam:** You process each point in five steps. Issue, Rule, Application, Risk, Commercial recommendation. Let's say an associate's note to me says: 'Clause nine allows the lender to demand repayment if ownership changes. This is a risk.' You send that back. Why?

**Nadia:** Because it stops at application. It is missing the size and likelihood of the exposure, and what the client should do about it. It does not say how much debt is repayable, whether the lender would really call it, or the fix.

**Sam:** The fix being lender consent before completion, or refinancing at closing with the cost taken off the price. Clients don't buy explanation, they buy a decision. Let's run a numeric decision tree on risk allocation.

**Nadia:** Go ahead.

**Sam:** I estimate a fifteen percent chance that a key customer terminates, costing me twenty million dirhams. The seller offers a choice. Option A: reduce the price by two million dirhams and I keep the whole risk. Option B: no price cut, but the seller reimburses my loss up to a limit of ten million dirhams if the customer terminates.

**Nadia:** Let us find your expected uncovered loss under Option B. If the customer terminates, you lose twenty million and recover ten million. Ten million is uncovered. Fifteen percent of that is one point five million dirhams.

**Sam:** Compare Option A. Expected loss is fifteen percent of twenty million, which is three million dirhams. Less the certain two million saving on the price, so a net expected cost of one million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Option A is better on average by five hundred thousand dirhams. Option B is better in the bad state by eight million dirhams, because you are down ten million, not eighteen million. Which one do you pick?

**Sam:** Looking purely at expected value, I would pick A. But a good lawyer might recommend Option B. Why?

**Nadia:** Because a twenty million dirham loss could breach a bank covenant. Before I recommend the cheaper option on average, I want to know whether the business could survive that loss.

**Sam:** So good advice states both the expected value and the worst case, and asks what my primary commercial goal is. Plus, Option B has a hidden risk: a promise to reimburse is only worth the seller's ability to pay when the claim actually arrives.

**Nadia:** Precisely. Now, how do we know what terms we can actually get? We look at 'market'. Market practice is the range of terms that parties in comparable deals have recently agreed.

**Sam:** So when someone calls eighteen months a market claims period, I want to know which deals they're comparing. What makes that a defensible request here?

**Nadia:** Yes. Asking for it costs you nothing, and refusing it costs you credibility. And the evidence for market is precedent. In court work, precedent means a binding past judgment. In deal work, it means the firm's past signed documents and templates.

**Sam:** So your firm holds a private record of where each term landed and why. That institutional knowledge is your real asset. What about public surveys?

**Nadia:** They add a benchmark. For example, the CMS European M&A Study 2026 reports that the seller's total liability was capped below half of the purchase price in fifty-eight percent of the deals it covered. But there is no comprehensive public survey for the Gulf, so market in the UAE is practitioner judgment informed by private precedent.

**Sam:** Let's roleplay. I act for the buyer. The seller's lawyer says, 'A cap on our liability of ten percent of the price is market.' I reply, 'We disagree. Our client requires one hundred percent.'

**Nadia:** That flat counter-demand gives the other side nothing to engage with. Accepting on the word 'market' alone is how amateurs lose points, but just saying 'no' is weak. The useful challenge is to ask which sample.

**Sam:** So I say: 'Market for which deals? Size, seller type and whether insurance is in place all move it. On a deal like this one, what precedent are you pointing to?'

**Nadia:** Exactly. Because market varies by region, deal size, who the seller is and the year.

**Sam:** If precedent is the asset, what exactly do you need to capture to reproduce your sense of 'market'? It can't just be the final number in each past deal.

**Nadia:** It needs the commercial context. Who had leverage, auction or one-to-one talks, seller type, whether insurance covered the risk, what was traded for what, and the opening positions on each side.

**Sam:** Right, a ten percent cap in an insured auction sale and ten percent in an uninsured founder sale are completely different scenarios. Your judgment relies on the surrounding context, and those conditions aren't written in the contract.

**Nadia:** Exactly. A partner reviewing the draft can explain why a clause the team left untouched still works for this buyer. What happens when the parties want different wording?

**Sam:** The markup, or redline. Deletions struck through, insertions underlined. It tracks the changes. Each exchange is a turn, and who holds the pen on the first draft sets the baseline.

**Nadia:** And we track everything in an issues list. A table with one row per disagreement, showing each side's position and a proposed resolution. Senior people negotiate from it, juniors conform the draft.

**Sam:** Take an email negotiation. I want to signal agreement on a price of seventy-six million dirhams but keep it subject to a signed contract. Would I write 'without prejudice' at the top?

**Nadia:** No. 'Without prejudice' is a label for genuine attempts to settle a dispute. It means statements cannot be shown to a court as admissions. It belongs to disputes, not deal negotiation.

**Sam:** So I use 'subject to contract'.

**Nadia:** Yes. It means nothing here binds us until a formal contract is signed. It keeps a negotiation email from becoming an accidental deal, because emails absolutely can form contracts.

**Sam:** What about caveats? You write in a memo: 'On the documents we have reviewed, it is arguable that the buyer could terminate.' I read that as 'we can probably terminate.'

**Nadia:** Reading hedged language as a confident yes is the most common client error. Hedging is not vagueness. It follows from signature liability. 'Arguable' is deliberately weaker than 'likely'. It means a respectable argument exists, but I would not bet on it.

**Sam:** And 'on the documents we have reviewed' is a scope caveat. It's a limitation on the advice. Other documents could change the answer.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Read caveats as scope definitions. If you need a number, ask for one. 'Better or worse than even?' Most partners will answer.

**Sam:** Okay, final section. I'm in the room with you. You're selling a prediction on where terms will land. I want to understand your reasoning clearly. You tell me that sellers backed by investment funds usually refuse to leave money behind after the sale to cover claims. I say, 'That seems unfair to buyers.'

**Nadia:** Appeals to fairness miss the point that deal terms are bargained. You are not learning how the system works.

**Sam:** So instead I say: 'So when the buyer still needs cover, what usually fills the gap, and does that change where the liability limit lands?'

**Nadia:** Much better. You treat the statement as a commercial reality and ask how it affects other terms. That is exactly how landing points are predicted.

**Sam:** And the best question I can ask you about any sticking point: 'Is that a legal constraint or a negotiating position?'

**Nadia:** That is such a productive question. A legal constraint cannot be traded. A statute forbids it, a court will not enforce it. No amount of leverage moves it.

**Sam:** Whereas a negotiating position can be bought with price or a concession elsewhere.

**Nadia:** Yes. Lawyers on the other side often present positions as constraints. When a partner hears you ask that question, it is a sign you understand the game. It tells you which rows of the issues list are actually negotiable.

**Sam:** Alright, let's wrap it up. Three takeaways. First, you are paying for signature liability and judgment, bounded by jurisdiction. Second, a law firm's real asset is its precedent, which depends heavily on context, so always ask what moves the 'market' position. Third, separate legal constraints from negotiating positions early.

**Nadia:** Perfectly summarized.
