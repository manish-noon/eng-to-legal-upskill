# Episode 11: Conditions, the gap and the right to walk

**Sam:** You sign a contract to buy a company, but you don't own it yet. You are in a suspended state, waiting for regulators, or banks, or customers to say yes. In software, that is an asynchronous callback. If it hangs, the whole system locks up.

**Nadia:** And in private M and A, it is called the gap between signing and closing. Welcome to episode eleven: Conditions, the gap and the right to walk.

**Sam:** So we split signing and closing because there is an external dependency. A competition authority needs to clear the deal, a landlord has to consent, or a buyer needs to draw down loans.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The legal mechanism is a condition precedent, or CP. It is an 'if' guard on the obligation to close. You are bound by the contract, but the actual transfer of shares and money only executes if every guard evaluates to true.

**Sam:** If one evaluates to false, the deal dies?

**Nadia:** Yes, and nobody is in breach, assuming nobody caused the failure. That is the entire economics of this phase. Every condition is a potential exit door. If a condition depends on your own choices, you hold a free option to walk away.

**Sam:** So as a buyer, I want as many CPs as possible.

**Nadia:** And as a seller, I want to strip them down to the bare legal minimum.

**Sam:** Let's test that. Say we have two CPs: merger clearance, and a key customer consenting to the change of control. Six weeks later, the customer refuses. I am the buyer. I still want the deal. But the seller just got a better offer and says, 'The condition failed, deal is dead.' Who wins?

**Nadia:** You do. The customer condition protects the buyer, because you are the one who suffers if they leave. Therefore, only the buyer can waive it. You drop the condition and force the seller to close once the merger clearance arrives.

**Sam:** The seller can't use my guard rail to crash the car.

**Nadia:** Precisely. A seller cannot use a condition written for your benefit as their own exit. Though notice you cannot waive the merger clearance. That is a mandatory legal requirement. Nobody can waive it.

**Sam:** Okay, but what if the condition is in the hands of a third party, like that regulator? I can't just sit back and hope they say yes. What is the SLA on my effort?

**Nadia:** You have to promise an efforts standard. English law uses a ladder. 'Reasonable endeavours' means you take a reasonable course of action, but you don't have to sacrifice your own commercial interests. 'Best endeavours' means doing everything a prudent, determined party would do, even if it costs you.

**Sam:** US law does this too, right? Commercially reasonable efforts, reasonable best efforts.

**Nadia:** Yes, different words, same ladder idea. In both, courts are reluctant to draw sharp lines between the rungs, so good drafting lists specific steps instead of trusting the label.

**Sam:** So let's say I promise reasonable endeavours for merger clearance. The authority says, 'We will clear this, but you have to sell off one of your own profitable divisions.' I refuse. The clock runs out, I terminate. Am I in breach?

**Nadia:** Probably not. Reasonable endeavours does not normally force you to burn your own furniture. You did not breach the obligation, so you are allowed to terminate. Which is exactly why sellers hate reasonable endeavours for regulatory risk.

**Sam:** They want 'hell or high water'.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Hell or high water means the buyer must do whatever the authority demands to get clearance, including divesting businesses and accepting restrictions. It shifts the regulatory risk entirely to the buyer.

**Sam:** You mentioned the clock running out. That is the long-stop date, or outside date in the US. The absolute deadline for all CPs to clear.

**Nadia:** Right. Because a deal cannot hang open forever. The seller's business is frozen, and the buyer's capital is reserved.

**Sam:** Setting that date is pure arithmetic plus a buffer. Let's run the numbers. Say signing is day zero. I need twenty days to prep a merger filing. The authority has fourteen days to confirm it is complete. Then a ninety-day review clock starts. The SPA says closing happens fourteen days after the last CP is satisfied. If the draft long-stop date is day one hundred and twenty, how badly do we overshoot if the regulator uses their full time?

**Nadia:** Let's add it up. Twenty days to file, plus fourteen to complete, plus ninety days review. That puts clearance at day one hundred and twenty-four.

**Sam:** Already four days past the long-stop.

**Nadia:** And you still need fourteen days to close, pushing it to day one hundred and thirty-eight. So you overshoot by eighteen days. The deal fails because the CPs were not met by day one hundred and twenty. A sensible lawyer would push that long-stop to roughly day one hundred and fifty to one hundred and eighty, or build in an automatic extension while the review is pending.

**Sam:** This brings up deal certainty. Certainty has a dollar value. Sellers don't just compare headline prices.

**Nadia:** It is a currency. Sellers compare price multiplied by the probability of closing, minus the damage of a failed deal. Staff and customers know the company was for sale, and other bidders have left.

**Sam:** Let's put numbers on that. Bidder A offers five hundred and twenty million dirhams, but they need a financing condition. It is an eighty percent chance of closing. Bidder B offers five hundred million dirhams, but they have committed funds and no antitrust overlap. Ninety-nine percent certain.

**Nadia:** And assume if the deal fails, the business will only fetch three hundred and eighty million dirhams in a stale second auction.

**Sam:** Right. So Bidder A's expected value is point eight times five hundred and twenty, plus point two times the three hundred and eighty failure state. That is four hundred and ninety-two million. Bidder B is point nine nine times five hundred, plus point zero one times three hundred and eighty. Four hundred and ninety-eight point eight million.

**Nadia:** Bidder B wins, even though their headline price is twenty million less. That is how sellers and their bankers actually think. It is why sellers reflexively reject financing conditions. They hand the buyer an exit based on a banking relationship the seller cannot observe or control.

**Sam:** The standard answer is the buyer warrants they have the funds, shows signed commitment letters at signing, and stays liable if the money doesn't arrive. Okay, so during this gap, the seller still runs the company, but the economic value belongs to the buyer. Classic agency problem. The operator no longer bears the consequences.

**Nadia:** Hence gap covenants, or interim covenants. The seller promises to run the business in the ordinary course, consistent with past practice. And the buyer gets a list of consent rights: things the target cannot do without the buyer's approval.

**Sam:** No large capital expenditure, no new debt, no firing senior staff, no paying dividends.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The buyer wants a long list with low monetary thresholds to prevent value drain. The seller wants a short list with high thresholds so they can actually trade if the deal falls through.

**Sam:** The hard constraint on the buyer here is gun-jumping. Under a suspensory merger regime, the two companies have to remain independent competitors until cleared.

**Nadia:** Yes. Consent rights that protect the value of your investment are accepted. Rights that let the buyer steer day-to-day commercial behavior are not. Authorities fine heavily for it.

**Sam:** Let's test the line. I am buying a competitor. Clearance is pending. I ask for four gap covenants. One: target cannot borrow more than five million dirhams without my consent. Two: target cannot sell a factory. Three: target must send all customer quotations above one million dirhams to my sales director for approval before issue. Four: target must give me monthly management accounts. Which one gets us fined?

**Nadia:** Number three. Approving live customer quotes means you are setting a competitor's prices before clearance. That is illegal control of ordinary commercial conduct. Blocking a factory sale or massive new debt is standard value protection. Even monthly accounts are normally fine, provided granular competitive data is restricted to a clean team.

**Sam:** Let's talk about things going wrong in the gap. The business deteriorates. Who pays? It depends on the price mechanism.

**Nadia:** It does. Let's start with a locked box.

**Sam:** Okay, locked box date is December thirty-first. We sign on March first. We close on June thirtieth. Between March and June, the target makes an ordinary trading loss of six million dirhams. No MAC clause, warranties aren't repeated, and the seller followed every covenant. Who eats the six million?

**Nadia:** The buyer. In a locked box, economic risk passes to you at the locked box date. A trading loss is not leakage. Leakage is value extracted by the seller side.

**Sam:** So if they also paid an unpermitted dividend of two million dirhams, and paid one point five million in ordinary salaries that were listed as permitted leakage?

**Nadia:** You only recover the two million dirham dividend, pound for pound. The trading loss is your risk, and the permitted salaries were already priced in.

**Sam:** Okay, flip it to completion accounts. English style, no MAC, warranties only given at signing. During the gap, the target's biggest customer gives notice. They are twenty-five percent of revenue. Cash, debt, and working capital at closing are exactly on target. What is my position as the buyer?

**Nadia:** You must close at the agreed price.

**Sam:** But the business just lost a quarter of its revenue!

**Nadia:** Completion accounts adjust the balance sheet, not future earnings. A lost future revenue stream changes the enterprise value, but that headline price was fixed at signing. Without a specific MAC clause, there is no implied right to walk, however large the event.

**Sam:** That is a brutal trap. People assume completion accounts put all gap risk on the seller.

**Nadia:** They protect against cash burning or debt rising. If you cared about that specific customer, you needed a condition precedent, a repeated warranty, or a price mechanism tied directly to them.

**Sam:** What about warranties? Can't I say they breached the warranty that no key customer has given notice?

**Nadia:** Not if the warranties were only given at signing. It was true when given. Buyers want warranties repeated at closing, which the US calls a bring-down. Sellers resist because it makes them the insurer of events they don't control.

**Sam:** So the English-style compromise is repeating only fundamental warranties, like title to the shares. Or if business warranties are repeated, the buyer can only sue for damages later, not walk away.

**Nadia:** Exactly. You still have to close. It does nothing for a catastrophic event.

**Sam:** Which brings us to the ultimate walk right. The Material Adverse Change, or MAC clause. Or MAE, Material Adverse Effect.

**Nadia:** A MAC lets the buyer refuse to close if something seriously bad happens to the target in the gap. It has a standard three-layer structure.

**Sam:** Layer one is the trigger. An event materially adverse to the group as a whole. Layer two is the carve-outs. We exclude changes in general economic conditions, industry-wide changes, changes in law, war, pandemics. Systemic risk stays with the buyer.

**Nadia:** And layer three is the disproportionate-effect exception. Those carved-out events get pulled back in to the extent they hit this specific target harder than its peers. The allocation is: the buyer takes the risk of the world, the seller takes the risk of the company.

**Sam:** Let's apply it. The SPA has that exact MAC definition. After signing, a regional conflict cuts air travel. The target is an airline caterer. Its EBITDA falls thirty percent. Its listed peers fall between twenty-six and thirty-two percent. Can I walk?

**Nadia:** Run the layers. Trigger is arguably met. But the carve-out applies because it is war and an industry-wide change. And the exception fails because thirty percent sits right inside the peer range. They aren't disproportionately affected. You cannot walk.

**Sam:** If peers had only fallen ten percent, maybe the extra twenty points count.

**Nadia:** Maybe. MAC clauses are enforceable, but incredibly hard to trigger. Courts read 'material' as a durable hit to earning power from the viewpoint of a long-term acquirer, not just a bad quarter. And the buyer carries the burden of proof.

**Sam:** Which is why they are mostly a renegotiation lever. You threaten the MAC, and the price drops a little.

**Nadia:** Culturally, usage is very split. The CMS twenty twenty-six study found MAC clauses in only fourteen percent of European deals, against ninety-six percent in the US. European sellers treat certainty of closing as the default.

**Sam:** And the UAE?

**Nadia:** UAE law does not define a MAC. The drafting does all the work. The rule of thumb is: if you need protection against one specific event in the gap, write that event as a numbered condition. Do not rely on a general MAC you would struggle to prove.

**Sam:** If I can't trigger a MAC, and the CPs are met, I have to close. Termination rights in the gap are deliberately few.

**Nadia:** Yes. CPs not satisfied by the long-stop date, a MAC if agreed, sometimes a material breach found before closing, and failure to perform at closing. And remember, termination itself is not a remedy for losses.

**Sam:** That gap is filled by fees. A break fee paid by the seller, or a reverse break fee paid by the buyer. A reverse break fee prices my option to walk if I can't get clearance or financing.

**Nadia:** Let's size one. Price is four hundred million dirhams. The seller estimates a failed deal would cost them thirty million in lost momentum. Their advisers put the chance of clearance being refused at twenty percent. What reverse break fee percentage makes the seller whole if clearance fails?

**Sam:** The fee is only paid in the failure state, so it just needs to equal the thirty million loss. Thirty divided by four hundred is seven point five percent. The twenty percent probability is a distractor for sizing it.

**Nadia:** Correct. The probability only matters for how the buyer prices giving that promise in expectation today, which would be point two times thirty, or six million dirhams.

**Sam:** We have talked around merger control a lot. This is the dominant CP in private M and A.

**Nadia:** It is. The mechanics are similar globally: thresholds based on turnover or market share, phases of review, and whether the regime is mandatory and suspensory.

**Sam:** Let's look at the UAE regime. It's mandatory and suspensory since March thirty-first, twenty twenty-five.

**Nadia:** The thresholds are three hundred million dirhams of sales in the relevant UAE market, or a forty percent combined market share.

**Sam:** So if I have three hundred and fifty million in UAE sales of commercial kitchen equipment, and the target only has forty million. Combined share is about twenty-two percent. Do we file?

**Nadia:** Yes. The limbs are alternatives. You hit the turnover limb because the buyer alone exceeds three hundred million. You need a clearance CP, a long-stop date sized for it, and counsel to confirm the exact market definition.

**Sam:** The timeline is tight. The Ministry checks completeness within ten business days, then a ninety-day clock runs. Practice is reported at thirty to forty-five days. But here is the massive catch: if no decision issues in time, the deal is deemed rejected.

**Nadia:** Which is the reverse of most merger regimes, where silence means clearance.

**Sam:** Right. If the ninety days expire and the Ministry has said nothing, my CFO says, 'Great, silence is approval, let's close on Monday.'

**Nadia:** Do not close. In the UAE, no decision in time means deemed rejection. The CP is not satisfied. If you close, you are gun-jumping a suspensory regime.

**Sam:** Which means the SPA needs specific engineering for the UAE. The long-stop date needs room for a second filing if the clock runs out. And the efforts clause needs to force the buyer to file early and chase a decision, because delay alone kills the deal.

**Nadia:** Exactly. And a quick note: Saudi Arabia has its own regime and thresholds, so confirm with local counsel on any deal with Saudi sales. Foreign Direct Investment, or FDI screening, is a parallel review for sensitive businesses like defense or data. It asks a national-interest question, not a competition one.

**Sam:** Alright, we survive the gap. Regulators say yes. We hit closing day. Closing is a scripted exchange.

**Nadia:** Highly scripted. The SPA schedule lists deliverables. The seller delivers signed share transfer instruments, director resignations, and payoff letters from their banks.

**Sam:** Payoff letters being a lender's statement of the exact amount needed to repay the debt and release their security.

**Nadia:** Correct. The buyer delivers the closing payment, their own approvals, and maybe the W and I policy. The funds flow memo lists every single payment on the day: who pays whom, how much, to which account, in what sequence.

**Sam:** And a chunk of that money goes straight to the target's lenders, not the seller.

**Nadia:** Because the price was set cash-free and debt-free. The debt must disappear at closing. Paying the bank directly against that payoff letter removes the risk that the seller takes the money and you inherit a company still charged to a lender.

**Sam:** But who goes first? I don't want to wire hundreds of millions if they haven't transferred the shares.

**Nadia:** The SPA says all closing steps are deemed to happen simultaneously. None is effective unless all are. If one side fails to deliver, the other may postpone, close as far as practicable, or terminate.

**Sam:** Though 'simultaneous' requires engineering for an onshore UAE LLC. The transfer needs a notarized document and an updated trade license. The seller won't sign away the shares before funds are secured, and I won't pay before the transfer is registered.

**Nadia:** An escrow of the price, released on the updated license, is the common bridge.

**Sam:** One last edge case. Long-stop date is September thirtieth. Clearance hasn't arrived because the seller, in breach of covenant, took eleven weeks to supply data. On October first, the seller serves a termination notice to take a higher bid.

**Nadia:** Invalid. The standard carve-out says a party may not terminate if its own breach caused the condition to fail. Otherwise, a long-stop date just rewards delay. The buyer can still terminate, or seek to extend and claim damages.

**Sam:** Time to wrap. Three takeaways. One: Every condition precedent is a potential exit. If you control the condition, you hold a free option to walk.

**Nadia:** Two: Completion accounts do not protect you against a loss of future earning power in the gap. If a key customer leaves, the balance sheet looks fine, but the enterprise value drops. You need a condition or a MAC for that.

**Sam:** Three: The UAE merger regime treats regulatory silence as a rejection, not an approval. Size your long-stop date and your efforts clauses to handle the clock running out.

**Nadia:** Next time, we cover the exact mechanics of that escrow, and how to hold back the price.
