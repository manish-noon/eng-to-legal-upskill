# Episode 14: How terms actually move: negotiation dynamics

**Sam:** Imagine you advise a buyer. A founder has granted you eight weeks of exclusivity. There's no auction. The founder's lawyer offers to save time by drafting the share purchase agreement. Do you take the offer?

**Nadia:** I'd ask to send our own draft. In this episode, How Terms Actually Move, the question is what the first draft lets you decide before a negotiation even starts.

**Sam:** But whoever drafts bears the legal cost. If the seller wants to pay their lawyers to do the heavy lifting, why not let them? It is just a template anyway.

**Nadia:** Because the drafting cost is trivial against the value of setting every default. The party that produces the first draft has the first draft advantage. We call it holding the pen. The drafter sets the structure, the defined terms, and the default answer to every single question nobody raises.

**Sam:** So if the seller's draft gives us twelve months to bring a warranty claim, that's where the conversation starts. If I want longer, I'm the one who has to make the case.

**Nadia:** Exactly. If they want to change anything, they have to find the problem, justify the change, and spend goodwill on it. Every clause they miss stays as drafted. It is a massive anchoring effect with a labour cost attached.

**Sam:** So convention settles who drafts based on leverage. In an auction, the seller's lawyers draft the SPA, put it in the data room, and bidders submit a markup with their bid.

**Nadia:** Yes, because the seller scores price and markup together. A heavy markup lowers the value of a bid, so bidders restrain themselves. The seller is literally converting competitive tension into contract terms.

**Sam:** But in a bilateral deal, like our founder with exclusivity, the buyer drafts. They are paying, they are taking the risk, and they have the diligence findings to draft around. Accepting seller paper here means giving up the pen for absolutely zero competitive reason.

**Nadia:** Correct. And the excuse that 'everything gets negotiated anyway' is false in practice. Only what gets noticed and is worth a fight gets negotiated. Everything else defaults to the pen-holder.

**Sam:** Let us talk about that fight. The markup cycle. One side sends a draft, the other returns a redline with tracked changes and margin comments. Then the legal teams distil those open points into an issues list.

**Nadia:** Which exists because the decision-maker will not read one hundred and twenty pages of legal markup. The alternative is a page turn, going through the document line by line on a call. Large deals use both.

**Sam:** As a principal, I rely on that issues list. But it is useless to me if it is just a flat list of fifty clauses. A good one is tiered by how much I actually care.

**Nadia:** Three tiers. Must-have, Trade, and Give. A must-have means the deal does not work without it. It is tied to a walk-away. A trade has real value, but is exchangeable for something else. A give is something you asked for, is cheap to drop, and is useful as currency.

**Sam:** And the tiers come from the specific facts, not the clause type. Let us run a scenario. I am the buyer. We have warranty and indemnity insurance in place with a limit of thirty percent of the price. Diligence found a customs duty exposure of about nine million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Check the policy. For this exercise, the insurer has excluded that known customs issue.

**Sam:** Exactly. The seller is a fund that will distribute the proceeds and wind up within a year. We have three open issues. A, the seller's general warranty cap, where they offer one dirham. B, a specific indemnity for the customs exposure, backed by an escrow. C, a non-compete of three years against their offered two.

**Nadia:** How are you tiering those?

**Sam:** Where would I collect the customs bill? The fund plans to distribute the sale proceeds. Its promise to reimburse us will be hard to collect once the money has gone. For me the specific indemnity and its funded escrow go together. That's my must-have.

**Nadia:** Spot on. And the general cap of one dirham?

**Sam:** It is a give. The W and I insurance covers unknown breaches up to thirty percent. The seller's general cap matters very little to me now. I can concede it and collect credit. And the non-compete, issue C, is a trade or a give. A fund seller is not going to start a competing operational business anyway.

**Nadia:** That is the right answer. A give, B must-have, C trade or give. The common instinct is that the general liability cap is always the biggest issue. That is an error. Your facts dictate your tiers.

**Sam:** Why would a buyer ask for ten changes when only three decide whether it can sign?

**Nadia:** Because positions come in threes. Opening position, what you ask for first. Fallback, what you will accept when pushed. Walk-away, the point where you prefer no deal. The other side only sees your opening. Their job is to estimate your walk-away.

**Sam:** So padding is rational because concessions are expected. If I open at my true walk-away, I have nothing left to give. I just look obstructive.

**Nadia:** Yes, and padding hides which three things actually matter, protecting them. Plus, you do not know the other side's internal costs. Some of your seven padded items might cost them nothing, and they just accept them outright.

**Sam:** Take a fictional acquisition of a packaging business. The buyer wants historical invoices to check customer rebates after closing. It asks for access to all the seller's customer files. The seller crosses out the whole paragraph. I'd assumed records access would be an easy point.

**Nadia:** What's in those files? If they include prices for customers unrelated to the acquisition, the buyer's request asks the seller to disclose more than the rebate check needs. Find out which records are sensitive. That gives you something specific to use in your next draft.

**Sam:** So who decides how hard to push? The lawyer?

**Nadia:** The client sets the priorities. Ask which risks would stop it signing and which changes can wait. A client trying to close in one round may prefer a short markup. A client buying an unfamiliar business may want to resolve more points before it commits.

**Sam:** I will ask the obvious question then. If both sides know the openings are padded, why not skip the dance? Why not just exchange true walk-aways and see if they overlap?

**Nadia:** Because neither side can verify the other's true position. If you reveal your real walk-away, the other side will just treat it as your new opening, and push you below it. Padding is the equilibrium of a game with private information.

**Sam:** Neither side can verify the other's limit. What would help them spend less time trading exaggerated opening demands?

**Nadia:** By knowing the usual landing zones. They discount the padding quickly and spend time only where the overlap is genuinely unclear. That shared knowledge is what we mean when we say a term is 'market'.

**Sam:** What counts as a comparable? A seller says a cap was two million dollars on another deal. I still need to know what the buyer paid, whether the claim was insured, and which warranties the cap covered.

**Nadia:** Try three invented, otherwise comparable uninsured acquisitions. General warranty caps were eight, twelve and sixteen percent of price. Those are the three observations for this exercise. They give us something to work with while we ask how well each deal fits ours.

**Sam:** Suppose our price is twenty-five million dollars. I'm acting for the seller, with instructions to open at the lowest observed cap. What dollar amount do I put in the markup?

**Nadia:** Eight percent of twenty-five million dollars is two million dollars. That is the maximum general warranty liability we propose, subject to the exclusions in the draft. Compare like with like when you describe that offer to the buyer.

**Sam:** And the buyer can point to sixteen percent. On our price that is four million dollars. So a seller opening there would already be offering the highest cap in our little sample.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Twelve percent would be three million dollars. Nothing about those three observations requires anyone to meet in the middle. If the seller has a credible competing offer, or the buyer has found a serious uninsured exposure, the discussion can move.

**Sam:** Now take a published European study. The buyer's draft has the general warranty cap at one hundred percent of the purchase price. We are the seller. The CMS twenty twenty-six study reports that fifty-eight percent of deals had a cap below fifty percent of price.

**Nadia:** And forty-three percent of large deals were below ten percent. There is no W and I insurance. Your instruction is 'normal aggression, I want a deal'. Where do you open?

**Sam:** I definitely do not open at fifty percent just because it is the 'reasonable middle'. If I do, fifty percent becomes my best-case scenario.

**Nadia:** Correct.

**Sam:** But I also do not open at one dirham. Without insurance, opening at one dirham has zero market support and destroys my credibility on every other point. I would open around ten to fifteen percent. It is the seller's end of a defensible range, leaving room to concede towards a landing well below fifty percent.

**Nadia:** Exactly. You use the study to justify that 'below fifty percent is common'. But you have to be honest about what studies can and cannot do. CMS 2026 covers European deals. The ABA 2025 study covers US deals. SRS Acquiom covers deals where it acted as agent.

**Sam:** Every dataset has a bias. And as the course notes, there is no public dataset for GCC deal terms. So if a seller's lawyer on a UAE deal says, 'a deductible basket is market, look at the US data', how do you counter that?

**Nadia:** You attack the fit of the sample. You say, 'That is US practice. This is an English-style SPA, and the European data has eighty-seven percent of baskets tipping. Neither study covers the GCC, so let us talk about why a deductible is right for this specific deal.'

**Sam:** You neutralize the statistic and force them back to the deal facts. That is where leverage actually lives. Leverage is just the ability to make them concede because not agreeing costs them more than it costs you.

**Nadia:** It comes from identifiable sources. Your BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Time pressure, like a fund at the end of its life. Information shifts from diligence. And competitive tension.

**Sam:** Let us apply that. A family-owned UAE manufacturer is for sale. The seller's bank has not disclosed that the other two bidders dropped out. But the seller's main lender is demanding repayment within four months. Our diligence found their top customer contract expires in six months.

**Nadia:** And the seller's lawyer opens with a cap of five percent and a six-month time limit, claiming they have 'other interest'. Who has the leverage?

**Sam:** The buyer has massive leverage. The seller has a hard debt deadline, a ticking time bomb with their top customer, and no visible competing bid. The aggressive opening is just cheap padding. I would hold my position and see how fast their fallback appears to test that 'other interest' claim.

**Nadia:** That is the right read. Leverage is read from facts, not from tone. But be careful. Just because you know they are under time pressure does not mean you push every term to the extreme.

**Sam:** If the buyer has that much leverage, why wouldn't it press every open point?

**Nadia:** Three reasons. One, the family might be staying on as managers, and you do not want to start that relationship with a squeeze. Two, extreme terms might push them to find an alternative you do not know about. Three, a seller pressed too hard on contract terms recovers it elsewhere. They become uncooperative in the gap to closing, or take harder lines on the completion accounts.

**Sam:** I'd want to spend whatever leverage I have on the points that protect real money or keep the business running after closing.

**Nadia:** Precisely. Now, how do we actually move those points? Let us talk tactics. Repetition, carve-outs, and escalation.

**Sam:** Repetition makes sense. If you ask for a protection in one clause and they delete it, it is gone. If you bake it into a warranty, a repetition at closing, and a specific indemnity, they have to fight it on three fronts. If the warranty is disclosed against, the indemnity still survives.

**Nadia:** Check that each protection actually survives the limits elsewhere in the agreement. Now take a clause that the seller deleted and explained in the margin. Can you preserve the protection while dealing with their objection?

**Sam:** Back to our fictional packaging acquisition. I'd limit the request to the invoices needed for the rebate audit. Let the seller redact unrelated customer details. If an entry is disputed, an independent accountant bound to confidentiality can check the underlying figure.

**Nadia:** Now ask the seller to mark that proposal. If it accepts, the buyer can verify the rebates and the seller can protect other customers' information. If it still objects, ask who actually holds the invoices, what it can provide and when. The second comment tells you what to investigate.

**Sam:** And when you have exchanged the same arguments twice, you are spinning wheels. That is when you escalate to the principals. But principals do not settle issues one by one. They package them. Logrolling.

**Nadia:** Trading across issues. You concede what is cheap for you and valuable to them, in exchange for the reverse. This only works if you put a price on each issue before the call. Probability times amount.

**Sam:** Let us run a pricing scenario. Price is one hundred million dirhams. The seller offers a package: they will extend the warranty time limit from eighteen to twenty-four months, if the buyer accepts a cap of twenty percent of price, down from thirty percent.

**Nadia:** What is the expected value of that trade for the buyer?

**Sam:** Okay, the buyer estimates a ten percent chance of a claim arising in months eighteen to twenty-four, with an average recoverable loss of four million dirhams. Ten percent of four million is a gain of four hundred thousand dirhams. Now the cost. The buyer estimates a two percent chance of a loss above twenty million, where the average amount lost to the lower cap would be five million dirhams. Two percent of five million is a cost of one hundred thousand dirhams. Gain of four hundred, cost of one hundred. Net positive three hundred thousand dirhams. The buyer should take it.

**Nadia:** And the seller may value a lower cap because it reduces exposure after closing. How much the fund can distribute also depends on the escrow, its reserves and its own rules. A lower cap by itself doesn't release cash. Ask what happens to the proceeds under each package.

**Sam:** If they had just split each issue down the middle, that surplus would not exist. Here is another trade. The buyer wants a specific indemnity it values at two million dirhams. It has three possible concessions to offer.

**Nadia:** Option one: drop the request for seller-paid W and I premium. Costs the buyer six hundred thousand dirhams, worth six hundred thousand to the seller.

**Sam:** Option two: shorten the general warranty period from twenty-four to eighteen months with W and I in place. Costs the buyer fifty thousand dirhams, worth five hundred thousand to a fund seller who wants an earlier release.

**Nadia:** Option three: raise the purchase price by seven hundred thousand dirhams. Which do you offer first?

**Sam:** Option two. It has the highest ratio of value to the seller over cost to the buyer. Options one and three are just zero-sum cash transfers. Every dirham they gain, I lose. Option two gives them half a million in value for fifty thousand of my cost, because the insurer is standing behind the warranties anyway.

**Nadia:** Yes. Now, how do you read the last few rounds? In a negotiated acquisition the parties can take different positions on each clause. Even the wording neither side changes can matter when you advise the client.

**Sam:** So unmarked text is not just neutral background. It is a position one side took and the other chose not to contest.

**Nadia:** If a clause is untouched, record that the current drafts match and keep your attention on open points. An express reservation elsewhere can still qualify agreement. A deletion with a reason gives you an opening to propose a narrower clause.

**Sam:** Let us test that. My buyer-side draft has a tipping basket at zero point five percent of price, a cap at fifty percent, and a twenty-four month time limit. The seller's markup changes the cap to fifteen percent with the comment 'consistent with our fund's requirements'. They change twenty-four months to twelve with no comment. They leave the basket clause untouched.

**Nadia:** How do you read it?

**Sam:** The current drafts match on the basket. I'd ask what fund requirement sets the fifteen percent cap. The move to twelve months is less clear. Before trading it away, ask whether it follows a real distribution deadline or is an opening request.

**Nadia:** Exactly. A comment gives you a lead. Silence alone tells you very little about a walk-away. When only a few points remain, get the people with authority to trade those points on the same call.

**Sam:** And they trade them as a package. 'You take the cap, we take the time limit and the escrow.' Because if you do it one by one, someone has to lose the last issue. As a package, both sides can claim balance.

**Nadia:** Plus, principals can trade across categories that lawyers cannot. A contract term against price, or against a commercial relationship after closing. Only principals know their true walk-aways.

**Sam:** What I'd take to the next negotiation is this. First, who drafts matters because each unchallenged word can survive to signing. Second, have an opening, a fallback and a walk-away. Compare the offer with relevant deals and give your client a reason for the number you recommend.

**Nadia:** Third, put a value on each change before you trade it. Ask what your concession buys in return. Read a margin comment as a question you can test in the next draft, and confirm where both sides finally agreed.

**Sam:** Next time we'll use those facts to estimate where a disputed term might land.
