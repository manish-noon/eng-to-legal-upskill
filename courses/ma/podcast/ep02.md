# Episode 2: Contract mechanics: the physics under every deal

**Sam:** A seller emails you: I offer you my company for fifty million dirhams, open until Friday. On Wednesday, you reply: Agreed at fifty million, provided you stay on as CEO for two years. On Thursday, you change your mind and write: Forget the CEO point, I accept your original offer. The seller refuses to sign. Do you have a contract?

**Nadia:** No. The Wednesday reply was a counter-offer. Under English law, a counter-offer destroys the original offer. There was nothing left to accept on Thursday. Welcome to episode two: Contract mechanics, the physics under every deal.

**Sam:** The seller said the offer stayed open until Friday. But my Wednesday reply added a condition. Was I accepting, or making a new proposal?

**Nadia:** You made a counter-offer by making your acceptance conditional on the new term. A simple inquiry about whether the seller might stay on would be different. For an ordinary English-law contract you also need consideration. Each side gives something of value, even if the bargain is unequal.

**Sam:** Unless it is a deed.

**Nadia:** Right. A deed is a document signed with extra formality. It must state that it is a deed, and each individual's signature is witnessed. A deed binds without consideration. Lawyers use them where one side gives nothing back, like a guarantee by a parent company. And, crucially, we use them to get a longer time limit for claims later.

**Sam:** What if the signed document is silent? When can I rely on a term that neither party actually wrote down?

**Nadia:** English courts imply terms very sparingly. A court will only add a term if it is so obvious or so necessary that the contract does not work without it. Because of that, professionally drafted contracts try to be complete. They usually contain an entire agreement clause stating that the document is the whole deal, and that nothing said earlier forms part of it.

**Sam:** So I'd put a promised commercial benefit in the signed agreement instead of hoping a court will fill the gap. Once it's there, why does it matter whether it's a condition, a warranty or a representation?

**Nadia:** Because what you can do about a breach depends on the promise and the remedies the agreement permits. Breach of a condition can allow the innocent party to terminate and claim damages.

**Sam:** And a warranty?

**Nadia:** In deal contracts, a warranty is a statement of fact about the thing sold, like 'the Company owns its software'. If it is false, you get damages only. The contract stays alive.

**Sam:** What about representations? I see buyers asking sellers to 'represent and warrant' that the code is theirs. And I see sellers under English law crossing out 'represent'. Why the fight over one word?

**Nadia:** Because a warranty claim is a pure contract claim. You get damages, bounded by everything the contract says about limits. A representation is a statement of fact made before the contract that induces the other side to sign. If it is false, it is a misrepresentation. It opens the door to rescission.

**Sam:** Rescission. Meaning unwinding the contract as if it had never been made. Handing the company back.

**Nadia:** Yes. And returning the purchase price. Nobody wants a sold company handed back two years later. It also opens up a different measure of damages, putting the buyer where it would be had it never signed, rather than where it would be had the statement been true.

**Sam:** That sounds like a catastrophic outcome. But in the US, I see 'represents and warrants' all the time, paired together. It's standard boilerplate.

**Nadia:** Standard there, but little usually turns on the distinction in US practice. Protection there comes from a negotiated chapter on reimbursement, called indemnification, which covers breaches of every statement. English-style contracts split the work. We use warranties with a damages claim for the unknown, and specific indemnities for known problems.

**Sam:** Let's test that. A buyer discovers a year after buying a company that a statement in the contract about its software licences was false. The buyer wants to hand the company back and recover the price. The English-law contract says, 'The Seller warrants to the Buyer that the Warranties are true. The Buyer acknowledges it has not relied on any other representation, and its sole remedy shall be a claim for damages.' What happens?

**Nadia:** The drafting did its job. The buyer is limited to damages. Rescission belongs to misrepresentation, which that clause entirely shut out. Unless there was fraud, because fraud is always an exception. Nothing in that drafting makes the statement a condition, so they cannot terminate either.

**Sam:** So we are left with damages. The default remedy is expectation damages: money to put the innocent party where it would have been if the contract had been performed. Not a punishment, not a refund. But you apply four filters to cut that claim down.

**Nadia:** Yes. Proof of loss, causation, remoteness, and mitigation. Proof of loss requires actual evidence; a breach with no provable loss just gives nominal damages, a token sum. Causation means the breach must have actually caused the loss.

**Sam:** Remoteness is the interesting one. The Hadley and Baxendale rule from 1854.

**Nadia:** Still governing commercial risk today. It has two limbs. Limb one is loss arising naturally from the breach in the ordinary course of things. Limb two is unusual loss that both parties had in contemplation when they signed, because the special circumstances were communicated.

**Sam:** Like the mill shaft in the original case. A carrier delivers a mill shaft late. They pay for the ordinary delay, which is limb one. But they do not pay for the mill standing idle, because they were never told the mill had no spare. That was limb two, and it was too remote.

**Nadia:** Suppose a supplier delivers the retailer's new order system two months late. The retailer hires temporary staff to process orders. It also loses a uniquely lucrative government bid the supplier never knew about. Which losses could the retailer reasonably claim?

**Sam:** The temporary staffing is limb one. It arises naturally from the delay. The government contract is limb two. Because the vendor was never told about it, it is unusual loss not in their contemplation at signing. So only the temporary staff costs are recoverable.

**Nadia:** Correct. Causation is satisfied for both, but remoteness filters the second one out. The practical lesson is: if you depend on something unusual, say so in the contract.

**Sam:** What about when contracts exclude 'indirect or consequential loss'? Commercial teams often read that as 'lost profits'.

**Nadia:** Read the clause. English courts have often distinguished losses that arise in the ordinary course from losses that depend on special circumstances. Lost profit can fall into either category. Check whether the parties excluded lost profit separately and how they defined it.

**Sam:** Let's run the numbers on the final filter: mitigation. The innocent party must take reasonable steps to reduce its loss. If they don't, the avoidable part is not recoverable.

**Nadia:** Let's do the math. A supplier agrees to deliver a machine for two million dirhams, not yet paid. It fails to deliver. An identical machine was available elsewhere for two point six million dirhams, with a one-week lead time. The buyer waits, and its replacement machine arrives three weeks after the breach. The buyer loses profit of one hundred and fifty thousand dirhams for each week without a machine, a loss the supplier knew about. What do they recover?

**Sam:** The extra cost of the replacement is two point six million minus two million, so six hundred thousand dirhams. For the lost profit, three weeks were lost. But a reasonable buyer would have had the replacement in one week. So only one week survives mitigation. One times one hundred and fifty thousand. Total recovery is seven hundred and fifty thousand dirhams. The other three hundred thousand of lost profit was caused by the buyer's own delay.

**Nadia:** Perfect. Mitigation caps the bleeding. But mitigation applies to damages claims. Let's look at debt claims, which operate differently.

**Sam:** A debt claim is for a fixed sum payable when an event occurs. And this is what an indemnity tries to create. 'If X happens, pay me the amount of X'.

**Nadia:** Yes. Lawyers call an indemnity pound for pound, or dollar for dollar in the US. Remoteness and mitigation, the filters built for damages, generally do not apply in the same way. The claimant just proves the event and the amount.

**Sam:** Let's quantify why buyers fight for indemnities. After buying a company, you find it owes three million dirhams in pre-sale tax. Had you filed a voluntary disclosure promptly, the bill would have been two point two million, but you did not. Assume the shares fell in value by the exact tax amount. Route one is a warranty claim for damages. Route two is an indemnity for 'an amount equal to any pre-sale tax liability', treated as a pure debt claim. What is the difference?

**Nadia:** Under Route one, mitigation applies. You recover the loss a reasonable buyer would have suffered, which is two point two million dirhams. Route two is a claim for the stated sum: three million dirhams. The difference is eight hundred thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** Eight hundred thousand dirhams just from the label on the promise. So a smart seller's lawyer will manually add 'the Buyer shall take reasonable steps to mitigate' directly into the indemnity.

**Nadia:** They will certainly try, though buyers fiercely resist it. We use indemnities for identified, quantifiable risks. We use warranties for the unknown.

**Sam:** Is an indemnity always better for the buyer, though? It sounds like a bad trade if the loss cascades.

**Nadia:** It can be a bad trade. Imagine you paid eight times annual profit for a company. The seller warranted that annual profit was five million dirhams. True recurring profit is four million because one contract had already been lost. An indemnity in the contract covers 'any loss suffered by the Company in the first year from the loss of that contract', which is one million dirhams.

**Sam:** Okay, the indemnity pays exactly one million. But warranty damages put me where I would be if the statement were true. Value as warranted is eight times five million: forty million dirhams. Actual value is eight times four million: thirty-two million. Damages are eight million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The warranty damages claim is worth seven million dirhams more than the indemnity. An indemnity reimburses a specific cost, while a warranty claim can capture lost value at the price multiple.

**Sam:** Let's move off money. There are non-money remedies. Specific performance, which is a court order to do what was promised, and injunctions, to stop doing something. Common-law courts only grant them when damages are inadequate.

**Nadia:** Yes, but shares in a private company are considered unique. So a court will usually order a seller to complete a signed sale.

**Sam:** So if a buyer has signed but not yet paid, and the seller gets a higher offer and refuses to complete. Why is specific performance so critical there?

**Nadia:** Because damages would just be the difference between the contract price and the true value. The buyer has to prove that value, endure years of litigation, and hope the seller hasn't spent the money. Specific performance delivers the company itself.

**Sam:** It also changes the incentives before the breach. If the seller knows a court will force the sale, the higher offer is useless to them. The remedy prevents the breach from happening.

**Nadia:** Precisely. Now, parties can also fix the consequences of breach in advance using liquidated damages, or LDs. Typically a daily rate for delay. The claimant does not need to prove its loss.

**Sam:** But English law will not enforce a penalty. The test comes from Cavendish and Makdessi in 2015, which was actually a company sale dispute. A clause is a penalty if the agreed sum is out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in having the contract performed.

**Nadia:** Yes, though commercial parties of equal strength rarely succeed in calling a negotiated clause a penalty. More often, they argue over liability caps.

**Sam:** Let's test an LD clause against a cap. An English-law contract with a price of four million dirhams sets liquidated damages for delay at forty thousand dirhams per day, capped at ten percent of the price. Delivery is twelve days late. The customer's real loss from the delay is nine hundred thousand dirhams. What do they recover?

**Nadia:** The LDs are twelve times forty thousand, which is four hundred and eighty thousand. The cap is ten percent of four million, which is four hundred thousand. The lower figure applies. Four hundred thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** And the real loss of nine hundred thousand?

**Nadia:** Irrelevant in both directions. LDs replace the damages claim for that breach. They are a floor with no need to prove loss, but they are also a ceiling.

**Sam:** What about time limits? Statute sets a limitation period for claims. In England, it is six years for an ordinary contract, and twelve years for a deed.

**Nadia:** And sellers do not want exposure for six or twelve years. So deal contracts replace the statutory period with a much shorter contractual one. The CMS European M&A Study 2026 reports that only thirty-two percent of deals allowed warranty claims for longer than twenty-four months.

**Sam:** So what if the clause is missing? A seller sold her company under a deed governed by English law. The deed says nothing about time limits. Four years later, the buyer finds a breach of warranty from signing. The seller says the claim is too late because two years is market practice. Who wins?

**Nadia:** The buyer wins. Short claim periods exist only because contracts create them. Market practice is not law. With no contractual time limit, the statute governs, and for a deed, it is twelve years. This is why a seller's lawyer never lets a time-limit clause be deleted.

**Sam:** Let's talk about the dispute resolution framework. Governing law, jurisdiction, and arbitration. Governing law is which system's rules interpret the contract. Jurisdiction is which courts hear the disputes.

**Nadia:** Yes, and a French court can apply English law, though slowly. Arbitration is an agreement to send disputes to a private tribunal in place of any court. And the choice between common law and civil law is critical here.

**Sam:** Common law, like England, DIFC, or ADGM, runs on freedom of contract. The court enforces what sophisticated parties wrote. Civil law, like onshore UAE, is based on a code. It builds in general duties, like good faith, that you cannot always remove.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Onshore UAE courts may adjust agreed compensation to match actual loss. Some clauses common in English-style contracts, like very short notification periods, might not work as drafted. That is why most serious UAE deals use English, DIFC, or ADGM law, even when the target is onshore.

**Sam:** Wait, if my English-law contract sets liquidated damages of five million dirhams, and I end up in a civil-law court, what goes wrong?

**Nadia:** Two things. If your actual loss is one million, the judge may cut the five million down, hurting the claimant who thought they had certainty. If actual loss is nine million, some civil-law systems let the judge increase the sum, hurting the party in breach who thought five million was their ceiling. The forum brings the need to prove loss right back.

**Sam:** Which destroys the whole point of the LD clause. Choice of forum is risk allocation. So why does arbitration dominate? The CMS 2026 study reports an arbitration clause in forty-four percent of European deals. In cross-border deals, it feels mandatory.

**Nadia:** Enforcement. Under the New York Convention of 1958, courts must enforce foreign arbitral awards with very limited grounds to refuse. Most trading nations, including the UAE, have joined it. No treaty of similar reach exists for court judgments.

**Sam:** So if I am buying a company from a seller whose assets are in a slow jurisdiction that has joined the Convention, I don't want English courts. An English judgment is excellent paper, but I might not be able to collect. I want English governing law with arbitration seated in a neutral Convention jurisdiction.

**Nadia:** Correct. A judgment you cannot enforce where the loser keeps its assets is worth nothing.

**Sam:** Let's finish with drafting vocabulary. The wording that moves money. 'Best endeavours' versus 'reasonable endeavours'. Both are promises to try, not to achieve.

**Nadia:** Under English law, best endeavours is heavy. You must take every step a determined, prudent person acting in its own interest would take, even at some cost. Reasonable endeavours is lighter. You take a reasonable course, with no duty to sacrifice your own commercial interests.

**Sam:** So if a regulator will only approve a sale if the seller divests a profitable side business, 'best endeavours' is the wording most likely to force that sacrifice?

**Nadia:** Yes, because it can require a party to bear real cost. But it doesn't require ruin, so it would still be argued. Good drafting spells it out: 'including by offering divestments of businesses with revenue below X'. Note that US contracts use 'best efforts' and 'commercially reasonable efforts', but US courts don't apply a consistent ranking, so US drafters often define the standard in the contract.

**Sam:** What about 'joint and several'? Three sellers hold sixty percent, thirty percent, and ten percent of a company. They sell it for one hundred million dirhams. They give warranties on a joint and several basis. The buyer has a valid claim of ten million dirhams. The sixty and thirty percent sellers have moved abroad. What can the buyer claim from the ten percent seller?

**Nadia:** Ten million dirhams. Joint and several liability lets the buyer recover the whole claim from any one seller. Which here is the ten percent seller's entire share of the price. They must then chase the others themselves.

**Sam:** Under several liability, it would just be ten percent of ten million, so one million dirhams. That is why minority sellers must insist on several liability, capped at the money they actually received.

**Nadia:** Exactly. One last phrase: 'Notwithstanding'. It means this clause overrides the one it names. 'Subject to' means it yields.

**Sam:** So if Clause 8 caps total liability at twenty million dirhams. And Clause 12 says, 'Notwithstanding any other provision, the Seller shall indemnify the Buyer for all Losses from the Tax Audit.' The tax audit costs thirty-five million. The buyer recovers thirty-five million.

**Nadia:** Yes. 'Notwithstanding' puts Clause 12 above Clause 8. The cap does not apply. A seller's lawyer would rewrite Clause 12 to begin 'Subject to Clause 8'.

**Sam:** I have to ask. Why do lawyers write eighty-page contracts when the commercial deal fits on one page?

**Nadia:** Because in a common-law system, the court will not rescue you with implied terms or good faith. Every bad outcome you have not allocated stays exactly where it falls. Each page is a row in the risk allocation table: who pays if X happens. The length is simply the cost of freedom of contract. A civil-law contract can be shorter because the code supplies defaults, but at the price of less control over what those defaults are.

**Sam:** Three things to take away. First, read the promise alongside the remedy. A false warranty can support damages. A misrepresentation may also support rescission. An indemnity pays the losses the parties agreed to cover, on the conditions they wrote.

**Nadia:** Two: damages are limited by remoteness and mitigation. If your business depends on something unusual, state it in the contract, or you cannot recover for it.

**Sam:** Three: remedies change pre-breach behaviour. Specific performance prevents the seller from entertaining higher offers, and arbitration ensures you can actually collect the money.

**Nadia:** Next time we'll look at who owns a business, who can sell it and how the choice between shares and assets changes the deal.
