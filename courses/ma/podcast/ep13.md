# Episode 13: The UAE and GCC overlay

**Sam:** You have a German buyer acquiring an onshore Dubai company from local founders. The buyer insists on a strict English-law SPA so the limitation regime works exactly as drafted. But to actually get the shares, you have to stand in front of a local notary and sign a two-page Arabic transfer document. If that short document contradicts your heavily negotiated SPA, which one actually dictates the deal?

**Nadia:** The Arabic document moves the shares. The SPA decides whether the buyer can successfully sue the founders when things go wrong. Welcome to 'The UAE and GCC overlay'.

**Sam:** I am Sam, and this is Calling the Deal. Today we are looking at how the private M and A mechanisms we have built so far change when your target is located in the UAE or the wider Gulf.

**Nadia:** The core friction on these deals is that the UAE contains three separate legal systems. Which one you are operating in depends entirely on where a company is registered, not on where its physical office is.

**Sam:** Right, it is a jurisdictional issue. First, we have onshore, or the mainland. That operates under UAE civil law with local and federal courts working in Arabic. Then we have two financial free zones: the DIFC in Dubai, and the ADGM in Abu Dhabi.

**Nadia:** And those free zones are entirely different legal environments. The DIFC uses its own common-law statutes and has English-language courts. The ADGM applies English common law directly, also with English-language courts.

**Sam:** So the jurisdiction of the target is a fixed legal reality. It fixes the mechanics of transferring title. If I am buying an onshore LLC, the shares only move by the onshore process, no matter what my SPA says.

**Nadia:** Exactly. But the law governing the SPA is a choice. You can absolutely sell an onshore company using an English-law contract. And where your disputes go is also a choice, and it is the one that makes the others real. A court decision is only worth what you can enforce where the losing party's assets are.

**Sam:** Which explains the typical structure we see in the reference material. You use an SPA governed by English, DIFC, or ADGM law. You choose arbitration, maybe under DIAC, ICC, or LCIA rules. But if the target is onshore, you pair that massive SPA with a short-form Arabic transfer document just for the notary.

**Nadia:** Yes, and you have to be careful there. Two documents now describe one sale. If your short Arabic instrument states a price or terms that differ from the SPA, you have created a conflict in the one document a local court will read first. The SPA must explicitly state that it prevails between the parties, and that the transfer instrument exists only to effect the transfer.

**Sam:** Why route the disputes to arbitration in the DIFC rather than just using onshore courts? The reference notes that DIFC-seated awards can be enforced onshore through what is called the conduit route.

**Nadia:** That is the mechanism of taking the award through the DIFC Courts and from there to onshore enforcement. Though you should always ask local counsel how that route is working in current practice. The goal is to get an enforceable result without exposing your English-style SPA to onshore courts directly.

**Sam:** One diligence point on arbitration centres: the reference flags that the DIFC-LCIA centre was abolished in September 2021. Its cases moved to DIAC. But legacy contracts naming it still turn up.

**Nadia:** They do. If you see the abolished DIFC-LCIA in a target's key commercial contract, or someone hands you a draft SPA with it, flag it for counsel immediately. You do not want to buy a built-in jurisdiction fight.

**Sam:** Let's stress-test what happens if an onshore court actually ends up hearing an SPA dispute. Say we have a draft SPA for an onshore target, governed by onshore UAE law. It has a reverse break fee fixed at fifteen million dirhams. It has a twenty-day claim notification deadline. And it has a clause letting only the buyer choose between court and arbitration.

**Nadia:** Under English law, those are standard commercial terms that are generally enforced as written. Under UAE civil law, you have three distinct surprises waiting for you. First, onshore courts may adjust a sum the parties fixed in advance as compensation. That fifteen million dirham break fee is less certain.

**Sam:** So liquidated damages or fixed leakage payments are vulnerable. What about the notification deadline?

**Nadia:** The short notice periods we discussed earlier need to work under the applicable law. Ask local counsel which contractual deadlines a court will enforce and how good faith could affect the answer.

**Sam:** And the asymmetric dispute clause, where only the buyer gets to choose the forum?

**Nadia:** Those have been held invalid onshore. This is why your working rule as a principal is to verify rather than assume. You must ask local counsel whether a specific cap or mechanism will hold if an onshore court hears it, rather than stating from general knowledge that it will.

**Sam:** Let's move to corporate mechanics. Foreign ownership of onshore companies used to be heavily restricted. But under Federal Decree-Law Number 32 of 2021, one hundred percent foreign ownership is now the default, except for activities with strategic impact under a 2021 Cabinet Resolution.

**Nadia:** Which means you check the target's licensed activities early in diligence. If any activity is on the restricted list, your deal structure has to change.

**Sam:** Take an onshore limited liability company. Its memorandum names the shareholders, and the licence records ownership details. I need the local adviser to confirm the notarised transfer, the amended memorandum, the register update and the evidence that proves each step.

**Nadia:** Yes, and the critical point is that the transfer only binds third parties once it is registered with the updated licence. Until then, the buyer's title is exposed.

**Sam:** Now I see the practical disagreement. The buyer wants evidence of registration before releasing the price. The seller wants secure funds before authorising transfer. How can the closing instructions satisfy both?

**Nadia:** A common answer sits in the closing mechanics clause: the price is held in escrow and released when the updated licence is actually issued. Ask counsel how they are handling that sequence in current practice.

**Sam:** What about pre-emption rights? Say I am buying seventy percent of an onshore LLC from the majority owner, and a third party holds the other thirty percent.

**Nadia:** That third party likely has a statutory right to buy the shares before they are sold to an outsider. If those rights are not waived or run through before the transfer, your transfer can be attacked. Getting that waiver is a vital condition precedent or signing deliverable.

**Sam:** Historically, founders set up a DIFC or ADGM holding company on top of their onshore business so they could issue preferred shares and use common-law drag and tag rights. But the reference covers Federal Decree-Law Number 20 of 2025. It introduced multiple share classes, statutory drag and tag rights, and re-domiciliation onshore.

**Nadia:** Yes, drag-along lets a majority force a minority to sell, and tag-along lets a minority join a sale. But the reference records a conflict on the effective date of those amendments. One firm, Cleary, notes October fifteenth, 2025. Another, Clyde and Co, says November fifteenth, 2025. You must confirm that before relying on it.

**Sam:** The real question is whether clients actually use these new tools onshore now, or if they still just put a DIFC holding company on top to keep the investor-level rights in a common-law entity.

**Nadia:** That is the live practical question for your counsel. Let's look at merger control. The UAE regime sits under Federal Decree-Law Number 36 of 2023, with thresholds under Cabinet Resolution Number 3 of 2025, effective March thirty-first, 2025.

**Sam:** The trigger is an economic concentration where sales in the relevant UAE market reach three hundred million dirhams, or the combined market share reaches forty percent. Either limb is enough. And it is mandatory and suspensory.

**Nadia:** Correct. You must file at least ninety days before completion. The Executive Regulations, in force from July thirtieth, 2026, mandate a completeness check within ten business days, followed by a ninety-day clock. Practice is reported at about thirty to forty-five days.

**Sam:** Let's run a worked example. A buyer sells two hundred and sixty million dirhams a year in the UAE market for packaged dairy. The target sells seventy million in the same market. Their combined share is thirty-one percent.

**Nadia:** Thirty-one percent is below forty, so the share limb is not triggered. For the turnover limb, if we read it on a combined basis, two hundred and sixty plus seventy is three hundred and thirty million dirhams. That is above three hundred million, so it triggers.

**Sam:** But if applied per party, neither party alone reaches three hundred million. The reference notes this is exactly the open point raised by Baker McKenzie. There is also a debate, noted by Eversheds, on how far the regime reaches deals between foreign companies.

**Nadia:** Because it is suspensory, you handle an unresolved filing question by assuming the filing is required until counsel confirms otherwise. The downside is asymmetric. The filing fee, under Cabinet Resolution Number 105 of 2026, is zero point zero two percent of aggregate turnover, capped at one hundred and fifty thousand dirhams.

**Sam:** So if aggregate turnover is nine hundred million dirhams, zero point zero two percent is one hundred and eighty thousand. The cap binds, so the fee is just one hundred and fifty thousand dirhams. The cost of filing is small. The cost of closing without clearance is massive. Penalties of up to ten percent of relevant UAE revenue are cited.

**Nadia:** And remember the deemed rejection rule. If the authority makes no decision within the review period, it is treated as refused. Also, third parties can object during the process.

**Sam:** Beyond competition, we might need sector regulator approvals. The Central Bank for finance, the Securities and Commodities Authority for listed companies. The reference mentions SCA Decision Number 18 RM of 2017, which sets a mandatory offer threshold at thirty percent plus one share, and a squeeze-out at ninety percent.

**Nadia:** Yes, though DIFC companies fall under the DFSA Takeover Rules. We focus on private deals, but you need to know those public thresholds exist. Now, let's talk about employment. The UAE has no automatic transfer rules for employees, no equivalent to TUPE in the UK.

**Sam:** In a share deal, the employer stays the same. But in an asset deal, each employee must be terminated by the seller and rehired by the buyer. And termination crystallises the end-of-service gratuity.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Gratuity is a lump sum owed on termination. The reference formula is twenty-one days of basic pay per year for the first five years, then thirty days of basic pay per year after that, capped at two years' pay.

**Sam:** Let's calculate it. Basic pay is fifteen thousand dirhams a month, and the employee has eight years of service. Taking a day's pay as monthly basic divided by thirty. Daily pay is five hundred dirhams.

**Nadia:** For the first five years: twenty-one days, times five hundred dirhams, times five years. That is fifty-two thousand, five hundred dirhams.

**Sam:** For years six to eight: thirty days, times five hundred dirhams, times three years. That is forty-five thousand dirhams. Total is ninety-seven thousand, five hundred dirhams. The cap would be twenty-four months at fifteen thousand, which is three hundred and sixty thousand, so we are well under the cap.

**Nadia:** Now run it for a long-serving manager. Basic pay of ten thousand dirhams a month, with thirty years of service.

**Sam:** Daily pay is three hundred and thirty-three point three three dirhams. First five years is thirty-five thousand. The next twenty-five years at thirty days a year is two hundred and fifty thousand. Uncapped total is two hundred and eighty-five thousand dirhams.

**Nadia:** But the cap is twenty-four months of basic pay. Twenty-four times ten thousand is two hundred and forty thousand dirhams. The cap binds. In a diligence model, forgetting the cap overstates the liability for long-serving staff.

**Sam:** This gratuity is debt-like. It grows every month, it is owed to creditors, and it is usually unfunded. In the reference's price bridge, accrued unfunded gratuity of two point five million dirhams is deducted from enterprise value alongside bank debt.

**Nadia:** Sellers will argue it is just an ordinary operating liability that never falls due all at once. The reference calls this a classic UAE negotiation point. The fight happens in the definition of 'Debt' in your SPA.

**Sam:** What about Emiratisation? Private employers must employ a minimum share of UAE nationals. For employers with fifty or more staff, they must add two percentage points a year, reaching ten percent by December thirty-first, 2026. And for fourteen specific sectors, employers with twenty to forty-nine staff must have at least two Emiratis.

**Nadia:** The penalty for missing the quota is steep. The reference reports financial contributions for unfilled posts at ten thousand dirhams a month per post, based on secondary sources, though some say nine thousand.

**Sam:** If diligence shows the target was five Emirati posts short for the whole of last year and paid nothing, we calculate five posts times ten thousand dirhams times twelve months. That is a six hundred thousand dirham exposure. Or five hundred and forty thousand at the lower rate. Because it is a known issue, it goes into a specific indemnity or a price deduction, not general warranties.

**Nadia:** Correct. Let's look at W and I insurance in the region. The market context is fascinating. PwC TransAct 2026 reports that Middle East deal volumes rose thirty-three percent in 2025. The UAE led with two hundred and seven deals.

**Sam:** And massive capital deployment. Middle East sovereign wealth funds invested one hundred and twenty-seven billion US dollars globally, with Mubadala at thirty-three point seven billion.

**Nadia:** A and O Shearman puts GCC deal value at seventy-two point seven billion US dollars to December first, 2025, with the UAE taking sixty point four billion. In the first half of 2026, the UAE accounted for eighty point nine percent of disclosed GCC value, which was fifty-two point six billion dollars. But Q1 2026 volumes fell to thirty-three deals from fifty-two.

**Sam:** Read that as leverage data. Abundant sovereign capital favours sellers. A volume dip with a conflict in the background favours buyers who can still close. And what about the insurance pricing? Marsh Q1 2026 puts Middle East premiums at zero point seven to one point eight percent of the policy limit. That is historic-low pricing, cheaper than the US.

**Nadia:** It is cheap, but the mechanism matters more than the numbers. Sidley's May 2026 report notes that after the 2026 regional conflict, policies carry strict exclusions for war, civil unrest, and sanctions. Iran is uninsurable and Iraq is largely off-limits.

**Sam:** So if a UAE logistics target earns fifteen percent of revenue from routes into an affected country, and the seller offers a warranty cap of one dirham and tells me to rely on W and I, that is a bad trade. The policy excludes sanctions and war. Nobody is standing behind those warranties.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The buyer must get a specific indemnity or a price reduction from the seller for those excluded risks, or accept the risk knowingly. The gap in coverage dictates where the remaining liability falls.

**Sam:** Finally, the rest of the GCC. The reference explicitly says this course does not cover Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, or Oman. They have their own company laws and competition regimes.

**Nadia:** But Saudi Arabia matters immensely because many UAE targets sell into it. And sales into a country may be enough to trigger its merger control regime, even if the target has no subsidiary there.

**Sam:** Which means in week one, you ask local counsel: does this deal trigger a filing in KSA? What are the thresholds? Is it suspensory? Because if it is, your long-stop date must accommodate the slowest authority in the region.

**Nadia:** Let's pull this together into three takeaways. First, the UAE's three legal systems mean you separate the mechanical transfer under local law from the commercial agreement governed by English or DIFC law, and you ensure the SPA explicitly prevails over the short-form Arabic transfer.

**Sam:** Second, do not assert English-law outcomes in onshore courts. Break fees can be adjusted, and short notice periods ignored. Ask counsel, don't assert.

**Nadia:** Third, price in the local liabilities early. Treat accrued end-of-service gratuity as debt, deduct known Emiratisation shortfalls from the price, and demand seller cover for W and I exclusions like sanctions and war.

**Sam:** Next time we'll take a draft into the negotiation room. Which comments tell you a term really matters, and which changes can you trade?
