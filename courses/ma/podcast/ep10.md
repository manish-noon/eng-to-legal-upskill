# Episode 10: Indemnities, W&I insurance and where the money comes from

**Sam:** The seller says, "Fine, we will just give you a warranty that this four million dirham lawsuit from our old distributor has absolutely no merit." Do we take that?

**Nadia:** No. You take a specific indemnity. If you accept a warranty for a problem you already know about, you are buying a lawsuit to recover a discounted loss from a seller who will just point to the disclosure letter.

**Sam:** Episode ten. Indemnities, W and I insurance, and where the money actually comes from.

**Nadia:** We ended the last module with a hard rule. Disclosure kills the warranty claim. So if your diligence finds a material problem, you cannot fix it with a warranty. The risk is known. You need a tool built for known risks.

**Sam:** Which is the specific indemnity. It is a mechanism drafted to bypass the entire warranty framework we just built.

**Nadia:** Exactly. It is a promise by the seller to reimburse a named loss if it occurs. The wording usually says the seller shall pay on demand an amount equal to all losses suffered by the buyer or any group company, arising out of the specific claim, whether or not disclosed.

**Sam:** Let's decode the mechanics there. "An amount equal to all losses" means pound for pound reimbursement. The actual loss itself, not the resulting fall in share value.

**Nadia:** Correct. In a dirham deal, dirham for dirham. "Or any group company" matters because the target company suffers the loss, but you, the buyer, hold the claim. And "whether or not disclosed" explicitly switches off the disclosure defence.

**Sam:** So why is a warranty that the distributor claim has "no merit" worth so much less? It sounds like they are standing behind their position.

**Nadia:** Four reasons. First, the claim is disclosed, so the seller will argue the warranty is qualified by it. Second, "no merit" is an opinion. A court can find that opinion was honestly held even if the company eventually loses the case.

**Sam:** So the warranty remains true even though the underlying liability actually materialises.

**Nadia:** Quite. Third, if you do establish breach, you receive the diminution in share value at the sale date, which is heavily discounted, not the four million dirhams. And fourth, it runs through the de minimis, basket, and general cap limitations.

**Sam:** So an indemnity strips away all four filters. It pays the actual loss, it has its own cap and time limit, and it sits outside the basket.

**Nadia:** Because it is so strong, sellers will negotiate its edges. They want a cap, often set at the realistic worst case. A time limit matched to when the risk will resolve. A tight definition of the indemnified matter, and conduct of claims, meaning they run the defence since they are paying.

**Sam:** Let's test this with a scenario. A former employee is suing the target for six million dirhams. Both sides' lawyers think the claim is weak but cannot rule out a loss. It will take about two years to resolve. The seller refuses any price reduction. What should I ask for?

**Nadia:** A specific indemnity for the claim, with part of the price held in escrow until it resolves.

**Sam:** Why not just walk away if they won't cut the price? A possible six million is too large to ignore.

**Nadia:** Because a full price cut over-compensates you on the expected outcome. If the claim is truly weak, deducting six million dirhams is a windfall for you. The seller will refuse it. The indemnity lets the seller keep the full price if they are right about the claim, while you are covered for the actual loss if they are wrong.

**Sam:** It perfectly aligns incentives. Each side is backing its own probability estimate. And a seller who insists on a small price cut but refuses an escrow is telling you their real estimate of losing is actually quite high.

**Nadia:** Exactly. Now, there are two standing indemnities that appear in most deals without any specific diligence finding. The tax covenant, sometimes called a tax deed, and the leakage indemnity.

**Sam:** Let's start with the tax covenant. How does that work?

**Nadia:** It is the English-style promise that all tax of the target for periods and events up to closing is the seller's responsibility, pound for pound, unless it was provided for in the closing accounts. The logic is simple allocation by period. The seller took the profits of those years, so it bears their tax.

**Sam:** US deals do this slightly differently, right? With a pre-closing tax indemnity inside the indemnification article.

**Nadia:** Yes, plus rules allocating tax for a period that straddles closing. It reaches the same result. The time limit for tax claims is usually long, often four to seven years in the reference data, because tax authorities can reopen old years.

**Sam:** But don't assume that four to seven year range fits a UAE target. You have to ask tax counsel which periods are actually open for corporate tax, VAT, and customs before you agree to a time limit.

**Nadia:** Crucial point. The other standing indemnity is for leakage in locked-box deals. The price was fixed at a past balance sheet date. Any value the seller extracted since then, like dividends or bonuses to itself, must come back pound for pound. It has a short time limit because leakage shows up quickly in the bank statements.

**Sam:** Okay, let's establish a framework for choosing the right tool. Every diligence finding has to go to one of these destinations: price, indemnity, condition, or warranty. Let's look at some examples.

**Nadia:** Go ahead.

**Sam:** Diligence finds the target under-declared VAT in two past quarters. The amount is three million dirhams including penalties. The target's own advisers agree with the figure, and it will be paid on the next return. Which tool is best?

**Nadia:** A three million dirham reduction in the price, treated as a debt-like item.

**Sam:** Not an indemnity? Or the tax covenant?

**Nadia:** No. The liability is certain and quantified. It is simply debt the company owes. Deducting it leaves you holding the cash, with no claim to bring and zero collection risk. An indemnity reaches the same number only after a claim process, and only if the seller is still good for it.

**Sam:** Right. Certainty goes to price. Uncertainty goes to an indemnity. What about this: the target is a software company. Diligence on its code found nothing wrong, but I am still worried some component infringes a third party's rights. Do I ask for a specific indemnity for all IP infringement?

**Nadia:** You can ask, but a competent seller will refuse. There is no identified problem, so there is nothing to name in an indemnity. A blanket indemnity for unidentified risk is just a warranty with all the limitations removed. You want an IP warranty, negotiated for a minimal knowledge qualification.

**Sam:** I see why the warranty might help with an undiscovered risk. But suppose we do get that indemnity. If the seller distributes the proceeds before our claim comes due, where do we collect the money?

**Nadia:** Precisely. The value of your claim is the amount of the right, multiplied by the probability that money is still there and reachable when you win. And three common seller profiles make that probability low.

**Sam:** Private equity funds, individuals, and SPVs. A fund nearing the end of its life may distribute the proceeds and wind up before a claim falls due. An individual may hold assets in a jurisdiction where collecting on a judgment takes years.

**Nadia:** And a special purpose vehicle, an SPV, is just a company that exists only to hold the shares. It is an empty shell the day after it pays the price up to its owners.

**Sam:** Let's put numbers on that. I buy a company from an SPV owned by a PE fund. The SPA has a specific indemnity for an environmental clean-up. Uncapped, ten-year time limit, no disclosure defence. No escrow, no guarantee. Three years later, the clean-up costs nine million dirhams and I win an arbitration award for that sum. What happens?

**Nadia:** Nothing happens. Your legal right was as strong as drafting can make it, proved all the way to an award. But it failed because the debtor is an empty shell. You should have required an escrow sized to the clean-up, or a guarantee from the fund.

**Sam:** Check the seller's balance sheet after closing, not before. So we need security. What are the mechanisms?

**Nadia:** Escrow, where part of the price goes to a neutral agent like a bank. Holdback, where you simply keep part of the price and pay it later. Set-off against deferred consideration. And guarantees.

**Sam:** Holdbacks heavily favour the buyer because the seller bears our credit risk and has to sue to get their own money. Escrows are neutral on leverage since neither side holds the cash. How common are they?

**Nadia:** Very. The SRS Acquiom 2026 study found an escrow or holdback in eighty-eight percent of 2025 deals. The median was ten point zero percent of deal value without representation and warranty insurance, and two point eight percent with it.

**Sam:** And Fasken puts the median indemnity-only escrow on insured deals at zero point five percent. Let's run the math on an escrow release. I need to trace the mechanics.

**Nadia:** Alright. Price is seventy-six million dirhams, with ten percent paid into escrow for eighteen months. The clause says on the release date, the agent pays the seller the balance less the aggregate estimated liability of all outstanding claims.

**Sam:** Okay. Ten percent of seventy-six million is seven point six million funded. In month nine, a warranty claim is settled and one point one million is paid to the buyer from the account. Balance is six point five million.

**Nadia:** In month sixteen, the buyer notifies a second claim with an estimated liability of two point four million, which is still unresolved at month eighteen. Ignore interest. How much is released to the seller?

**Sam:** We take the six point five million balance, less the two point four million estimated liability. That means four million, one hundred thousand is released. The two point four million stays frozen until the second claim ends.

**Nadia:** Correct. If it later settles at one point five million, you receive one point five, and the remaining nine hundred thousand goes to the seller. This is why sellers demand that your estimated liability be reasonable and supported by counsel's opinion, so you cannot freeze the fund with an inflated notice.

**Sam:** What about set-off? Say I owe deferred consideration of ten million dirhams at month twelve. The SPA lets me set off only claims that are agreed or finally determined. At month twelve, I have an indemnity claim the seller agreed at three million, and a disputed warranty claim I value at four million. How much do I pay?

**Nadia:** Only the agreed three million can be set off. Ten million minus three million is seven million dirhams. You must pay the seven million.

**Sam:** Wait, so I hand over seven million in cash, and then separately chase them for the disputed four million? I just paid away the very money I would have collected from.

**Nadia:** Yes. Had the clause allowed set-off of merely notified claims, you would pay three million and hold four million as security. That one phrase decides who finances the dispute.

**Sam:** Let's shift to Warranty and Indemnity insurance. W and I, or R W I in the US. This fundamentally changes the transaction structure. We are replacing the seller with an insurer as the payer for unknown warranty breaches.

**Nadia:** It does. In a standard buy-side policy, the buyer is the insured. The seller's cap for business warranties drops to a nominal amount, usually one dirham, or a small percentage. The buyer's sole recourse is the policy, whether or not the policy responds.

**Sam:** So I am taking on the risk of exclusions, insurer insolvency, and declined claims. How do the layers of liability work?

**Nadia:** You have a retention, which is the first layer of loss borne by the buyer. It works like a deductible basket. Then you have the policy limit, which is the maximum the insurer pays above the retention. It plays the role of the cap.

**Sam:** How much of this claim would the policy pay? Enterprise value is one hundred million dirhams. Price is seventy-six million. The policy has a retention of zero point five percent of enterprise value, and a limit of twenty million dirhams. Seller's cap is one dirham.

**Nadia:** An unknown, covered accounts warranty breach causes a loss of eight million dirhams. How much does the insurer pay?

**Sam:** Retention is zero point five percent of the one hundred million enterprise value. That is five hundred thousand dirhams. The insurer pays the loss above the retention, up to the limit. Eight million minus half a million is seven point five million. Since that is below the twenty million limit, the insurer pays seven million, five hundred thousand.

**Nadia:** Note the base. The retention is a percentage of enterprise value, not of the price. Let us try one that breaches the limit. Enterprise value is one hundred and fifty million dirhams. Retention is zero point four percent of enterprise value. Policy limit is fifteen million. Seller's cap is one dirham.

**Sam:** Okay.

**Nadia:** A covered business warranty breach causes a loss of twenty-five million dirhams. Ignoring the one dirham, how much of the loss do you bear yourself?

**Sam:** Retention is zero point four percent of one hundred and fifty million, which is six hundred thousand. The loss above the retention is twenty-four point four million. The policy limit is fifteen million, so the insurer pays exactly fifteen million. I bear the six hundred thousand retention, plus the excess above the policy. Twenty-five million minus point six, minus fifteen, leaves nine point four million. I bear ten million dirhams in total.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The policy limit is the real cap in an insured deal, and it is a purchasing decision the buyer makes for itself. If you want more cover, buy a higher limit.

**Sam:** What does it cost? Marsh Q1 2026 reports premiums of zero point seven percent to one point eight percent of the policy limit in the Middle East, with retentions of zero point fifteen percent to zero point five percent of enterprise value.

**Nadia:** The UK is at zero point four percent to one point two percent, and the US and Canada at three point zero percent to three point seven percent. W and I appeared in twenty-three percent of European deals and fifty-nine percent of large ones according to CMS 2026. In sixty-three percent of US deals per ABA 2025.

**Sam:** And Aon 2026 reports EMEA claim notifications are up forty-seven percent, from 70 to 119. So claims are happening. But what happens if the insurer pays, can they sue the seller to get the money back?

**Nadia:** That is subrogation. And the insurer waives it against the seller, except for fraud. Without that waiver, the seller would have no clean exit and no reason to agree to the structure.

**Sam:** So a seller who knowingly gives a false warranty can still be pursued for the full loss because of the fraud exception. But what stops them giving careless warranties?

**Nadia:** The insurer does its own check through your diligence. They cannot inspect the target, so they inspect your advisers' reports. Their rule is mechanical. Where there was no diligence, or thin diligence, the matching warranties are excluded.

**Sam:** Adverse selection. A buyer that doesn't look won't find, and would otherwise insure the problems it chose not to see. So insurance doesn't replace diligence, it requires it.

**Nadia:** Look at a failed strategy. To save fees, a buyer does no tax diligence on the target's Saudi subsidiary. The deal has W and I insurance and a one dirham seller cap. There is no tax covenant because of the insurance structure. A year later, a pre-closing tax assessment of four million dirhams hits the subsidiary. What is the outcome?

**Sam:** The buyer eats it. The insurer excluded the Saudi tax because it was undiligenced. The seller's liability is capped at one dirham. The saved diligence fee bought an uninsured, unindemnified gap. Diligence scope is cover scope.

**Nadia:** Precisely. Now, what about standard exclusions? Forward-looking warranties. Price adjustments and leakage. And per Sidley, May 2026, Middle East policies now carry war, civil unrest and sanctions exclusions, with Iran uninsurable and Iraq largely off-limits.

**Sam:** And anything the deal team knew. Which brings up the biggest trap. My diligence report flags that the target has been processing customer data without required consents. The deal has insurance and a one dirham seller cap. The SPA includes an unqualified data-protection warranty and the seller makes no disclosure.

**Nadia:** After closing, a regulator acts and the cost is two million dirhams. Where does the loss fall?

**Sam:** On the buyer. The matter was known to the deal team because it was in our own report, so it is excluded from the policy. The seller is technically in breach, but their cap is one dirham.

**Nadia:** Exactly. The policy tests the buyer's knowledge, not the seller's disclosure. A known issue in an insured deal needs a specific indemnity outside the nominal cap, or a price reduction. Insurance changed who pays for the unknown. It changed nothing for the known.

**Sam:** One last piece of the liability structure. Fundamental warranties, like title to the shares. The SPA makes the seller liable for fundamental losses to the extent not recovered under the policy, up to the purchase price.

**Nadia:** Let us use our earlier numbers. Price is seventy-six million. Policy limit is twenty million, retention is half a million. It emerges the seller did not validly own a large block of the shares, and the covered loss is forty million dirhams. The insurer pays its full entitlement. How much must the seller pay?

**Sam:** The insurer pays the loss above the half million retention, capped at twenty million. The unrecovered loss is forty million minus twenty million, which is twenty million. That includes the retention. Since twenty million is within the seventy-six million fundamental cap, the seller pays twenty million dirhams.

**Nadia:** Assuming they have the money. Recourse for the fundamental layer still needs thought even in an insured deal.

**Sam:** So if the seller's cap is one dirham for everything else, they stop caring about warranty scope, right? Why doesn't that give me a perfect set of warranties?

**Nadia:** Because the insurer becomes your real counterparty. They review the warranties and will exclude or read down those they find too broad, like forward-looking statements or warranties with no knowledge qualifier on matters nobody could verify. An over-generous SPA warranty that the policy does not cover is worth one dirham.

**Sam:** Three takeaways. First, match the tool to the problem. Certainty goes to price, uncertainty to an indemnity, and the unknown to warranties.

**Nadia:** Second, a legal right is just an option on the seller's solvency. Check their balance sheet after closing, and hold back cash or use an escrow if you are buying from an empty shell.

**Sam:** Third, W and I insurance covers the unknown. Undiligenced areas are excluded, and known risks still require a specific indemnity or a price cut.

**Nadia:** Next time, we look at conditions precedent and how a deal actually closes.
