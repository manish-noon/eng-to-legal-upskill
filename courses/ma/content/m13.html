Negotiation dynamics: how terms actually move

After this module you can read a negotiation as a process: who drafts, how positions are padded and traded, what creates leverage, and how to tell from a markup where an issue is going to land.

Who holds the pen

The party that produces the first draft has the first draft advantage, usually called holding the pen. The drafter sets the structure, the defined terms and the default answer to every question nobody raises. The other side must find each problem, justify each change and spend goodwill on each one. Every clause they miss stays as drafted. It is an anchoring effect with a labour cost attached.

Convention settles who drafts:

  • Auction: the seller's lawyers draft the SPA and put it in the data room. Bidders submit a markup with their bid, and the seller scores price and markup together. A heavy markup lowers the value of a bid, so bidders restrain themselves. The seller converts competitive tension into contract terms.
  • Bilateral deal: the buyer usually drafts. It is the party paying and taking the risk, and it has the diligence findings to draft around.
  • Ancillary documents follow the same logic: whoever has more at stake in a document tends to draft it.

A founder is approached by one strategic buyer. No bank is running a process and the founder has granted eight weeks of exclusivity. The founder's lawyer offers to "save time" by producing the first SPA draft. From the buyer's side, what do you say?

  • Accept: whoever drafts bears the cost, so let the seller pay
  • Decline politely: in a bilateral deal the buyer normally holds the pen, and it has no competitive pressure forcing it to accept seller paper
  • Accept: seller drafts are the norm in every deal type
  • It makes no difference, since everything will be negotiated anyway

The drafting cost is trivial against the value of setting every default. Seller paper is the auction convention, where competition justifies it. Here the buyer is the only bidder and has exclusivity, so it gives up the pen for nothing. "Everything gets negotiated" is false in practice: only what gets noticed and is worth a fight gets negotiated.

The markup cycle and the issues list

Negotiation runs in rounds, called turns:

  1. Draft. One side sends the document.
  2. Markup (or redline). The other side returns it with tracked changes: insertions, deletions and margin comments giving reasons.
  3. Issues list. Each legal team distils the open points for its client.
  4. All-hands call. Both teams and often the principals talk through the list. Some points close, some get a direction.
  5. Next turn. Compare the revised draft with the last markup and the call notes. A comment marked “agreed in principle” stays on the issues list until the operative clause, definitions and schedules give effect to the agreement.

An issues list is a table of the open points: clause, what the draft says, why it matters, our proposal, our fallback. It exists because the decision-maker will not read 120 pages. The alternative is a page turn: going through the document line by line on a call. Large deals use both.

A useful issues list is tiered by how much the client cares:

TierMeaningExample for a buyer
Must-haveThe deal does not work without it. Tied to a walk-away.Specific indemnity for a known tax exposure larger than the warranty cap
TradeReal value, but exchangeable for something else.24 months against 18 months for warranty claims
GiveAsked for, cheap to drop, useful as currency.Seller to pay the buyer's costs of preparing the completion accounts

The tiers come from the client's facts, not from the clause type. A cap is a must-have for one buyer and a give for another who has W&I insurance.

You act for a buyer. Facts: W&I insurance is in place with a limit of 30% of price. Diligence found a customs duty exposure of about AED 9m, which the insurer has excluded as a known issue. The seller is a fund that will distribute the proceeds and wind up within a year. Three open issues: (A) seller's general warranty cap, seller offers AED 1; (B) specific indemnity for the customs exposure, backed by an escrow; (C) non-compete of 3 years against the offered 2. How do you tier them?

  • A must-have, B trade, C give
  • A must-have, B must-have, C trade
  • A give, B must-have, C trade or give
  • A trade, B give, C must-have

With W&I covering unknown breaches, the seller's general cap matters little: concede it and collect credit. The insurer expressly excluded customs. A warranty claim would require proving a breach and overcoming the proposed nominal cap. A specific indemnity needs a funded or otherwise credible source of recovery once the seller distributes the price. That makes B the walk-away item. A fund seller rarely gives a real non-compete anyway (Module 11), so C is not where to spend. The instinct "cap is always the big issue" is the error.

Positions come in threes, and why padding is rational

For each issue a negotiator holds three values:

  • Opening position: what you ask for first.
  • Fallback: what you will accept when pushed, often several steps.
  • Walk-away: the point beyond which you prefer no deal. It should be set by the client before negotiation starts.

The other side sees your opening. It has to infer how far you can move. Compare the least each side would accept on the whole package. If those positions overlap, there may be room for a deal. A change in the facts, a price adjustment or a concession on another issue can change that overlap.

Consider a fictional acquisition of a packaging business. The buyer needs historical invoices to check customer rebates after closing. The seller retains some records with its other businesses. The buyer asks for access to all the files, delivery in its preferred format and help from the seller’s finance staff. Its real priority is evidence of what was sold to the customers concerned. It can give up the format and still achieve that. If the other side objects to all three requests, find out which would actually cost it money or expose it to a claim.

The cost of padding is credibility and time. Open absurdly and the other side discounts everything you say, or a seller in an auction simply picks another bidder.

Take the client’s instructions. Before sending the next draft, explain what each concession buys. In the packaging example, agreeing to accept extracts might save both sides time. Giving up access altogether could leave the buyer unable to answer a customer who demands a rebate. Those are different decisions for the client.

“We can accept redacted invoices if they still show the dates, quantities and prices used to calculate the rebate. What information would finance need to settle a disputed amount?”
Before reading on: if both sides know openings are padded, why not skip the dance and exchange true positions?

Because neither side can verify the other's "true" position, a party that reveals its real walk-away has simply made that its opening, and will be pushed below it. Padding is the equilibrium of a game with private information. What experienced lawyers do instead is shorten the dance: they know the usual landing zones, so they can discount padding quickly and spend time only where the overlap is genuinely unclear. That shared knowledge is what "market" means.

Client data, market knowledge and honest use of studies

Two kinds of knowledge sit behind a number in a draft. Client data is a fact about one deal: this buyer paid this price and agreed this figure. Market evidence comes from relevant comparables. Record the source, date, sample and denominator so the client can judge how much weight to give it.

Suppose three fictional agreements for comparable uninsured acquisitions cap general warranty claims at 8%, 12% and 16% of price. Those examples let you explain why a seller might open at 8% and what that would mean in cash. To use a real agreement as a comparable, verify the denominator, which warranties the cap covers, and whether a policy or holdback changes who ultimately pays.

A buyer with no competing offer might ask for 16%. A seller in a competitive auction may have little reason to move from 8%. Neither opening tells you whether the buyer can afford the uninsured exposure. Read the uncovered risks beside the cap before you recommend a fallback.

Time limits need a different comparison. Ask when the buyer would have the information to discover the breach, and what the clause requires it to do before the deadline. The CMS European M&A Study 2026 gives context: roughly a third of the surveyed deals had limits of 18 to 24 months and another third had longer limits. That comparison leaves a separate question for this buyer: will its first audit finish before its notice deadline?

The purchase price is USD 25m. Use the fictional general warranty caps above. The seller instructs you to open at the lowest observed percentage. What amount do you put in the first draft? Answer in USD.

At 8% of USD 25m, the seller’s opening cap is USD 2m. At 16%, the upper example would give the buyer a USD 4m cap. The figures are illustrations. Neither number establishes the amount the buyer could collect on a particular claim.

You act for the seller of a company in a European-style deal. The buyer's draft has the general warranty cap at 100% of price. You know from CMS 2026 that 58% of deals had a cap below 50% of price, and 43% of large deals below 10%. There is no W&I. The seller’s team has verified comparable uninsured transactions supporting an opening between 10% and 15%. The client has authorised that opening. Which opening is best?

  • 50%: open at the reasonable middle to save time
  • Around 10% to 15%: the seller's end of a defensible range, leaving room to concede towards a landing well below 50%
  • AED 1: always open at the extreme
  • Accept 100% and seek a longer list of disclosures instead

You open at your own end of a range you can justify with a source. Opening at 50% makes 50% your best case, since the landing lies between the openings. AED 1 without insurance has no market support, so it costs credibility on every other point. The study justifies "below 50% is common". It does not prove any particular number is right for this deal.

What the studies can and cannot do. The CMS European M&A Study 2026 covers 601 deals from 2025 on which CMS advised. The ABA 2025 study covers US deals with publicly filed agreements, mostly mid-market. SRS Acquiom covers deals where it acted as agent. Each is a sample with a bias. The reference course is explicit that no public dataset of GCC deal terms exists.

So a study can do three things: show that your position is not unusual, show the other side's position is an outlier, and give both sides a neutral number to settle on. It cannot tell you what is right for this deal. A 58% figure says nothing about whether this seller, with these diligence findings and no insurance, should get a low cap. "Market" is an argument, not a rule. The other side will answer with its own facts, and leverage decides. Three habits keep you honest: name the study, state what its sample is, and never quote a number you cannot source.

On a call about a UAE deal, the seller's lawyer says: "A deductible basket is market. Look at the US data." You know from the reference that 49% of US baskets are deductible against 13% in Europe, and that CMS 2026 shows 87% of European baskets as tipping. The SPA is English-law style. Best reply?

  • "You are wrong, tipping is the rule in the UAE."
  • "That is US practice. This is an English-style SPA, and the European data has 87% of baskets tipping. Neither study covers the GCC, so let's talk about why a deductible is right for this deal."
  • "Agreed, if it is market we accept."
  • "Studies are irrelevant to negotiation."

The honest counter attacks the fit of the sample, offers a better-matched one, admits its limit, and moves the discussion to deal-specific reasons. Option 1 asserts a UAE "rule" that no dataset supports, the exact mistake the reference warns against. Option 3 treats "market" as binding. Option 4 throws away a useful tool.

Leverage: what makes one side move

Leverage is the ability to make the other side concede because not agreeing costs them more than it costs you. It has identifiable sources:

  • Alternatives. Your BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) is what you do if this deal fails. A seller with a second bidder has a strong one. A buyer with another target has one too. Your walk-away on every issue derives from it.
  • Time pressure. A seller facing a loan repayment, a fund at the end of its life, a buyer whose exclusivity expires on Friday. Test the cost of missing the deadline and whether the other side knows about it.
  • Information. The seller knows the business. The buyer knows its own price ceiling and synergies. Diligence shifts information to the buyer, and each finding is a lever.
  • Competitive tension. Real or believed rival bidders. This is what an auction manufactures.
  • Market cycle. In a seller's market, capital chases few assets and terms drift to sellers. In a buyer's market the reverse.

Leverage moves the landing inside the range. It rarely moves it outside what the market has seen before, because both sets of lawyers anchor to that range.

Facts: a family-owned UAE manufacturer is for sale. One bidder remains after the other two dropped out, which the seller's bank has not disclosed. The seller's main lender has required repayment within four months. The buyer's diligence found the top customer contract expires in six months. The seller's lawyer opens with a cap of 5% and a 6-month time limit, saying "we have other interest". Who has the leverage, and what should the buyer infer?

  • The seller: it has declared other interest and opened aggressively, so the buyer should move towards it
  • The buyer: the seller has a deadline, a diligence weakness and no visible competing bid, so the aggressive opening is padding and the buyer should test the "other interest" claim
  • Neither: leverage is equal in a bilateral negotiation
  • The seller, because family owners never need to sell

Leverage is read from facts, not from tone. Time pressure and an information finding both favour the buyer. The competitive tension is asserted, not shown: no second markup, no process deadline. Aggressive openings from a weak position are common, because opening costs nothing. The buyer tests it by holding its position and watching how fast the seller's fallback appears.

Before reading on: the buyer in that example knows the seller is under time pressure. Why might it still not push every term to the extreme?

Three reasons. The family may be staying on as managers or minority owners, and you do not want to start that relationship with a squeeze. Extreme terms push the seller to find any alternative, including one you do not know about. And a seller pressed too hard on contract terms recovers it elsewhere: less cooperation in the gap, harder lines on the completion accounts. Leverage is used most efficiently on the two or three points that carry real money.

Moves that work: repetition, carve-outs, trades

Follow the protection into the schedules. In the fictional packaging sale, the seller offers to list the relevant rebate arrangements in a schedule. The list identifies which customers matter. It says nothing about who must keep the invoices, who can request them or what happens when the figures are disputed. Keep the list and add those obligations to the records covenant. One provision identifies the records and the other gives the buyer access to them.

Read the objection behind the deletion. The seller deletes the buyer’s request for the full files and comments, “Different businesses share these invoices. We owe confidentiality to other customers.” A carve-out is a narrow exception. Offer redaction of unrelated customer details, while preserving the figures the buyer needs to verify a rebate. Ask whether an independent accountant can inspect a disputed entry under confidentiality. If the objection persists, find out whether it concerns the accountant’s access, the cost of retrieving records or a restriction in the customer contract itself.

For each customer listed in Schedule R, the Seller shall provide invoice extracts showing the quantities and prices used to calculate the rebate. The Seller may redact information about other customers. For a disputed entry, an independent accountant appointed by both parties may inspect the supporting records under an undertaking of confidentiality.

Put the remaining choice to the client. In this exercise, the seller will provide extracts but has reserved its position on the accountant. Finance can use the extracts for routine reconciliations. A disputed rebate may require evidence behind a redaction. Put that exposure on the issues list and ask the client whether to insist on independent verification, seek a different remedy, or accept the risk. Record its instruction and check the next draft against it.

Packaging and trading. Issues are not settled one by one on their merits. They are bundled. Logrolling is trading across issues: concede what is cheap for you and valuable to them, in exchange for the reverse. It creates value when the parties value things differently, and they always do: a fund seller values a low cap and short time limit highly (it must return money to investors), while a strategic buyer may value a specific indemnity or a longer tax period more.

This needs a price on each issue. A workable estimate is probability times amount. If the buyer thinks there is a 10% chance of a claim surfacing between month 18 and month 24, with average recoverable loss of AED 4m, six more months are worth about AED 400,000 to it.

Price AED 100m. The seller offers a package: it extends the warranty time limit from 18 to 24 months if the buyer accepts a cap of 20% of price, down from 30%. The buyer estimates: a 10% chance of a claim arising in months 18 to 24, average recoverable loss AED 4m. And a 2% chance of a loss above AED 20m, where the average amount lost to the lower cap would be AED 5m. What is the net expected value of the package to the buyer? Answer in AED.

Gain: 0.10 × 4,000,000 = 400,000. Cost: 0.02 × 5,000,000 = 100,000. Net: +AED 300,000, so the buyer should take it. The seller may value a lower maximum liability. How much cash it can distribute at closing depends on any holdback, reserve policy and other obligations. Both sides better off is the point of a trade.

Two open issues. Issue A: the buyer wants it, values it at AED 250,000, and conceding it costs the seller AED 50,000. Issue B: the seller wants it, values it at AED 300,000, and conceding it costs the buyer AED 80,000. They trade A for B. What is the total value created across both parties, compared with neither concession being made? Answer in AED.

Buyer: +250,000 − 80,000 = +170,000. Seller: +300,000 − 50,000 = +250,000. Total: AED 420,000. Equivalent: (250,000 − 50,000) + (300,000 − 80,000). If they had split each issue down the middle, most of that surplus would not exist. This is why you price issues before the call.

A buyer has three concessions it could offer to get a specific indemnity it values at AED 2m. Its own cost of each, and the seller's value of each: (1) drop the request for seller-paid W&I premium: costs buyer AED 600k, worth AED 600k to seller. (2) Shorten general warranty period from 24 to 18 months with W&I in place: costs buyer AED 50k, worth AED 500k to a fund seller. (3) Raise the price by AED 700k. Which should it offer first?

  • (1), because it is the cleanest: a simple cash item
  • (2): it has the highest ratio of value to the seller over cost to the buyer
  • (3), because price is what sellers care about most
  • None: never concede before the other side does

Options 1 and 3 are zero-sum cash transfers: every dirham the seller gains, the buyer loses. Option 2 costs the buyer AED 50k (the insurer, not the seller, stands behind the warranties) and gives the seller AED 500k of value (earlier release of its funds). Efficient concessions are the ones the other side values far above your cost. Refusing to move first just delays the trade.

Symmetry, signals and the end-game

Who can insist on its form? An auction seller can ask all bidders to mark up the same SPA. A bidder who needs a particular protection must weigh it against the chance of losing the deal. With one credible buyer and a seller under pressure to close, that buyer has more room to propose different wording. The same deletion can signal confidence in another bidder, a genuine operating constraint or simply the pen-holder’s preference. Ask a question that would distinguish them.

Reading a markup. What the other side did not change is information:

  • Left untouched. Record that the provision was unchanged in this turn. Check reservations and the related definitions. An unchanged paragraph can have a different effect if a definition has moved.
  • Accepted with edits. Read the revised sentence alongside the old one. A small change to who may claim or when notice must arrive can remove much of the protection.
  • Deleted with a reason: the reason is an invitation to draft a carve-out.
  • Deleted without comment. Ask why. The lawyer may have received an instruction, spotted conflicting language elsewhere or proposed an opening position.
  • Deleted and replaced by different wording that does the same job: they accept the substance but want their own words.
  • Repeated objection. Ask who has authority to change the position and what evidence or concession would justify taking it to that person.

Your buyer-side draft contained a tipping basket at 0.5% of price, a cap at 50%, and a 24-month time limit. The seller's markup changes the cap to 15% with the comment "consistent with our fund's requirements", changes 24 months to 12 with no comment, and leaves the basket clause untouched. What is the best reading?

  • The basket is still open: silence means they reserve their position
  • The basket is unchanged in this turn. Ask what drives the cap and shorter notice period before deciding which concession to trade.
  • All three are firm positions, since the seller's lawyer reviewed every clause
  • The cap is the padding, because it came with a justification

Record what the markup shows. The fund comment gives you a lead on the cap, but you still need to ask whether the constraint concerns the liability amount, the cash held back or the date of release. The shorter time limit has no explanation. Confirm both the proposed deadline and the unchanged basket during the next discussion.

In round one a seller deletes your warranty that "no key customer has given notice of termination", commenting that the seller cannot know what customers privately intend. What is the strongest next turn?

  • Reinstate the warranty unchanged and say it is a must-have
  • Accept the deletion, since the objection is reasonable
  • Reinstate it with a carve-out matched to the objection: limit it to written notices actually received by the target, or qualify it by the seller's knowledge
  • Escalate to the principals immediately

The comment refers to a customer’s private intentions. The draft asks about a notice already given. Bring that distinction to the call, identify which people receive notices and agree what evidence they should check. A continuing refusal is reason to ask further questions and review correspondence.

After three turns, one point remains: the buyer wants a 36-month non-compete, the seller offers 24. Both lawyers have repeated the same arguments twice. The difference is worth little to the buyer in money terms. What should the buyer's lawyer do?

  • Hold firm for another turn: the seller will tire first
  • Concede on the lawyer's own judgement to get the deal done
  • Put it to the client with the value at stake and a recommendation, take the client's decision, and record it
  • Introduce three new requests to create trading material

Repeated arguments mean the lawyers have run out of authority, not out of arguments. The decision belongs to the principal, who can weigh it against the whole deal. Conceding without instructions exceeds the lawyer's role, even when the answer is obvious. New requests late in a deal destroy trust and reopen closed points.

The end-game. Drafts get cleaner each turn until a handful of points remain, typically the ones each side marked must-have plus a few stragglers. The lawyers prepare a one-page list of the last five or so issues with a proposed package. The principals get on a call, usually without many lawyers talking, and trade them in one go: "you take the cap, we take the time limit and the escrow". Price sometimes moves a little to balance the package. The lawyers then paper exactly what was agreed. The predictable shape of that final call (which issues are still open, and which way each will fall) is what the rest of this course teaches you to forecast.

Before reading on: why are the last few issues settled by principals as a package and not one at a time by the lawyers?

One at a time, each issue is a win or a loss for someone, and nobody wants to lose the last one. As a package, each side can win some and present the whole as balanced. Principals can also trade across categories that lawyers cannot: a contract term against price, or against a commercial relationship after closing. And only principals know their true walk-aways.

Before reading on: in an auction, one bidder returns the seller's draft SPA with only four changes. What does that tell the seller, and why do bidders do it?

It signals a bidder competing on terms: it believes the auction is tight, or its price is not the highest and it is making up the difference with certainty and speed. Sellers treat a light markup as worth real money, because every unmarked clause is a risk the seller no longer has to negotiate. This is why bidders tier their issues ruthlessly before marking up auction paper: only must-haves and a few trades go in.

Key terms

Holding the pen
Controlling the drafting of the agreement, which sets the structure and the default answer on every unraised point.
Markup (redline)
A version of the draft returned with tracked insertions, deletions and comments.
Turn
One round of the negotiation cycle: a draft or markup going from one side to the other.
Issues list
A table of the open points with each side's position, the proposal and the fallback, used to brief decision-makers.
Page turn
A meeting or call in which the parties go through the document page by page.
Opening position
The first position a party states on an issue, normally more favourable to itself than what it expects to get.
Fallback
A position a party is prepared to retreat to if its opening is refused.
Walk-away
The point on an issue beyond which a party prefers no deal, set by the client.
BATNA
Best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what a party will do if this deal fails, which determines its walk-away.
Leverage
The ability to obtain concessions because failure to agree costs the other side more than it costs you.
Negotiating mandate
The client’s instructions on priorities, acceptable concessions and decisions its lawyer must refer back.
Carve-out
A narrow exception written into a clause, commonly used to meet a specific objection while keeping the clause.
Logrolling
Trading concessions across different issues so that each side gives what is cheap to it and valuable to the other.
Records covenant
A promise to keep or provide identified records. Read who can request them, when, and what happens if the supporting figures are disputed.
Comparable
An agreement selected as evidence for a particular term because its relevant features resemble the proposed deal.
