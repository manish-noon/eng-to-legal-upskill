Warranties and disclosure: the information game

After this module you can read a warranty schedule and a disclosure letter, decide whether a post-closing discovery is a claim at all, estimate what it is worth, and say which qualifier moved which risk to which side.

Why warranties exist

The default rule in a share sale is caveat emptor ("buyer beware"): the seller has no general duty to volunteer problems, and the buyer takes the company as it finds it. The seller has run the business for years. You have had six weeks in a data room. That is a textbook information asymmetry, and without a fix a rational buyer assumes the worst and discounts the price, which hurts honest sellers most.

A warranty is a contractual statement of fact about the target, made by the seller, with damages payable if it is untrue. It does two separate jobs.

  1. Forcing disclosure (before signing). Warranties are drafted as absolutes: "there is no litigation". The seller escapes liability only by listing the exceptions. So the warranty schedule is a questionnaire with a financial penalty for wrong answers. Most of its value is delivered before you sign, because what comes out changes the price or the deal.
  2. Adjusting the price (after closing). For anything untrue that was not disclosed, you get money back. The seller pays even if it was honest and careful. A warranty allocates the risk of the unknown. It is not a statement about the seller's good faith.
Before reading on: caveat emptor protects the seller. Why would a seller ever volunteer to give warranties?

Because the buyer prices what it cannot verify. With no warranties, the buyer must assume hidden problems and bid lower (the "market for lemons" result). A seller who knows the company is clean can sell that knowledge by standing behind it. Warranties are a credible signal precisely because they cost a dishonest seller more than an honest one. The negotiation is then about how much of the signal the seller will fund: scope, qualifiers and the financial limits in the next module.

Title and capacity are fundamental warranties: if they are false you did not get the company at all, so they carry higher limits (next module). An encumbrance is any third-party right over an asset, such as a pledge or an option.

The Seller is the sole legal and beneficial owner of the Shares, free from all Encumbrances.
No Group Company is engaged in any litigation, arbitration or administrative proceedings, and so far as the Seller is aware none is threatened.

Decode: the first is absolute. The second is absolute for existing claims but limited to the seller's awareness for threatened ones. The third imports an accounting standard ("true and fair"), so a breach is proved with accountants, not lawyers.

Who gives them. With several sellers, liability is either several (each seller is liable only for its own share, usually its percentage of the price) or joint and several (you can recover 100% from any one seller, who must then chase the others). Joint and several moves the collection risk from you to the sellers. Private equity funds refuse business warranties: they did not run the company and must distribute proceeds to their investors. They give title and capacity only. Business warranties then come from management, who know the facts but have little money. That gap is what insurance fills (module 9).

Three sellers hold 60%, 30% and 10%. Liability is several, in proportion to shareholding. A warranty breach causes a loss of AED 5m. The 60% seller is insolvent. What is the most you can recover? Answer in AED.

Several liability: each seller owes only its own share. 30% of 5m = 1.5m, plus 10% of 5m = 0.5m. Total 2,000,000. The insolvent seller's 3m is your loss. Under joint and several liability you could have taken the full 5m from either solvent seller, and the insolvency would have been their problem.

You buy 100% of a company from a PE fund (85%) and three managers (15%). The fund gives title and capacity warranties only. The managers give the business warranties, with liability capped at a small fraction of their proceeds. Six months later the accounts turn out to be materially wrong. With no other protection in place, what is your position?

  • You claim against the fund, because it received most of the price
  • You can claim only against the managers, up to their low cap, and most of the loss stays with you
  • You claim against the fund under the title warranty, because the shares were worth less than stated
  • You can rescind the contract, because the accounts warranty is a fundamental warranty

Liability follows who gave the warranty, not who got the money. The fund warranted ownership of the shares, which was true. A title warranty says nothing about what the shares are worth. Accounts warranties are business warranties, not fundamental ones. This is the structural gap in PE exits, and the reason such deals usually carry warranty insurance.

What a breach is worth

Under English law the buyer must prove two things: the warranty was untrue (breach) and the buyer lost money as a result (loss). The measure of loss is diminution in value: the value of the shares as warranted minus their actual value, normally assessed at the date of the sale. It is not the cost of fixing the problem. That has two consequences that point in opposite directions.

Example 1: recurring profit. You paid AED 76m, built from enterprise value of 10 × EBITDA of AED 10m, less net adjustments of 24m. After closing you find AED 0.5m of the EBITDA was never real and will not recur. True EBITDA is 9.5m. At the same multiple, enterprise value is 95m and the shares are worth 71m. Loss: AED 5m, ten times the misstatement, because you paid a multiple for every dirham of profit.

Example 2: one-off liability. A former distributor is suing the company for AED 2m. It was not disclosed. At the sale date an informed buyer would have put the chance of losing at 40% and paid about AED 0.8m less. Loss: about AED 0.8m, even if the company later loses and pays 2m. A one-off item is never multiplied, and a contingent one is discounted.

A warranty can also be breached with no loss at all: a missing board minute changes no buyer's price.

Engineers assume damages equal the repair cost. For warranties they equal the price difference. Sometimes that is far more than the repair cost (recurring earnings), sometimes less (contingent or one-off items). If you want the repair cost, you need an indemnity (module 9).
US-style agreements do not rely on the common-law measure. The seller agrees to indemnify the buyer for "Losses" from any inaccuracy in a representation, dollar for dollar. Whether a multiple can be claimed then depends on drafting: sellers try to exclude "diminution in value" and "multiples of earnings" from the definition of Losses.

Price AED 80m, built as 8 × EBITDA of AED 12m (enterprise value 96m) less net debt of 16m. The accounts warranty is unqualified. After closing you find that AED 0.75m a year of recurring revenue, at 100% margin, never existed. English-law measure, same multiple. What is the loss? Answer in AED.

True EBITDA = 12m - 0.75m = 11.25m. Enterprise value = 8 × 11.25m = 90m. Shares as warranted: 96m - 16m = 80m. Shares as they are: 90m - 16m = 74m. Loss = 6,000,000, which is 0.75m × 8. Net debt is unchanged, so the whole enterprise value drop falls on the shares.

The litigation warranty is unqualified and nothing was disclosed. At the sale date a customer claim of AED 4m was pending, which an informed buyer would have given a 30% chance of success, deducting the expected value from the price. Eighteen months later the company loses and pays the full 4m. On the standard measure, valued at the sale date without hindsight, what is the warranty loss? Answer in AED.

Value as warranted minus actual value at the sale date = the price reduction a buyer would have demanded = 30% × 4m = 1,200,000. The company's later cash cost was 4m, so the buyer is 2.8m short. Courts sometimes look at later events when valuing a contingency, so the seller cannot rely on this number being the ceiling, but the planning point stands: for a known, lumpy risk a warranty is the wrong tool. An indemnity would pay the 4m.

Why English sellers refuse the word "represents". Under English law a false representation (a statement of fact that induced the other party to contract) opens the law of misrepresentation. Its remedies include rescission (unwinding the whole sale) and damages on the tort measure (putting you back where you were before the deal, which can exceed the contract measure if you overpaid). Sellers therefore "warrant" only and add a clause stating the buyer relied on nothing outside the contract. US-style agreements say "represents and warrants" throughout, because the remedy is the contractual indemnification article anyway and is usually stated to be exclusive.

Qualifiers: knowledge and materiality

An unqualified warranty puts the whole risk of the unknown on the seller. Two qualifiers hand part of it back to you.

Knowledge (awareness). "So far as the Seller is aware, no third party is infringing the Company's IP." If nobody on the seller's side knew, there is no breach, however large the loss. Three parameters decide how much risk moves:

  • Actual knowledge: what the named people in fact knew. Constructive knowledge: what they would have known after reasonable enquiry.
  • Whose knowledge: two directors, or everyone in senior management.
  • Deemed enquiry: a clause stating the seller is treated as having asked specified people (the CTO, the finance team, the auditors).
Any Warranty qualified by the expression "so far as the Seller is aware" is deemed to include a statement that it is made after due and careful enquiry of the Key Managers.

Sellers accept knowledge qualifiers most readily for things outside their control (what third parties intend, threatened claims). Buyers resist them on matters the company itself should know (its own contracts, its own accounts). In the ABA 2025 study of US deals, 90% used a constructive knowledge standard.

Materiality. "The Company has complied in all material respects with applicable laws." Small failures are not breaches. The seller's argument is fair (no company complies perfectly). The cost to you is that "material" is undefined, so the line is argued after the event.

Materiality scrape. A clause telling the reader to ignore materiality qualifiers for stated purposes. A single scrape ignores them only when calculating loss, once a breach exists. A double scrape ignores them both for deciding whether there is a breach and for calculating loss. Take the compliance warranty above, on a AED 76m deal:

The buyer's argument: the financial thresholds in the next module already filter small claims, so a materiality word plus a threshold filters twice. The seller's argument: a double scrape deletes the qualifier it negotiated for. The scrape is a US-style device and is less common in English-style agreements.

The IP warranty reads "so far as the Seller is aware, the Company does not infringe third-party IP". Awareness is defined as the actual knowledge of the CEO and CFO, with no enquiry clause. Before signing, the CTO received a cease-and-desist letter and told nobody. After closing the claim costs AED 3m. Is there a breach?

  • Yes: the company received the letter, and the company's knowledge is the seller's knowledge
  • Yes: a knowledge qualifier only protects against matters nobody could have discovered
  • No: neither named person actually knew. There would be a breach if the definition deemed enquiry of the CTO
  • No: IP infringement by the company can never be the subject of a warranty claim

The warranty is only as wide as its knowledge definition. Actual knowledge of two named people who did not know means the statement was true as drafted. Nothing in the contract attributes the company's or the CTO's knowledge to the seller. A constructive standard, or deemed enquiry of the CTO, would have caught it, because a reasonable enquiry of the CTO would have surfaced the letter. This is why the definition is negotiated as hard as the warranty.

Warranty: "The Company has complied in all material respects with employment laws." Price AED 80m. After closing you find two unrelated underpayments costing AED 150k and AED 100k. Neither is material alone or together. Under which drafting is there a breach, and what loss enters the claim?

  • No scrape: breach, AED 250k
  • Single scrape: breach, AED 250k, because the qualifier is ignored for loss
  • Double scrape only: breach, AED 250k
  • None: materiality qualifiers cannot be removed by another clause

A single scrape switches off "material" only at the loss stage, and you never reach that stage if no breach exists. With the qualifier in place these failures are not breaches. Only a double scrape reads the warranty as "has complied with employment laws", which is breached by any failure. The 250k then still has to pass the financial thresholds in module 8.

Same warranty, same deal, single scrape (loss only). You find three failures costing AED 900k, AED 150k and AED 100k. The parties agree the 900k failure is material and the others are not. Ignoring the thresholds of the next module, and treating cost as loss, what loss is claimable? Answer in AED.

Step 1, breach: decided with the qualifier in place. The 900k failure is material, so the warranty is breached. Step 2, loss: the scrape removes "material", so loss is measured against full compliance: 900k + 150k + 100k = 1,150,000. With no scrape the seller would argue for 900k. With a double scrape the answer is also 1,150,000. The double scrape only changes the outcome when no single failure is material.

Disclosure: how a breach becomes a non-claim

The warranties are given "except as Disclosed". In English-style deals the exceptions sit in a separate disclosure letter from seller to buyer, delivered at signing. It has two parts.

  • General disclosures: whole categories deemed known to you, such as public registers, the accounts and often the entire data room.
  • Specific disclosures: individual facts, each set against a numbered warranty ("Warranty 9.1: the Company is defendant in a claim by X for AED 2m").

Anything disclosed cannot found a warranty claim. So disclosure is the seller's main defence, and its quality standard is the main fight. The buyer's standard is fair disclosure:

"Disclosed" means fairly disclosed in the Disclosure Letter, with sufficient detail to enable a reasonable buyer to identify the nature and scope of the matter disclosed.

Decode: a hint is not enough. A document from which a careful reader could have inferred a problem, after further enquiry, does not qualify. Sellers want the opposite: everything in the data room deemed disclosed against every warranty. That turns your diligence into their defence and puts on you the cost of reading every page perfectly.

US-style deals use disclosure schedules: exceptions listed section by section, forming part of the agreement. A blanket data-room disclosure is unusual. A disclosure normally qualifies only its own section and others where its relevance is reasonably apparent.

The consequence is the most important sentence in this module: a warranty protects you only against what you were not told. Once an issue is disclosed, the warranty is useless for it. A known issue must go to price, a condition, or a specific indemnity (a promise to reimburse a named loss, module 9).

Before reading on: the seller offers to disclose "all matters contained in the data room". Why is that worse for you than 200 specific disclosures?

Specific disclosures are a list of known defects you can price one by one. A general data-room disclosure is a transfer of search cost and error risk: any document among thousands that reveals a problem defeats your claim, whether or not anyone on your team understood it. You also lose the forcing function: the seller no longer has to think about which facts contradict which warranty. If you must accept it, insist on the fair disclosure standard and a frozen copy of the data room, so there is no argument later about what was in it.

The seller specifically discloses, against the litigation warranty, a customs dispute with a stated maximum exposure of AED 3m. You sign without further protection. The company later loses and pays AED 3m. What can you recover under the litigation warranty?

  • AED 3m: the warranty said there was no litigation and there was
  • The diminution in value, probably less than AED 3m
  • Nothing: the matter was fairly disclosed, so it is not a breach
  • AED 3m, but only if you can show the seller expected to lose

The warranty reads "no litigation, except as Disclosed". The dispute was disclosed with its nature and scope, so the statement was true. The seller's expectations are irrelevant. The diminution answer would be right for an undisclosed claim. You should have taken a price reduction or a specific indemnity. Disclosure is the moment risk moves to you, so every specific disclosure needs a decision before signing.

The SPA uses the fair disclosure standard and deems the data room disclosed. Among 4,000 documents is a board minute with one line: "The board noted recent correspondence with the regulator." Nothing else refers to it. The correspondence was a threat to revoke the company's main licence. After closing the licence is suspended. Does the disclosure defeat your claim under the compliance warranty?

  • Yes: the data room was disclosed and the minute was in it
  • Yes: your advisers had access, so you had constructive knowledge
  • No: the minute does not let a reasonable buyer identify the nature and scope of the matter
  • No: general disclosures never qualify warranties

Under fair disclosure the test is what the disclosed material tells a reasonable buyer, not whether a clue existed. One line that would require further enquiry reveals neither nature (a revocation threat) nor scope (the main licence). General disclosures can be effective, so the last option overstates it. If the SPA had deemed you to know everything "contained in or referred to in" the data room with no fairness test, the seller's argument would be much stronger. The definition of "Disclosed" decides the case.

What the buyer knew: sandbagging

Sandbagging means claiming for a breach you already knew about when you signed. Three drafting positions exist:

  • Pro-sandbagging: "The Buyer's rights shall not be affected by any investigation made by it or any knowledge it has." The buyer's view: I paid for the warranty, and my diligence should not reduce it.
  • Anti-sandbagging: "The Buyer may not claim to the extent its deal team had actual knowledge of the relevant facts at signing." The seller's view: you knew, you priced it, you cannot be paid twice.
  • Silent: the governing law decides.

English law has a tension here. The contract may say knowledge is irrelevant, but the damages rule asks what you lost, and a seller will argue that a buyer who knew the truth priced it in and lost nothing. Do not treat a pro-sandbagging clause in an English-law SPA as fully reliable for things you actually knew. In the US the answer varies by state. The ABA 2025 study found 68% of deals silent, 28% pro-sandbagging and 4% anti-sandbagging.

"If we know about it, we should not be relying on a warranty for it. Let's move it to a specific indemnity or take it off the price."
UAE deals of any size are usually written under English, DIFC or ADGM law, so the English analysis above applies. If onshore UAE law governs, do not assume these clauses work as drafted: ask counsel how the courts treat buyer knowledge and contractual limits.

English-law SPA, silent on buyer's knowledge. Your own diligence report flagged that a key licence had lapsed. The seller did not disclose it. You said nothing, signed at full price, and now claim under the unqualified licences warranty. What is the most accurate assessment?

  • The claim succeeds: contract liability is strict and the buyer's knowledge is legally irrelevant
  • The claim is barred: a silent SPA is treated as anti-sandbagging
  • There is a breach, but recovery is uncertain: the seller will argue you knew, priced it in and suffered no loss
  • The claim succeeds only if you prove the seller knew as well

The warranty was untrue and not disclosed, so there is a breach. But English damages compensate loss, and a buyer who knew the truth when agreeing the price has a hard time proving it overpaid. Neither automatic outcome is right. The seller's knowledge is irrelevant to an unqualified warranty. The practical lesson: silence was the wrong tactic. Raising the point would have earned a price reduction or an indemnity, both of which are certain.

Repetition at closing, and the positions in one table

Warranties speak at signing. If closing is weeks later, the buyer wants them repeated at closing, so that events in the gap are the seller's risk. The seller replies that it cannot warrant the future and asks for the right to update the disclosure letter at closing. If updated disclosure qualifies the repeated warranties, repetition gives you nothing: every new problem is disclosed away. The outcomes to look for: (a) no repetition, (b) repetition with updated disclosure that removes the claim, sometimes with a right to walk away if the new matter is serious, or (c) repetition with updated disclosure for information only.

US-style deals add a bring-down condition: the buyer need not close unless the representations are still true at closing, usually tested "in all material respects" or subject to a material adverse effect standard. That is a walk right, separate from the damages claim.

Warranties are repeated at closing. The SPA lets the seller deliver an updated disclosure letter at closing, effective against the repeated warranties, covering events after signing. There is no termination right. In the gap, the largest customer gives notice to terminate. The seller discloses it in the update. What is your position?

  • You can refuse to close, because a repeated warranty is untrue
  • You must close and can then claim under the repeated contracts warranty
  • You must close and have no warranty claim: the gap risk is yours
  • You must close, but the price reduces automatically by the lost profit

Repetition creates a claim only for what is not disclosed, and the update disclosed it. A right to walk exists only if the SPA gives one (a condition or termination right), and here it does not. Nothing in a share purchase agreement adjusts the price automatically without a mechanism. This combination looks like protection and delivers none. The buyer needed either updated disclosure "for information only" or a walk right.

Think first: the seller offers you a choice. Either an unqualified IP warranty with data-room disclosure, or a knowledge-qualified IP warranty with specific disclosure only. Which do you take for a software target?

Compare what each leaves with you. Option 1 leaves you the risk of anything in the data room you failed to understand, but the seller bears everything truly unknown. Option 2 leaves you the risk of everything the seller's named people did not know, which for IP infringement by third-party code is most of the real risk. For a software target the unknown-unknowns are the danger, so option 1 is usually better, provided you insist on fair disclosure and your technical team reads the IP folders properly. The general method: for each package, list the fact patterns where you end up uncompensated, then weigh them by likelihood and size.

Think first: why is an EBITDA misstatement of AED 0.5m more dangerous to a seller than an undisclosed AED 2m lawsuit?

Because damages follow the price difference. Recurring profit was bought at a multiple, so 0.5m at 10 times is 5m. The lawsuit is a one-off and contingent, so it is discounted, not multiplied. Sellers therefore fight hardest over the accounts warranties and try, in US-style deals, to exclude multiples from recoverable losses.

Key terms

Warranty
A contractual statement of fact about the target which, if untrue and undisclosed, entitles the buyer to damages for the resulting loss.
Fundamental warranties
The warranties on title to the shares and the seller's capacity to sell, treated with higher caps and longer time limits than business warranties.
Diminution in value
The English-law measure of warranty damages: the value of the shares as warranted minus their actual value.
Knowledge qualifier
Wording that limits a warranty to what specified people knew (actual) or ought to have known after reasonable enquiry (constructive).
Materiality qualifier
Wording that makes only significant inaccuracies a breach of the warranty.
Materiality scrape
A clause disregarding materiality qualifiers for calculating loss only (single) or for both establishing breach and calculating loss (double).
Disclosure letter
The seller's letter listing general and specific exceptions to the warranties, which prevent a claim for the matters disclosed.
Disclosure schedules
The US-style equivalent of the disclosure letter: exceptions listed section by section as part of the agreement.
Fair disclosure
The standard that a disclosure counts only if it gives enough detail for a reasonable buyer to identify the nature and scope of the matter.
Sandbagging
A buyer claiming for a warranty breach it knew about before signing, permitted by a pro-sandbagging clause and barred by an anti-sandbagging clause.
Repetition (bring-down)
Restating the warranties at closing so that they also cover events between signing and closing. In US-style deals their accuracy at closing is also a condition to closing.
Several liability
Each seller is liable only for its own share of a claim.
Joint and several liability
The buyer may recover the whole claim from any one seller, who must then seek contribution from the others.
Representation
A statement of fact that induced the contract and which, if false, can give misrepresentation remedies under English law, including rescission.
Rescission
Unwinding a contract so the parties are returned to their positions before it was made.
