Everything else that bites: restrictive covenants, boilerplate, ancillary documents, after closing

After this module you can name the clause that decides a given post-closing dispute, test a restrictive covenant for enforceability, compute a claim deadline, and say what each ancillary document in a closing bundle is for.

Restrictive covenants: protecting the goodwill you paid for

Much of the price of a business is often goodwill: the value of its customer relationships, reputation and team, over and above its hard assets. Goodwill lives in people's heads. A seller who takes your money and opens a competing shop next door walks off with what you bought. Restrictive covenants are the seller's promises not to do that for a period after closing:

  • Non-compete: the seller will not carry on or invest in a competing business.
  • Non-solicit of customers: the seller will not approach the target's customers to win their business.
  • Non-solicit of employees (non-poach): the seller will not hire away the target's key staff.
  • Confidentiality: the seller will not use or disclose the target's confidential information.

The enforceability logic matters more than the list. Courts dislike contracts that stop people trading. The common-law starting point is that a restraint is void unless it protects a legitimate interest and goes no further than reasonably necessary. Three dials are tested: scope (which activities), geography (where) and duration (how long). The benchmark is the business actually sold, at the time of sale. Courts are more generous to covenants given by a seller of a business (who was paid for the goodwill and negotiated as an equal) than to covenants given by an employee.

The consequence is counter-intuitive: asking for more can leave you with nothing. If a covenant is too wide, many courts will not rewrite it down to a reasonable size. They strike it out. Some will delete a severable offending phrase, some will rewrite, some will not touch it at all. This varies by governing law, so the drafting uses separate, independent sub-clauses so that the loss of one does not kill the rest. The reference course gives typical durations of one to three years, with enforceability depending on reasonableness under the governing law.

The Seller shall not, for 24 months after Completion, in the Territory, be engaged or interested in any business that competes with the Business as carried on at Completion. Each undertaking in this clause is a separate undertaking, and if any is held unenforceable the others remain in force.

Decode: scope is tied to the business as it was at closing, not whatever the buyer does later. "Territory" will be a defined list of places where the target actually trades. The last sentence is the severability device.

A buyer acquires a catering company that operates only in Dubai and Abu Dhabi. The SPA non-compete is a single sentence: the seller shall not engage in "any food or hospitality business anywhere in the world for 10 years". Two years later the seller opens a rival caterer in Dubai. The governing law follows the strict common-law approach (courts strike down, they do not rewrite). Likely outcome?

  • The buyer wins: the seller is doing exactly what the clause forbids
  • The court reduces the covenant to Dubai catering for three years and enforces it
  • The buyer is at serious risk of losing: the covenant goes far beyond what protects the goodwill sold, and a court that will not rewrite it may strike the whole restraint
  • The buyer wins because the seller was paid for goodwill, so reasonableness is not tested

The seller's conduct is plainly within a reasonable restraint, which is what makes this painful. Reasonableness is tested on the clause as drafted, not on the breach that happened. Worldwide, ten years and "hospitality" all exceed the business sold, and a single-sentence covenant gives the court nothing to sever. Seller covenants get more latitude than employee ones, but they are still tested. Some laws would cut it down: you ask local counsel which kind you are under.

Before reading on: a private equity fund is selling. Why will it refuse a non-compete almost regardless of price, and what does the buyer take instead?

A fund's business is owning companies, and its documents may stop it restricting future investments. A non-compete binding "the Seller's group" could bar the fund's other portfolio companies and its next acquisition. The buyer normally settles for non-solicits of customers and staff plus confidentiality from the fund, and takes real non-competes from the individual managers, who carry the relationships anyway.

Guarantees: who stands behind the promises

An SPA is only as good as the party who signed it. Sellers often sell through a holding company that will be an empty shell the day after it distributes the price. Buyers often buy through a newly formed company with no assets. A guarantee is a promise by a third party with real assets to perform or pay if the contracting party does not.

  • Seller's guarantee: the seller's parent or ultimate owner guarantees the seller's obligations (warranty claims, indemnities, restrictive covenants). The alternatives you already know do the same job: escrow, retention, W&I insurance.
  • Buyer's guarantee: the buyer's parent guarantees payment of the price, deferred consideration and earn-out. A fund-backed buyer usually offers an equity commitment letter instead: the fund's written promise to put the money into the buying vehicle.

The buyer wants the guarantor to be liable as a primary debtor so that the guarantee survives changes to the SPA. The seller's side wants the guarantor's liability capped at the same limits as the seller's. It usually lands there.

Boilerplate, part 1: what the deal is and who can enforce it

Boilerplate is the set of standard clauses at the back of every contract. People skip them. They decide a large share of real disputes, because they answer the procedural questions that arise before anyone reaches the merits.

Entire agreement. The written contract is the whole deal: nothing said or sent before signing is a term. Purpose: certainty. Without it, every management presentation and email is a candidate promise.

Non-reliance. The buyer confirms it has not relied on any statement outside the contract. This is aimed at a different claim: not "that was a term" but "you induced me to sign by a false statement" (misrepresentation), which could otherwise escape the SPA's limits. The two clauses work as a pair. Neither protects against fraud: the law does not allow a party to exclude liability for its own fraud.

This Agreement constitutes the entire agreement between the parties. The Buyer acknowledges that it has not relied on any statement, representation or warranty other than the Warranties. Nothing in this clause limits liability for fraud.

Exclusive remedy. The buyer's only remedy for a breach of warranty is a damages claim under the SPA, subject to its limits. No rescission (unwinding the deal), no claims in tort (non-contractual claims such as negligence or misrepresentation). Without this, the cap and time limits you studied could be bypassed by relabelling the claim.

During a management presentation the seller's CEO said next year's revenue "will be at least AED 90m". It came in at AED 60m. The SPA contains no warranty about forecasts, and contains the clause quoted above. The buyer sues for the shortfall. There is no evidence the CEO disbelieved the forecast. Which clause decides the case, and for whom?

  • The exclusive remedy clause, for the buyer
  • The entire agreement and non-reliance clauses, for the seller
  • The further assurance clause, for the buyer
  • The warranty cap, for the seller

The statement is outside the contract, so the entire agreement clause stops it being a term and the non-reliance clause blocks a misrepresentation claim. The cap never comes into play because there is no warranty to breach. The fraud exception would reopen the door only if the CEO knew the statement was false. Lesson for the buyer: if a statement matters to your price, get it into the warranties.

Further assurance. Each party will sign whatever further documents are needed to give effect to the deal. It rescues transfers that turn out incomplete (an overlooked trade mark, a registry form). It cannot be used to add new commercial obligations.

Assignment. To assign a contract right is to transfer the right to sue on it to someone else. The default clause prohibits assignment without consent. The buyer needs two exceptions. First, its lender will want the warranty rights assigned as security, so that the bank can pursue the seller if the buyer defaults. Second, the buyer may later sell the target and want to pass the warranties to the next owner. Sellers accept the first routinely and resist the second, and always add that the assignee can recover no more than the original buyer could have.

The trap: warranties belong to the buyer as a contracting party. They are not attached to the target's shares. A later purchaser of the target gets nothing from the original SPA unless the rights were validly assigned to it.

Third-party rights. The basic rule (privity) is that only parties to a contract can enforce it. Some laws let a third party enforce a term written for its benefit, so the SPA normally switches that off, with named exceptions (for example, outgoing directors who are promised a release).

Buyer A buys Target from Seller under an SPA that prohibits assignment without the Seller's consent, except to A's lenders by way of security. Two years later A sells Target to Buyer B. There is no assignment of the first SPA. B then discovers a breach of the Seller's tax warranties, still within the time limit. Who can sue the Seller under the first SPA?

  • B, because the warranties run with the shares
  • Target itself, because it suffered the tax loss
  • Only A (or its lender if the security assignment was enforced): B has no rights under a contract it is not party to and that was not assigned to it
  • Nobody, because the sale of Target extinguished the warranties

Privity plus the assignment clause. B's remedy is against A, under the warranties A gave B in the second SPA. A can still sue the Seller in principle, though it will have to show its own loss (for example, what it paid B). Target was never a party. Warranties do not travel with shares, which is exactly why a buyer planning a resale negotiates a right to assign.

Boilerplate, part 2: money and mechanics

  • Costs. Each party pays its own advisers. It stops a failed deal turning into a fee claim, and stops the target (soon the buyer's) paying the seller's bills, which would be leakage.
  • Notices. How a formal communication must be sent (to whom, at which address, by which method) and when it is deemed received.
  • Counterparts and e-signature. Each party may sign a separate copy, and electronic signatures count. This is what allows a closing with signatories in five cities.
  • Set-off. Set-off is deducting what the other side owes you from what you owe them. A buyer who owes deferred consideration and holds a warranty claim wants to net them. Sellers insert "all payments shall be made without set-off", so the buyer must pay in full and sue separately.
  • Gross-up. If a payment under the SPA is taxed or subject to withholding, the payer increases it so the recipient keeps the intended amount.
  • Default interest. A stated rate on late payments, so that delay is not free financing.
  • Severability. If one clause is invalid, the rest survives.
  • Variation. The contract can be changed only in writing signed by all parties. It blocks claims that a deadline was extended on a phone call.
  • Waiver. Not enforcing a right once, or being slow, does not give the right up.

Notice mechanics defeat more claims than any substantive defence. The limitation regime you learned has two clocks: a deadline to notify a claim, and usually a second deadline to issue and serve court or arbitration proceedings after notifying, failing which the claim is treated as withdrawn. A notice that goes to the wrong address, by a method the clause does not list, or without the detail the clause requires, may be no notice at all. Courts enforce these clauses strictly because the parties chose a bright line.

The Seller shall not be liable for a Claim unless the Buyer gives written notice of it, setting out in reasonable detail the nature of the Claim and the amount claimed, within 30 days of becoming aware of it and in any event within 18 months after Completion. Any Claim so notified shall be deemed withdrawn unless proceedings are issued and served within six months of the notice.

Decode: three gates, all of which must be passed. Awareness plus 30 days. Completion plus 18 months. Notice plus six months to start proceedings. Miss any one and a good claim is worth zero.

The clause above applies. Completion was 15 January 2026. The buyer's finance team became aware of a warranty breach on 1 June 2027. The buyer sent a compliant notice on 10 July 2027. Count day 1 as 2 June. By how many days did the buyer miss a binding deadline? Answer in days (enter 0 if it was in time).

Two notice deadlines apply. The 18-month backstop expires 15 July 2027: met, with 5 days to spare. The awareness deadline is 30 days from 1 June: June has 30 days, so it expires 1 July 2027. The notice went on 10 July: 9 days late. Because the clause says the seller "shall not be liable unless" both are met, the claim is barred. People check the backstop and forget the awareness clock.

Another deal: general warranty claims must be notified within 24 months of completion, and proceedings issued and served within 9 months of the notice. The buyer notifies a claim exactly 20 months after completion. What is the latest point, in months after completion, at which proceedings can be issued and served? Answer in months.

The second clock runs from the notice, not from the end of the warranty period: 20 + 9 = 29 months. The tempting answer is 33 (24 + 9). Notifying early starts the litigation clock early, which is why buyers sometimes hold a notice until the claim is well prepared, so long as no awareness deadline forces their hand.

The notices clause says notices must be delivered by hand or courier to the seller's registered office, marked for the General Counsel. On the last day of the warranty period the buyer's lawyer emails a detailed claim letter to the seller's CEO, who reads it that day. The SPA has a standard waiver clause. Is the claim notified in time?

  • Yes: the seller had actual knowledge, which is the purpose of a notice clause
  • Probably not: the contract defines what counts as notice, email is not a permitted method, and actual knowledge does not cure it
  • Yes: the counterparts and e-signature clause permits electronic communication
  • Yes: the severability clause removes the defective delivery requirement

A contractual notice requirement is a condition the parties designed, and courts generally hold them to it. Purposive arguments ("he read it") are weak against a clear clause. E-signature clauses are about how the contract is signed, not how notices are served. The practical rule: serve early, by every permitted method, exactly as the clause says.

The seller owes the buyer AED 1,000,000 under an indemnity. The SPA has a gross-up clause. Assume, for this question only, that the receipt is taxed at 20% in the buyer's hands. How much must the seller pay so that the buyer keeps AED 1,000,000 after tax? Answer in AED.

Solve P × (1 − 0.20) = 1,000,000. P = 1,000,000 / 0.8 = AED 1,250,000. The common error is adding 20% to get 1,200,000, which leaves the buyer with only 960,000. Whether such a receipt is taxed at all depends on the tax law in question: the clause allocates the risk whichever way that falls.

The buyer owes the seller AED 10m of deferred consideration on 1 March. In February the buyer notifies a AED 4m warranty claim, which the seller disputes. The SPA says "all sums payable under this Agreement shall be paid in full without set-off or counterclaim". The buyer pays AED 6m. What follows?

  • Nothing: a party may always net mutual debts
  • The buyer is in breach for AED 4m and owes default interest on it, and must pursue its warranty claim separately
  • The buyer is protected because it gave notice of the claim before the payment date
  • The AED 4m is automatically held in escrow until the claim is decided

The no set-off clause exists for exactly this moment. It keeps the seller's cash certain and makes the buyer carry the cost and delay of proving its claim. A buyer who wants to withhold must negotiate an express right to set off (often limited to claims that are agreed or supported by a barrister's opinion) or an escrow. Nothing is held back automatically.

Governing law and dispute resolution

Two separate choices. Governing law is the legal system whose rules interpret the contract. Dispute resolution is who decides and where.

  • Courts. Public, with appeals, and able to join third parties. A judgment is enforced abroad only where the other country's rules or a treaty allow it.
  • Arbitration. A private tribunal chosen by the parties, usually confidential, with almost no appeal. Its decision (an award) is enforceable in most countries under a widely adopted international convention, which is the main reason cross-border deals use it. The clause must fix the seat (the legal home of the arbitration, whose courts supervise it and whose law governs the procedure), the institution whose rules apply (for example ICC, LCIA, DIAC), the number of arbitrators and the language.
  • Expert determination. For accounting disagreements (completion accounts, earn-out calculations), an independent accountant decides quickly as an expert, and the result is final except for manifest error (an obvious mistake). It is the right tool for "what is the number" and the wrong tool for "what does the contract mean".

Split clauses are dangerous in two ways. If the SPA says arbitration and the escrow agreement or tax deed says courts, one dispute can run in two forums with inconsistent results. And a single clause that mixes mechanisms carelessly ("disputes shall be referred to arbitration or the courts of X") may be too uncertain to enforce at all. Every document in the bundle should carry the same clause, and the boundary between expert and tribunal must be drawn expressly.

From the reference course: asymmetric dispute clauses (one side may choose between forums, the other may not) have been held invalid onshore. The DIFC-LCIA arbitration centre was abolished in September 2021 and its cases moved to DIAC, yet old clauses naming it still turn up in due diligence. Treat any dispute clause touching the UAE as a point to ask counsel about.

After closing, the buyer's draft completion accounts value inventory AED 3m lower than the seller thinks right. The seller says the buyer used a provisioning method not found in the SPA's accounting policies schedule. The SPA sends "disputes on the Completion Accounts" to an independent accountant as expert, and "all other disputes" to arbitration. The buyer files for arbitration. Best analysis?

  • Arbitration is right: any dispute worth AED 3m belongs before a tribunal
  • It belongs with the expert, as a dispute on the Completion Accounts, although the seller may argue that the meaning of the policies schedule is a legal question, which is why good clauses say who decides that boundary
  • Neither: completion accounts become final once the buyer delivers them
  • The courts, because accounting disputes cannot be arbitrated

The clause routes by subject, not size. Inventory valuation in the completion accounts is the expert's core job. The real-world fight is at the edge: is "which policy applies" accounting or contract interpretation? A well-drafted clause either gives the expert power to decide points of interpretation needed for the number, or carves them out. Draft accounts become final only if the seller fails to object within its review period.

Ancillary documents: the rest of the bundle

The SPA is the hub. Ancillary documents are the other agreements signed with it. Each exists because one job does not fit inside the SPA.

Before reading on: in a carve-out, the seller offers a TSA for 6 months. The buyer's CTO (you) thinks migration off the seller's ERP takes 10. What do you ask for, and what will the seller say?

Ask for 12 months with a buyer option to extend, service levels equal to what the business received before the sale, and migration assistance. The seller will say it is not a service provider, wants out of the old business, and prices extensions at a rising premium to push you off. Usual landing: a term near your realistic estimate, a short extension right at a stepped-up price, and "same standard as the prior 12 months" as the service level.

After closing: where deals actually end up in dispute

Completion accounts. The SPA sets a timetable: the buyer (who now controls the books) prepares draft accounts within a fixed period, the seller has a review period to accept or serve a dispute notice listing each item and amount, the parties negotiate, and unresolved items go to the expert. Silence in the review period means deemed acceptance. Disputes come from accounting judgement: provisions, cut-off, inventory. The defence is specificity. SRS Acquiom 2026 reports that 39% of working-capital adjustments use an agreed worksheet methodology in place of a generic reference to accounting standards.

Earn-outs. The buyer runs the business while the seller's payment depends on its results. The seller needs information rights and conduct covenants (run the business in good faith, no diverting revenue, no loading costs). The buyer needs freedom to integrate. The reference course calls earn-outs the biggest source of post-closing disputes.

Warranty claims. The process is the three gates above, plus conduct rules: if a third party sues the target, who controls the defence, and the buyer's duty to mitigate.

Escrow release. On the release date the agent pays the seller the balance less amounts for claims notified and unresolved. Expect a dispute over how much a pending claim may lock up.

Integration. Systems, contracts and people are merged. Legally this is where TSA exits, novations (transfers of a contract to a new party with the counterparty's consent) and post-closing covenants are tracked, and where they are missed.

It is widely observed in practice that price mechanics (completion accounts and earn-outs) generate far more post-closing disputes than warranty claims. The mechanism explains it: a price adjustment happens on every deal that has one, the amounts are certain to be computed, both sides have accountants ready, and there is no cap or basket in the way. A warranty claim needs an unknown problem to surface, survive disclosure, clear the limits and meet the notice gates.

"Before we argue about the cap, can we look at the accounting policies schedule and the earn-out covenants? That is where I would expect the money to move after closing."
Before reading on: why would a buyer prefer a dispute about the completion accounts to a warranty claim for the same AED 2m?

A completion accounts adjustment is pound for pound, with no de minimis, basket or cap, no disclosure defence and no need to prove breach or loss: only that the number is wrong under the agreed policies. It is decided by an expert in weeks. This is why sellers insist that the buyer cannot recover the same item twice, and why sellers fight hard over which items sit in the accounts.

An earn-out pays the seller 5 times any EBITDA above AED 20m in year one. After closing the buyer moves the target's best sales team into its own subsidiary, and the target's EBITDA comes in at AED 19m. The SPA has no earn-out conduct covenants, an entire agreement clause, and gives the buyer "full discretion to operate the business". The seller sues. What decides it?

  • The warranty limitations: the seller's claim is capped
  • The restrictive covenants, which stop the buyer competing with the target
  • The absence of earn-out protection covenants, combined with the buyer's express discretion: the seller has little to stand on unless the governing law implies a duty it can use
  • The further assurance clause, which obliges the buyer to maximise the earn-out

Warranty limits protect the seller, not the buyer, and restrictive covenants bind the seller. The seller's protection has to be written: ring-fencing, no diversion of business, an adjustment if the buyer restructures. Some legal systems imply good-faith limits on a discretion, which is a point for counsel, but relying on it is a weak position. Further assurance only perfects what was agreed.

Six months after closing the buyer discovers that a key software licence, which the SPA required the seller to have transferred into the target before closing, is still held by the seller's founder personally. The IP warranty is breached, but the loss is hard to quantify and the claim would fall under the basket. What is the buyer's most useful clause?

  • The exclusive remedy clause
  • The further assurance clause, to require the seller to procure the transfer of the licence so that the buyer gets what was sold
  • The set-off clause
  • The variation clause

The buyer does not want damages, it wants the asset. Further assurance obliges the seller to do what is needed to give full effect to the sale, which is the route to an IP assignment after the event. A damages claim would be cut by the basket. Exclusive remedy points the other way: it restricts the buyer.

Key terms

Goodwill
The value of a business above its identifiable assets, made up of customer relationships, reputation and workforce.
Restrictive covenants
Post-closing promises by the seller not to compete, solicit customers or staff, or misuse confidential information, enforceable only so far as reasonable.
Guarantee
A third party's promise to perform or pay a contracting party's obligations if that party does not.
Entire agreement clause
A clause stating that the written contract is the whole deal, so that earlier statements are not terms.
Non-reliance clause
A confirmation that a party did not rely on statements outside the contract, aimed at blocking misrepresentation claims other than fraud.
Exclusive remedy clause
A clause limiting the buyer to damages under the SPA and excluding rescission and non-contractual claims.
Assignment
The transfer to another person of the right to enforce a contract.
Privity
The rule that only the parties to a contract can enforce it.
Set-off
Deducting a sum the other party owes you from a sum you owe it.
Gross-up
An increase in a payment so that the recipient keeps the intended amount after tax or withholding.
Seat of arbitration
The legal home of an arbitration, whose law governs the procedure and whose courts supervise it.
Expert determination
A fast, final decision on a technical question, typically accounting, by an independent expert and not by a court or tribunal.
Transitional services agreement (TSA)
A contract under which the seller's group keeps supplying shared services to a carved-out business for a limited period after closing.
Shareholders' agreement (SHA)
The contract between a company's owners governing control, share transfers and exit.
Power of attorney (POA)
A document authorising one person to sign or act on behalf of another.
