Due diligence: turning unknowns into price, protection or a walk

After this module you can take any diligence finding and route it to the one contractual outcome that fits it, and explain why the other four do not.

Why diligence exists

The seller has run the business for years. The buyer has seen it for weeks. That gap is information asymmetry, and it creates the standard problem: a buyer who cannot tell good companies from bad ones must assume the worst and discount every price.

The legal starting point makes it sharper. Under caveat emptor ("buyer beware"), the common-law default for a sale of shares, the buyer has no protection it did not bargain for. The seller has no general duty to volunteer problems. It must not lie, but it may stay silent. So anything the buyer does not find, and does not get a contractual promise about, is the buyer's loss.

Due diligence (DD) is the buyer's investigation of the target before it commits. It is not an audit and it does not aim at completeness. Its output is a list of decisions: what to pay, what protection to demand, what must be fixed first, and whether to proceed at all.

Before reading on: if the seller will give warranties anyway, why spend money on diligence?

Three reasons. A warranty claim pays damages years later, after a fight, subject to financial limits, and only if the seller still has money: finding the problem now and cutting the price is worth far more. Some problems are fatal to the reason for the deal, and no payment fixes them. And warranties are negotiated: you need to know where the risks are to know which warranties to insist on. Diligence and warranties are complements. Diligence handles what can be found; warranties handle what cannot.

The streams overlap, and findings in one change the meaning of findings in another. An unpaid tax liability found by the tax team becomes a debt-like item for the accountants and a clause for the lawyers. Someone must hold the whole picture: usually the lead M&A lawyer and the buyer's deal lead.

The machinery that feeds those workstreams:

  • Data room (virtual data room, VDR): a secure online folder structure where the seller uploads documents against an index. The seller controls what goes in, when, and who sees it. Sensitive documents may be redacted or shown only to a restricted clean team (named individuals, often outside advisers, allowed to see competitively sensitive data).
  • Q&A process: the buyer submits written questions through the data room; the seller answers in writing. Sellers cap the number of questions in auctions. The log matters later: answers can become part of what the seller has disclosed.
  • Management presentation: the target's executives present the business and take questions. It is the buyer's chance to test people, and the seller's lawyers attend to stop executives making statements the seller will be held to.
  • Vendor due diligence (VDD) report: a diligence report commissioned by the seller from its own advisers and given to bidders, common in auctions. It speeds up the process and lets the seller frame the issues first. The problem is reliance: the adviser's duty is owed to its client, the seller. A bidder can sue over a negligent report only if the adviser has agreed the bidder may rely on it, through a reliance letter, normally given only to the winning bidder and with the adviser's liability capped.

In an auction you receive a legal VDD report prepared by the seller's law firm. It says there is no material litigation. You win, close, and discover a large claim that was pending at the time. You never received a reliance letter. Against whom do you most plausibly have a claim?

  • The seller's law firm, for a negligent report
  • The seller, but only if the SPA contains a litigation warranty and the seller did not formally tell you of the claim as an exception to it
  • Both, jointly
  • The seller, for misrepresentation in the VDD report

Without a reliance letter the law firm owes you no duty: the report was written for the seller. The SPA will say you did not rely on anything outside it, which closes off the report as a basis of claim against the seller, absent fraud. What remains is the contract: a warranty. This is caveat emptor operating as designed. A VDD report without reliance is information, not protection.

Before reading on: you are a CTO. Why is "the company owns its code" a legal question and not a technical one?

Because authorship and ownership are different things. The default rule in many legal systems is that a contractor, unlike an employee, owns what they create unless they assign it in writing. So code can be in the company's repository, written to its specification and paid for, and still belong to someone else. The check is documentary: for each significant contributor, is there a signed assignment? Technical diligence tells you the code works. Legal diligence tells you the buyer can stop others using it and cannot itself be stopped.

Scope, materiality and the red flag report

A mid-sized target has thousands of documents. Reviewing all of them equally is slow, expensive and buries the problems. So scope is cut by materiality thresholds: only contracts above a value (say, the top 20 customers and suppliers, or contracts above AED 1m a year), only litigation above an amount, only the last three to five years.

The output is normally a red flag report: an exceptions-only report listing issues that could affect price, protection or the decision, each with a recommendation. The alternative, a descriptive report summarising every document, costs several times more and tells the buyer mostly what is normal. A red flag report is a filter tuned for decisions.

The threshold is a risk decision, not an administrative one. Whatever falls below it is unreviewed, and the buyer must cover it some other way, usually by warranties. Thresholds should follow the deal thesis: if you are buying a company for one patent family, IP is reviewed to the bottom whatever its book value.

A buyer is acquiring a SaaS company with 4,000 customers on standard online terms, 12 enterprise customers on negotiated contracts making up 55% of revenue, and one reseller agreement, which has produced almost no revenue so far, that grants the reseller exclusivity "in the Middle East for all current and future products of the Company and its Affiliates". The budget allows detailed review of 15 contracts. Which scope is best?

  • The 15 largest contracts by revenue
  • A random sample of 15 of the 4,000 standard-terms customers, plus the template
  • The 12 enterprise contracts, the standard template, and the reseller agreement, with the remaining slot left for whatever those reveal
  • The 12 enterprise contracts and three randomly chosen others

Materiality is not only revenue. The reseller contract is small by value but binds "Affiliates", which after closing includes the buyer's whole group: it could block the buyer's own products in the region. The template covers 4,000 customers in one read. The top-15-by-revenue answer misses the reseller entirely, because it earns almost nothing. Scope by what can hurt the deal thesis, then by size.

The core skill: route every finding to one of five outcomes

A finding with no consequence in the contract or the price was a waste of fees. Each one goes to exactly one primary home.

The decision rule, in order: Is it fatal? Walk. Can it be fixed before closing? Make it a condition. Is it known and can you put a number on it? Price. Known but you cannot? Indemnity. Not known at all? Warranty.

Two refinements make this real.

First, a warranty does not protect against a known problem. The seller will disclose the problem against the warranty (next section), and a disclosed matter cannot found a claim. Insurers take the same view: warranty insurance covers unknown breaches only and excludes what diligence found. So known issues need price or an indemnity.

Second, an indemnity is only as good as the person giving it. If the seller is a fund that will distribute the proceeds and dissolve, or an individual moving abroad, a promise to pay later has little value. The fix is to back the indemnity with an escrow, or to convert it to a price reduction now. Sellers prefer an indemnity to a price cut because the loss may never arise; buyers prefer price because it is certain. An indemnity with escrow is the usual compromise.

The Seller shall indemnify the Buyer against all Losses suffered or incurred by the Buyer or the Company arising out of or in connection with the Al Noor Claim, including any settlement approved by the Seller (such approval not to be unreasonably withheld).

Decode: one named problem, reimbursement of whatever it costs, and a seller veto over cheap settlements paid with the seller's money.

Price was agreed at 8 × EBITDA. Financial diligence shows that the largest customer renegotiated its contract last month, cutting the target's annual profit by AED 1.5m permanently. Tax diligence finds AED 2m of unpaid tax for a prior year, undisputed. What total price reduction should the buyer seek? Answer in AED m.

The contract repricing is recurring, so it reduces EBITDA and is multiplied: 8 × 1.5 = 12. The tax is a one-off known, quantified liability: 2, as a debt-like item. Total 14. Both route to price because both are known and quantifiable; the only question was which goes through the multiple.

A former distributor is suing the target for AED 10m. The seller's lawyers rate the claim as weak. The seller offers a warranty that "there is no litigation against the Company other than as disclosed", and discloses the claim. How is the buyer protected?

  • Fully: if the claim succeeds the warranty is breached
  • Partly: it can claim damages above the disclosed amount
  • Not at all on this claim: it needs a specific indemnity
  • Not at all, and nothing can be done because the claim is known

The warranty is true: there is no litigation other than as disclosed. Disclosure removes the claim from the warranty's reach. The matter is known but unquantified (it may cost zero or 10m), which is the indemnity case. A price cut of 10m overpays for a weak claim; the seller would refuse it. The last option confuses "known" with "unprotectable": knowledge changes the tool, not the availability of protection.

The target's core algorithm was written four years ago by a contractor. There is no written IP assignment. The contractor is friendly, still does occasional work for the company and lives locally. Best route?

  • Warranty that the company owns all its IP
  • Price reduction equal to the cost of rewriting the algorithm
  • Condition precedent: a signed assignment from the contractor before closing
  • Walk away

It is fixable, cheaply, now: one signature removes the risk. Fixing beats pricing whenever it is available. A warranty is the wrong tool for a known defect (it will be disclosed) and damages would arrive after a competitor has licensed the code. A rewrite-cost price cut leaves the buyer owning a product it has no right to. Walking is premature.

Same facts, but the contractor emigrated, cannot be traced, and the algorithm is the reason the buyer wants the company. The seller offers an uncapped indemnity for any IP claim by the contractor. Best advice?

  • Accept the indemnity: uncapped cover is the strongest protection available
  • Take a price reduction equal to the expected value of a claim
  • Treat it as potentially fatal: money cannot give the buyer ownership, so either the seller establishes title another way or the buyer should not proceed on the current thesis
  • Rely on an IP ownership warranty backed by warranty insurance

An indemnity pays cash. The risk here is not a cost but the loss of exclusive rights to the core asset: the contractor could license it to a competitor or stop the buyer using it. No payment restores that. The same finding routes differently when it stops being fixable and goes to the heart of the deal. Insurance excludes known issues. The honest answer to an ambiguous case is to name what would make it acceptable (for example, a legal opinion that title passed under the governing law of the engagement, or a rewrite completed before closing).

Diligence quantifies an under-accrual of employee benefits at AED 1.3m, undisputed. The seller is a PE fund in its final year, which will distribute sale proceeds to its investors and wind up. The seller proposes: "No price change, but we will give you a specific indemnity for the full amount." What should the buyer say?

  • Accept: an indemnity recovers one for one, which is better than a negotiated price cut
  • Decline: it is known and quantified, so it belongs in price, and an indemnity from an entity about to distribute its cash and dissolve is worth little
  • Accept, provided the indemnity is uncapped
  • Convert it to a warranty so that insurance can respond

Two independent reasons point to price. The rule: known plus quantifiable goes to price, because there is no uncertainty for an indemnity to cover. The counterparty: a promise from a fund that will have no assets is uncollectable, and "uncapped" adds nothing to an empty pocket. If the seller insists on an indemnity, the buyer needs the amount in escrow, which economically is a price deduction held in suspense. Insurance does not cover known issues.

The target's factory has operated on the same site for 30 years. The data room contains no environmental reports. Management says there has never been a problem. A full soil survey would take three months and the seller will not allow it in an auction. Best route?

  • Price reduction for estimated clean-up cost
  • Walk away: unverifiable risk
  • Environmental warranties, with attention to their time limit and cap, and a question whether insurance will cover them
  • Condition precedent that no contamination exists

Nothing has been found, so there is nothing to quantify, fix or indemnify specifically. This is the unknown, and the warranty is the tool built for it: it shifts the risk to the party with better information and prompts disclosure of anything the seller does know. A condition "that no contamination exists" cannot be verified without the survey that is unavailable. Because environmental problems surface slowly, the buyer should press for a longer claim period for this warranty than for the rest.

Compliance diligence finds that 6% of the target's revenue comes from a distributor that resells into a sanctioned country. The seller says the buyer can simply terminate that distributor after closing. The buyer is a listed group with US investors. What is the best structure?

  • Close, then terminate the distributor
  • Specific indemnity for any sanctions fines
  • Require the business to be terminated and the exposure assessed before closing, as a condition; keep an indemnity for historic breaches; walk if the assessment shows the problem is wider
  • Reduce the price by 6% of EV

This finding has three parts and each takes a different route. The ongoing conduct is fixable, so it becomes a pre-closing condition: the buyer must not own the business while it continues, since liability can attach to the new owner. The historic exposure is known but unquantified: indemnity. And the open question (is it only this distributor?) decides whether it is fatal. A 6% price cut prices lost revenue and ignores the liability. Real findings are often compound; split them before routing.

"For each red flag I would like the report to say which bucket it goes in: price, indemnity, condition, warranty cover, or deal-breaker. If it does not land in one of those, why is it in the report?"
Treating a warranty as a catch-all. "We will get a warranty on that" is the wrong answer for anything diligence has already found.

Diligence, disclosure and knowledge (preview)

The seller answers the warranties with a disclosure letter: a document listing facts that make the warranties untrue. A matter fairly disclosed cannot found a warranty claim. This is where the data room matters legally. Sellers want general disclosure of the whole data room, so that everything in it counts as disclosed. Buyers want specific disclosure only: each exception stated against the warranty it qualifies. If the whole data room is deemed disclosed, every document your team did not read is a risk you have accepted.

A related question is whether a buyer who actually knew of a problem before signing can still claim for it. Claiming despite knowledge is called sandbagging. Contracts may allow it, forbid it or say nothing; in the US sample of ABA 2025, 68% were silent, 28% expressly allowed it and 4% forbade it. The default answer when the contract is silent varies by jurisdiction. The practical consequence: what your diligence team knows can be held against you, so findings must be routed, not filed.

What AI does well and badly in diligence

CMS 2026 reports that AI tools were used in 60% of deals involving legal technology, double the previous year, with 72% of users reporting efficiency gains.

There is also a confidentiality failure mode: client documents put into consumer tools. And responsibility does not move: the UAE Ministry of Justice's 2026 guiding principles for law firms and the DIFC Courts' practice guidance both call for human review of AI output. The pattern is that AI is strong at extraction, where the answer is in the document, and weak at routing, where the answer depends on the deal thesis, the counterparty and the law. Routing is the part this module trains.

An AI tool reviews 2,000 customer contracts and reports: "No change of control clauses found." Which follow-up check addresses the most likely failure?

  • Re-run the same prompt to see whether the answer is stable
  • Check how the contracts define "assignment" and whether termination rights are triggered through other clauses or defined terms, and sample the Arabic-language contracts by hand
  • Ask the tool how confident it is
  • Accept the result: clause extraction is what these tools do well

Extraction is reliable when the concept sits in one labelled clause. The known failures are cross-references (an "assignment" definition that deems a change in ownership to be an assignment, so the anti-assignment clause does the work) and Arabic documents. Re-running or asking for confidence tests consistency, not correctness. The final option is the tempting one, and it is right about the tool's strength but wrong about where a negative result can hide.

UAE-specific diligence items. Licences and activities: every mainland and free-zone company holds a trade licence listing permitted activities; check the business does only those, in the places the licence covers. Foreign ownership history: before the foreign ownership reforms (the current companies law, in force from 2 January 2022, makes 100% foreign ownership the default outside strategic activities), most mainland companies needed a majority UAE-national shareholder, and many used nominee arrangements (a local shareholder holding on behalf of a foreign owner under side agreements). Trace how title was held and whether the unwinding was done properly. Gratuity: recompute the accrual; it is commonly under-provided. Emiratisation: private employers with 50 or more staff face rising quotas for UAE-national employees, with monthly financial contributions for unfilled posts; check compliance and accrued exposure. Arabic documents: constitutional documents, licences, court papers and many government filings are in Arabic, and the Arabic text generally prevails onshore; budget for translation and a lawyer who reads the original. Tax: VAT and the federal corporate tax are both recent, so filing histories are short and positions untested; check registrations and returns from the start of each tax.

A UAE target has 50 employees, each with exactly 7 years' service and basic pay of AED 12,000 a month. Gratuity is 21 days' basic pay per year for the first five years and 30 days per year thereafter. Take one day's pay as monthly basic pay divided by 30. The books accrue AED 40,000 per employee. What is the total under-accrual? Answer in AED.

Days: 5 × 21 + 2 × 30 = 165. Daily pay: 12,000 / 30 = 400. Entitlement: 165 × 400 = 66,000 per employee (well under the two-year cap). Shortfall per employee: 66,000 − 40,000 = 26,000. Times 50 = 1,300,000. Routing: known and quantified, so price. The full 66,000 × 50 = 3.3m is the debt-like item; 1.3m is the part the seller's own books missed.

Before reading on: the seller's team says "everything is in the data room". Why should that sentence worry a buyer?

Because it previews the disclosure position. If the SPA deems the whole data room disclosed against the warranties, then anything in those thousands of documents, read or not, cannot support a claim. A large, disorganised data room uploaded late is then a liability shield for the seller. The buyer's responses: insist on specific disclosure, fix a cut-off date for uploads, and require that disclosure be "fair", meaning in enough detail for a reasonable buyer to identify the nature and scope of the matter.

Key terms

Caveat emptor
Default rule that a buyer has no protection it did not bargain for in the contract.
Due diligence
The buyer's pre-contract investigation of the target, aimed at decisions on price, protection and whether to proceed.
Data room
Secure online repository where the seller makes documents available to bidders.
Vendor due diligence report
Diligence report commissioned by the seller and provided to bidders.
Reliance letter
Letter by which an adviser agrees that a third party may rely on its report, usually with capped liability.
Red flag report
Exceptions-only diligence report listing issues that affect price, protection or the decision to proceed.
Materiality threshold
Value or importance cut-off below which documents or issues are not reviewed or reported.
Change of control clause
Contract term giving the counterparty rights, often termination, if the company's ownership changes.
Specific indemnity
Seller's promise to reimburse an identified, known risk one for one if it results in loss.
Disclosure letter
Seller's document listing facts that qualify the warranties, preventing claims for matters disclosed.
General and specific disclosure
General disclosure deems whole categories such as the data room disclosed; specific disclosure states a fact against a particular warranty.
Sandbagging
A buyer claiming for a warranty breach it knew about before signing.
Nominee arrangement
Structure in which a registered shareholder holds shares on behalf of another under a side agreement.
Quality of earnings
Accountants' analysis of whether reported EBITDA is real and repeatable.
