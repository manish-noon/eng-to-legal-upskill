The UAE and GCC overlay

After this module you can take any SPA clause you have learned and say what changes when the target is in the UAE, run a merger control threshold check and a gratuity calculation, and know which points to ask counsel about instead of asserting.

Everything UAE-specific here comes from the reference course, which is a research summary and not legal advice. Where it says "confirm" or relies on secondary sources, this module says so too. Statute references are those given in the reference course.

Three legal systems in one country

The UAE contains three separate legal systems. Which one you are in depends on where a company is registered, not on where its office is.

SystemUAE civil lawCommon law, under the DIFC's own statutesEnglish common law applied directly
CourtsLocal and federal courts, working in ArabicDIFC Courts, working in EnglishADGM Courts, working in English

Civil law means the rules come primarily from written codes that judges apply. Common law means the rules come largely from past court decisions, and contracts are enforced closely as written. The DIFC (Dubai International Financial Centre) and ADGM (Abu Dhabi Global Market) are financial free zones with their own civil and commercial law and their own courts. Onshore or mainland means the rest of the UAE.

The three systems answer three different questions on a deal, and the answers do not have to match:

  1. Where is the target incorporated? This is a fact, not a choice. It fixes the mechanics of transferring title: which document, which registrar, which formalities. An onshore LLC's shares move only by the onshore process, whatever the SPA says.
  2. Which law governs the SPA? This is a choice. The parties can sell an onshore company under an English-law contract. The contract law decides what the warranties, limits and remedies mean.
  3. Where do disputes go, and how is the result enforced? Also a choice, and the one that makes the others real. A decision is worth only what can be enforced where the losing party's assets are.

The reference course's typical structure: an SPA under English, DIFC or ADGM law, with arbitration (DIAC, ICC or LCIA rules), plus a short-form Arabic transfer document for the notary when the target is onshore. The reasons are mechanical. Parties want a contract law under which the SPA machinery you have studied works as drafted. They want an English-language forum. They want an enforceable result: the reference notes that DIFC-seated awards can be enforced onshore through what is called the conduit route, meaning the award is taken through the DIFC Courts and from there to onshore enforcement. Ask counsel how that route is working in current practice. And the notary and licensing authority need a document in the form they accept, so the long SPA is accompanied by a short local instrument that does nothing except transfer the shares.

Two documents now describe one sale. If the short Arabic transfer instrument states a price or terms that differ from the SPA, you have created a conflict in the one document a local court will read first. The SPA should say that it prevails between the parties and that the transfer instrument exists only to effect the transfer.

One historical point appears in due diligence. The DIFC-LCIA arbitration centre was abolished in September 2021 and its cases moved to DIAC. Old contracts naming it still turn up. When you see one in a target's key contract, or in a draft handed to you, flag it for counsel.

A German buyer is acquiring 100% of an onshore Dubai LLC from two founders who live in Dubai and hold most of their assets there. The buyer wants the English-style limitation regime to work as drafted and wants to be able to enforce against the founders. Which package matches the typical structure in the reference course?

  • Onshore UAE law SPA, onshore courts, no separate transfer document
  • English (or DIFC/ADGM) law SPA, DIFC-seated arbitration, plus a short Arabic transfer instrument for the notary
  • English law SPA with German courts, and no notarial step because English law governs
  • DIFC law SPA and DIFC share transfer form, since the parties can opt the company into the DIFC

Governing law and forum are choices, the transfer mechanics are not. An onshore LLC transfers by notarised document, MOA amendment and licence update whatever law governs the SPA, so options 3 and 4 fail on mechanics. A German court judgment against Dubai-based founders raises an enforcement question that the DIFC seat is chosen to avoid. Option 1 puts the SPA under onshore law and courts, where the English-style limits may not work as drafted, which is the one thing the buyer asked for.

Civil-law surprises onshore: ask, don't assert

If onshore UAE law governs a contract, or an onshore court ends up hearing it, several things you learned may not work as drafted. The reference course lists three:

  • Agreed compensation can be adjusted. Onshore courts may adjust a sum the parties fixed in advance as compensation. A break fee, a liquidated damages figure or a fixed leakage payment is therefore less certain than under English law, where a properly drafted sum is generally enforced.
  • Short notification periods may not work as drafted. The claim notice gates from Module 11 depend on the court enforcing a contractual deadline strictly.
  • Asymmetric dispute clauses have been held invalid onshore. A clause that lets one party choose the forum and not the other is at risk.

More generally, civil-law systems tend to give good faith a larger role than English law does: a general duty to perform contracts honestly and fairly, which a judge can use to temper a strictly worded clause. How far this goes in a given UAE dispute is a question for qualified counsel, not something to state from general knowledge.

That is the meaning of "Ask, don't assert" for you. You are an engineer in a room with lawyers who are qualified in this system. You will be right more often and trusted sooner if you turn each onshore point into a question: "does this cap hold if an onshore court hears it?" A confident wrong statement about local law is the fastest way to lose the room. It is also the reason the standard structure avoids onshore law for the SPA in the first place.

"We have a fixed reverse break fee in the draft. If this ever reached an onshore court, could the amount be adjusted, and does that change how we should structure it?"

A draft SPA for an onshore target is governed by onshore UAE law with onshore courts. It contains (i) a reverse break fee fixed at AED 15m, (ii) a 20-day claim notification deadline, and (iii) a clause letting only the buyer choose between court and arbitration. A colleague says: "These are standard, they will be enforced as written." Best response?

  • Agree: parties are free to contract and the clauses are clear
  • Disagree on (iii) only: the fee and the deadline are pure commercial terms
  • Flag all three for counsel: the reference identifies each as a point where onshore outcomes can differ from the drafting, and consider moving the SPA to English/DIFC/ADGM law with arbitration
  • Delete all three, since they are illegal onshore

Each item maps to one of the three surprises: court adjustment of agreed compensation, short notification periods, asymmetric clauses. "Illegal" overstates it: the reference says "may adjust" and "may not work as drafted". The right move is a question to counsel and a structural fix, not an assertion in either direction.

Corporate law: ownership and how shares actually move

Foreign ownership. Under Federal Decree-Law No. 32 of 2021 (in force 2 January 2022), 100% foreign ownership of onshore companies is the default, except for activities with "strategic impact" (Cabinet Resolution No. 55 of 2021). For a deal this means: check the target's licensed activities early. If any is on the restricted list, the structure changes.

LLC share transfers. An onshore LLC's constitutional document is its memorandum of association (MOA), which names the shareholders. Its permission to trade is its trade licence, which also lists the owners. So a transfer has three parts: a notarised transfer document, an amendment of the MOA, and an updated licence. The reference states that the transfer binds third parties once registered with the updated licence. Until then the buyer's title is exposed.

This creates a sequencing problem that English-style closings do not have. The seller should not hand over the shares before funds are secure. The buyer should not release funds before the registration that makes its title good against the world. A common answer sits in the closing mechanics clause: the price is held in escrow and released when the updated licence is issued. Ask counsel how the sequence is handled in current practice.

Pre-emption rights. A pre-emption right is an existing shareholder's right to buy shares before they are sold to an outsider. If the target has other shareholders, their rights must be waived or run through before the transfer, or the transfer can be attacked. This belongs in the CPs or the signing deliverables.

The 2025 amendments. Federal Decree-Law No. 20 of 2025 introduced multiple share classes, statutory drag and tag rights, and re-domiciliation. Drag-along lets a majority force a minority to sell alongside it, so a buyer can get 100%. Tag-along lets a minority join a majority's sale on the same terms. Re-domiciliation is moving a company's place of registration without dissolving it. The reference course records a conflict on the effective date: 15 October 2025 according to one firm's note (Cleary) and 15 November 2025 according to another (Clyde & Co). Confirm before relying on it. The live practical question is whether clients now use these tools onshore or still put a DIFC or ADGM holding company on top to get them.

Before reading on: before these amendments, why would founders of an onshore business set up a DIFC or ADGM holding company above it before taking investors?

Investors want share classes (preferred shares), drag and tag, and a common-law shareholders' agreement enforced in English. Putting a free-zone holding company on top lets all the investor-level rights live in a common-law entity, while the onshore company underneath simply has one shareholder. A later sale is then a sale of holding company shares: transfer form and register, no notary. The amendments raise the question of whether that extra layer is still needed.

A buyer is acquiring 70% of an onshore LLC from its majority owner. A third party holds the other 30% and is not part of the deal. The draft SPA has a single CP: merger clearance. Which addition is most clearly required by the corporate law points above?

  • A MAC clause, because minority shareholders increase risk
  • A condition or signing deliverable dealing with the minority's pre-emption rights, and closing mechanics tied to the notarised transfer and updated licence
  • A higher warranty cap
  • A DIFC share transfer form signed by the minority

The 30% holder may have a right to buy the shares first. If that is not waived or exhausted, the buyer's transfer is vulnerable regardless of how good the SPA is. The caps and the MAC are commercial terms unrelated to title. A DIFC form is irrelevant to an onshore LLC.

Merger control, sector regulators and listed companies

You met the general mechanics in Module 10. The UAE regime in full, as the reference course gives it:

  • Source: Federal Decree-Law No. 36 of 2023. Thresholds under Cabinet Resolution No. 3 of 2025, effective 31 March 2025.
  • Trigger: an economic concentration (the UAE term for a notifiable deal) where sales in the relevant UAE market reach AED 300m, or the combined market share reaches 40%. Either limb is enough.
  • Nature: mandatory and suspensory. File at least 90 days before completion.
  • Timing: Executive Regulations in force 30 July 2026. Completeness check within 10 business days, then a 90-day clock. Practice reported at about 30 to 45 days.
  • Silence: no decision in time means deemed rejection. Third parties can object.
  • Fee: Cabinet Resolution No. 105 of 2026: 0.02% of aggregate turnover, capped at AED 150,000 (from 12 July 2026).
  • Open points: whether thresholds apply per party (raised by Baker McKenzie), how far the regime reaches deals between foreign companies (debated, Eversheds), and penalty levels (figures vary; up to 10% of relevant UAE revenue is cited).

Worked example. Buyer sells AED 260m a year in the UAE market for packaged dairy. Target sells AED 70m in the same market. Combined share is 31%.

  1. Share limb: 31% is below 40%. Not triggered.
  2. Turnover limb, read on a combined basis: 260 + 70 = AED 330m, above AED 300m. Triggered.
  3. Turnover limb, if applied per party: neither party alone reaches AED 300m. This is exactly the open point.
  4. Market definition: if the relevant market is narrower (say fresh milk only), both numbers change.

Conclusion: you cannot call this one from the desk. The cost of filing is small (fee capped at AED 150,000, some weeks of time). The cost of wrongly not filing under a suspensory regime is large. So the working assumption is a clearance CP and a long-stop date sized for it, with counsel confirming.

Buyer: AED 90m of sales in the UAE market for industrial gases. Target: AED 60m in the same market. Together they hold 46% of that market. Is the UAE threshold met?

  • No: combined sales of AED 150m are well below AED 300m
  • Yes on the market share limb: 46% exceeds 40%, and the limbs are alternatives
  • No: a filing needs both AED 300m and 40%
  • Only if a third party objects

Small markets catch people out. The turnover figure looks harmless, but two mid-sized players in a niche can hold a large share. Either limb triggers. Third-party objections are part of the review process, not a trigger.

Buyer: AED 210m of sales in the relevant UAE market. Target: AED 120m in the same market. Combined share 25%. A junior on the team writes: "Threshold not met, since no party has AED 300m." What is the most accurate correction?

  • He is right: the test is clearly per party
  • He is wrong: the test is clearly combined, so a filing is certain
  • Whether the turnover threshold is applied per party or combined is an open point in the reference, so treat a filing as likely needed, plan the CP and timetable for it, and get counsel to confirm
  • It does not matter, because below 40% share no filing is ever needed

Combined sales are AED 330m. On one reading that triggers, on the other it does not. Both confident answers assert something the source flags as unsettled. In deal planning, an unresolved suspensory-filing question is handled as if the filing is required until counsel says otherwise, because the downside is asymmetric.

The parties to a notifiable deal have aggregate turnover of AED 900m. Using the fee rule above, what is the filing fee? Answer in AED.

0.02% of 900,000,000 = 0.0002 × 900,000,000 = AED 180,000. The cap is AED 150,000, so the fee is AED 150,000. The cap binds for any aggregate turnover above AED 750m (150,000 / 0.0002).

Sector regulators. Depending on the target, approval may also be needed from the Central Bank (banks, insurers, finance companies), the Securities and Commodities Authority, SCA (listed companies), other sector regulators, and the relevant free-zone authority for a transfer of a free-zone company. Each is a possible CP with its own timetable.

Listed companies. The reference gives headline rules under SCA Decision No. 18/RM of 2017: a mandatory offer (an obligation to offer to buy out all other shareholders) at 30% plus one share, and a squeeze-out (a right to compulsorily buy the remaining minority) above 90%. It flags that the current threshold should be checked for changes. DIFC companies fall under the DFSA Takeover Rules. This course is about private deals: know these exist and ask.

Employment: gratuity and Emiratisation

No automatic transfer. Some countries have rules that move employees automatically to the buyer of a business, with their terms intact (in the UK this is known as TUPE). The UAE has no equivalent. In a share deal nothing changes: the employer is the same company, so accrued service stays with the target. In an asset deal each employee must be terminated by the seller and rehired by the buyer, and termination triggers the end-of-service payment.

End-of-service gratuity is a lump sum the employer owes an employee when employment ends, based on length of service. The reference formula: 21 days' basic pay per year for the first five years, 30 days' basic pay per year after that, capped at two years' pay.

Worked example. Basic pay AED 15,000 a month, 8 years' service. Taking a day's pay as monthly basic pay divided by 30 (a working convention: confirm the exact basis with employment counsel):

  • Daily basic pay: 15,000 / 30 = AED 500.
  • Years 1 to 5: 21 × 500 × 5 = AED 52,500.
  • Years 6 to 8: 30 × 500 × 3 = AED 45,000.
  • Total: AED 97,500. Cap: 24 × 15,000 = AED 360,000, not reached.

Why it is debt-like. The liability grows every month, is owed to a certain class of creditors, and is usually unfunded: no cash has been set aside. A buyer of the shares inherits an obligation the seller's years of ownership created. In the reference's price bridge, accrued unfunded gratuity of AED 2.5m is deducted from enterprise value alongside bank debt. Sellers argue it is an ordinary operating liability that never falls due in one go. The reference calls it a classic UAE negotiation point. The fight happens in the definition of "Debt".

An employee has basic pay of AED 18,000 a month and 9 years' service. Using the formula above and a 30-day month, what is the accrued gratuity? Answer in AED.

Daily pay: 18,000 / 30 = 600. First five years: 21 × 600 × 5 = 63,000. Next four years: 30 × 600 × 4 = 72,000. Total AED 135,000. The cap (24 × 18,000 = 432,000) is not reached. The common error is applying 30 days to all nine years (162,000) or 21 days to all nine (113,400).

A long-serving manager has basic pay of AED 10,000 a month and 30 years' service. Treat the cap as 24 months of basic pay and use a 30-day month. What gratuity is owed? Answer in AED.

Daily pay: 10,000 / 30 = 333.33. First five years: 21 × 333.33 × 5 = 35,000. Next 25 years: 30 × 333.33 × 25 = 250,000. Uncapped total 285,000. Cap: 24 × 10,000 = 240,000. The cap binds, so the answer is AED 240,000. In a diligence model, forgetting the cap overstates the liability for long-serving staff.

A buyer wants only one division of a UAE company and structures an asset deal. The division has 120 employees with accrued gratuity of AED 3.1m. The draft asset purchase agreement is silent on employees. What is the gap?

  • None: employees transfer automatically with the business and keep their accrued service
  • None: gratuity is only payable on retirement
  • There is no automatic transfer, so the employees must be terminated and rehired, which triggers gratuity: the agreement must say who pays the AED 3.1m and how the rehiring works
  • The buyer must take the whole company, because UAE law does not allow asset deals

The first option imports a rule from other jurisdictions that the UAE does not have. Terminate-and-rehire crystallises a cash cost on day one that a share deal would have left as an accruing liability. It also creates retention risk: each employee has a moment at which he can simply not sign the new contract. Both go into the price and the CPs.

Emiratisation is the requirement for private employers to employ a minimum share of UAE nationals. The reference figures, with their caveats: employers with 50 or more employees must add 2 percentage points a year, reaching 10% by 31 December 2026. Financial contributions for unfilled posts are reported at AED 10,000 a month per post (from secondary sources; some say AED 9,000). Employers with 20 to 49 employees in 14 sectors must have at least two Emiratis. For a deal this is a compliance warranty, a diligence question (what is the shortfall, what has been paid) and, if there is a known shortfall, a specific indemnity or price item.

Diligence shows the target was 5 Emirati posts short of its quota for the whole of last year and paid nothing. Using the AED 10,000 per month per unfilled post figure that the reference reports from secondary sources, what exposure for that year do you put on the issues list for counsel to confirm? Answer in AED.

5 posts × AED 10,000 × 12 months = AED 600,000. At the alternative AED 9,000 figure it would be AED 540,000, so present it as a range and mark the rate as unconfirmed. Because this is a known issue, it belongs in a specific indemnity or the price, not in the general warranties (which disclosure would defeat).

W&I insurance and the market in 2025 to 2026

W&I in the Middle East. Marsh (Q1 2026) puts Middle East premiums at 0.7% to 1.8% of the policy limit, with retentions of 0.15% to 0.5% of enterprise value. For comparison it gives 0.4% to 1.2% for the UK and 3.0% to 3.7% for the US and Canada. Marsh saw more than 100 inquiries in the Middle East and Africa in 2025, with a median deal size of US$390m, at historic-low pricing. The catch, from Sidley (May 2026): after the 2026 regional conflict, policies carry exclusions for war, civil unrest and sanctions. Iran is uninsurable and Iraq largely off-limits. The reference also notes that cyber due diligence has become central.

The mechanism matters more than the numbers. W&I replaces the seller's cap only for risks the insurer accepts. Each exclusion reopens the question of who bears that risk. If the policy excludes sanctions and the target trades in the region, the buyer must get a specific indemnity or a price reduction from the seller, or keep the risk knowingly.

Market context. PwC TransAct 2026: Middle East deal volumes rose 33% in 2025, the UAE led with 207 deals, and Middle East sovereign wealth funds invested US$127bn globally (Mubadala US$33.7bn). A&O Shearman: GCC deal value of US$72.7bn to 1 December 2025, of which the UAE was US$60.4bn. In the first half of 2026 the UAE accounted for 80.9% of disclosed GCC value (US$52.6bn), while Q1 2026 volumes fell to 33 deals from 52. Read this as leverage data: abundant sovereign and family capital chasing assets favours sellers, and a volume dip with a conflict in the background favours buyers who can still close.

There is no public dataset of GCC deal terms. The reference is explicit that "market" in the UAE is practitioner judgement. CMS and ABA figures describe Europe and the US.
Before reading on: W&I is cheap in the Middle East. Why might a seller in a UAE auction still fail to get its "clean exit" from it?

Because the clean exit only covers what the policy covers. Known issues from diligence are excluded everywhere. In this region the war, civil unrest and sanctions exclusions add a second gap. A buyer will ask the seller to stand behind those gaps. So the seller's real exposure is the list of exclusions, and a well-advised seller negotiates that list with the insurer before launching the auction.

A UAE logistics target earns 15% of revenue from routes into a country affected by the 2026 conflict. The seller proposes: seller's warranty cap of AED 1, buyer to claim under a W&I policy. The draft policy has war and sanctions exclusions. What must change in the SPA?

  • Nothing: W&I is cheap in the region, so the buyer is covered
  • The governing law, to DIFC law
  • The buyer needs seller cover for the excluded risks: a specific indemnity or price adjustment for sanctions and conflict-related losses, outside the AED 1 cap
  • The buyer should ask for a general MAC instead

A AED 1 cap plus a policy with exclusions means that, for the excluded risks, nobody stands behind the warranties. That is precisely the exposure this target has. Low premiums are irrelevant to an excluded risk. A MAC only helps before closing and is hard to invoke. Governing law does not create cover.

You are handed a fact sheet: "UAE onshore target, English-law SPA, disputes to DIFC-LCIA arbitration, price paid to seller at signing of the notarial transfer." Which two items do you flag first?

  • English law (should be onshore law) and the notarial transfer (not needed)
  • The DIFC-LCIA reference (the centre was abolished in 2021, cases moved to DIAC) and payment before the updated licence (title binds third parties only on registration)
  • Arbitration (courts are safer) and the price (should be deferred)
  • Nothing: this is the typical structure

It looks like the standard structure, which is why it is easy to wave through. English law with arbitration is fine. The named institution no longer exists, so the clause needs fixing before it becomes a jurisdiction fight. And releasing the price at the notary, before the licence is updated, leaves the buyer paid-out with title not yet effective against third parties: use an escrow.

Saudi Arabia and the rest of the GCC. Each GCC state (Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman) has its own company law, competition regime, foreign ownership rules and labour law. None of it is covered by the reference course, and nothing in this module should be assumed to carry over. KSA matters most because many UAE targets sell there, and sales into a country may be enough to bring its regime into play even when the target has no company there. Questions to put to local counsel:

  • Does this deal trigger a merger filing in KSA or any other GCC state? What are the thresholds, and are they measured on local or worldwide turnover?
  • Is the regime suspensory? What is the realistic timetable, and what happens if the authority is silent?
  • What are the filing fees and the penalties for closing without clearance?
  • Are there foreign ownership limits or licensing steps for the target's activities, and does a change of control need re-approval?
  • How do shares transfer mechanically, and when is title effective?
  • What are the end-of-service and nationalisation (local hiring quota) obligations, and how are shortfalls penalised?
  • Will a foreign-law SPA and a foreign-seated arbitration award be enforced locally, and are fixed sums and contractual time limits respected?
Before reading on: a UAE target has no Saudi subsidiary but earns a third of its revenue from Saudi customers. Why can that alone add months to your timetable?

Merger control generally follows where sales are made, not where companies are registered (Module 10). Sales into a country can trigger that country's filing. If its regime is suspensory, closing waits for it, and your long-stop date must cover the slowest authority in the set. You do not know the Saudi thresholds from this course, so the correct action is the first question on the list above, asked in week one.

Key terms

Onshore (mainland)
The part of the UAE outside the financial free zones, governed by UAE civil law with Arabic-language courts.
DIFC
Dubai International Financial Centre, a free zone with its own common-law statutes and English-language courts.
ADGM
Abu Dhabi Global Market, a free zone that applies English common law directly and has English-language courts.
Conduit route
Enforcing a DIFC-seated arbitration award onshore by passing it through the DIFC Courts.
Memorandum of association (MOA)
The constitutional document of an onshore LLC, which names its shareholders and must be amended on a share transfer.
Trade licence
An onshore company's permission to carry on its activities, which lists its owners and must be updated for a transfer to bind third parties.
Pre-emption right
An existing shareholder's right to buy shares before they are transferred to an outsider.
Drag-along / tag-along
A majority's right to force a minority to sell with it, and a minority's right to join a majority's sale on the same terms.
Economic concentration
The UAE merger control term for a transaction that must be notified if the thresholds are met.
Deemed rejection
The UAE rule that a merger filing with no decision within the review period is treated as refused.
End-of-service gratuity
A lump sum owed to an employee on termination: 21 days' basic pay per year for five years, then 30 days per year, capped at two years' pay.
Emiratisation
The requirement for private-sector employers to employ a minimum proportion of UAE nationals, with financial contributions for shortfalls.
Mandatory offer
An obligation on a person who crosses a set shareholding in a listed company to offer to buy out the other shareholders.
Ask, don't assert
The working rule that onshore law points are put to qualified counsel as questions, never stated as fact by a non-lawyer.
