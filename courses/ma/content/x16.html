Part A: foundations and the deal

A seller promises: "If the pending claim by Customer X results in any liability, the Seller will pay the Buyer an amount equal to it." The buyer knew all about the claim before signing. The claim is lost. Which is correct?

  • The buyer cannot recover, because it knew
  • The buyer recovers the amount, because this is an indemnity and knowledge and disclosure do not defeat it
  • The buyer must prove the shares fell in value
  • The claim is subject to the general basket

A promise to pay if a specified event occurs is an indemnity. It pays the loss itself and is the right tool for known risks precisely because knowledge does not matter. Specific indemnities normally sit outside the basket. Modules 1 and 9.

A buyer wants a business but not its historic environmental liabilities, and the business has only twelve contracts. Which structure fits best?

  • Share purchase with a higher cap
  • Asset purchase
  • Merger
  • Minority investment

In an asset purchase the buyer picks the assets and liabilities it takes. The cost is transferring each contract and employee, which is manageable with twelve contracts. Module 2.

An LOI states price, structure, a 45-day exclusivity period and that it is "not legally binding save for clauses 7 to 9 (exclusivity, confidentiality, costs)". During exclusivity the seller negotiates with another party. What is the buyer's position?

  • No claim: the LOI is non-binding
  • A claim for breach of the binding exclusivity clause
  • An automatic right to buy at the LOI price
  • A claim only if the SPA was already signed

An LOI is usually non-binding on price and structure but expressly binding on exclusivity, confidentiality and costs. Module 3.

Enterprise value AED 240m. Bank debt 60m. Cash 14m. Unfunded end-of-service gratuity 6m, agreed to be debt-like. Working capital at closing 31m against a peg of 35m. What is the equity price, in AED m? (Enter 184 for 184m.)

240 − 60 − 6 + 14 − (35 − 31) = AED 184m. The gratuity reduces price one for one because it is debt-like; the working capital shortfall reduces it by 4. Module 4.

In a locked-box deal, the target's profits between the locked-box date and closing belong economically to:

  • The seller, paid out at closing
  • The buyer, which is why sellers ask for a ticker
  • Whoever the board decides
  • They are split equally

The price is fixed as at the locked-box date, so everything after it accrues to the buyer. The seller is compensated for waiting through a daily ticker, and must not extract value (leakage). Module 4.

Locked-box deal. Price USD 80m. The ticker is 5% a year on the price, simple interest, for the 73 days from the locked-box date to closing (365-day year). What ticker amount is paid, in USD?

80,000,000 × 0.05 × 73 ÷ 365 = USD 800,000. Module 4.

Diligence finds that the target has under-accrued staff bonuses by exactly AED 900k, payable next month. Best treatment?

  • A warranty that bonuses are properly accrued
  • A specific indemnity
  • A price reduction (debt-like item)
  • A condition precedent

Known and exactly countable: take it off the price. An indemnity is for uncertainty; a warranty would be defeated by the buyer's knowledge and by disclosure. Module 5.

In a US-style agreement you read: "The representations and warranties shall terminate at the Closing." What does it tell you?

  • The deal has no warranties
  • After closing the buyer has no claim against the seller for untrue representations (fraud aside), so recourse is probably an insurance policy
  • Warranties are repeated at closing
  • The seller's cap is 100%

No survival means no post-closing claim against the seller. In the precedent set this structure appears with insurance or financial sellers. Modules 6 and 9.

Part B: risk allocation

A warranty states that EBITDA for the last year was AED 12m. It was really AED 11.5m, and the shortfall is recurring. The price was set at 8 × EBITDA. On the standard measure of damages, what is the loss, in AED m? (Enter 4 for 4m.)

Value as warranted 8 × 12 = 96; actual value 8 × 11.5 = 92. Loss = AED 4m: the shortfall times the multiple, not the AED 0.5m itself. Module 7.

A warranty reads: "So far as the Seller is aware, no customer intends to terminate." "Seller is aware" is defined as the actual knowledge of two named directors, without enquiry. A regional manager knew a customer would leave; the directors did not. Outcome?

  • Breach: the company knew
  • No breach: neither named person actually knew, and they had no duty to ask
  • Breach, but damages are halved
  • No breach, because customers can always leave

An actual-knowledge qualifier limited to named people puts the risk of what others knew on the buyer. Buyers ask for constructive knowledge (deemed reasonable enquiry) and a wider group. Module 7.

A warranty says there has been no "material" breach of contract, and claims count only above a basket. The SPA has a double materiality scrape. A contract breach causing AED 80k of loss occurs. Effect of the scrape?

  • None
  • "Material" is ignored both when deciding whether the warranty is breached and when measuring loss, so the AED 80k counts toward the basket
  • The basket is removed
  • The cap is doubled

Without a scrape the seller gets two layers of protection for small matters: the materiality qualifier and the basket. A double scrape removes the first, for both breach and loss. Module 7.

Price AED 200m. De minimis 0.1%. Basket 1%, deductible. Cap 15%. General warranty claims notified in time: AED 150k, AED 900k and AED 1.4m. How much does the seller pay, in AED?

De minimis = 200k, so the 150k claim does not count. Counting claims = 900k + 1.4m = 2.3m. Basket = 2m, deductible: pay the excess, AED 300k. With a tipping basket it would be 2.3m. Module 8.

Same deal. A further claim: the seller did not own 10% of the shares it sold, causing a loss of AED 20m. How is that claim treated?

  • Subject to the 15% cap with the others
  • As a fundamental warranty claim: outside de minimis and basket, capped at the price
  • Excluded as too large
  • Subject to the basket but not the cap

Title is a fundamental warranty: it goes to whether the buyer got what it paid for. It skips the small-claim filters and has its own cap, normally 100% of price. Module 8.

The general time limit is 18 months, with tax claims at 7 years. Which rationale best explains the two periods?

  • Tradition
  • General problems surface within one or two audit cycles, while tax authorities can reopen past years for much longer
  • Tax claims are larger
  • Sellers prefer long tax periods

Time limits track when problems can be discovered. A buyer should match each period to the discovery window of the risk. Module 8.

Price USD 100m. W&I policy: retention 0.5% of price, limit USD 20m. The seller's cap is USD 1. An unknown warranty breach causes a loss of USD 6m. How much does the insurer pay, in USD?

Retention = USD 0.5m. Insurer pays the loss above it: 6m − 0.5m = USD 5.5m, well within the limit. The buyer keeps the retention. Module 9.

The same policy excludes "matters known to the deal team", and the buyer's diligence report mentions a possible data-protection breach. A fine arrives. Who pays?

  • The insurer
  • The buyer, unless it obtained a specific indemnity or price reduction from the seller
  • The seller, automatically
  • The broker

Insurance covers unknown breaches only. Known matters must be dealt with by indemnity or price, which is why insurance does not end the negotiation with the seller. Module 9.

A buyer signs with one condition: merger clearance. Before clearance, it starts instructing the target's managers on pricing. What is the main problem?

  • Breach of the non-compete
  • Gun-jumping: taking control before clearance
  • It waives the condition
  • None

Until clearance and closing, the buyer and target must act as independent businesses. Gap covenants give the buyer veto rights over unusual actions, not day-to-day control. Module 10.

Between signing and closing a fire destroys uninsured stock worth 5% of working capital. The SPA uses completion accounts and has no MAC clause. Who bears the loss?

  • The buyer: risk passed at signing
  • The seller: the price adjusts by reference to the balance sheet at closing
  • Neither: the deal terminates automatically
  • They share it equally

With completion accounts the price is trued up to the closing balance sheet, so the lost stock reduces working capital and therefore the price. In a locked box the same loss would fall on the buyer. Modules 4 and 10.

A buyer notifies a warranty claim by email to the seller's CEO one day before the deadline. The notices clause requires delivery by hand or courier to the seller's registered office and says email is not valid notice. The seller says the claim is out of time. Likely result?

  • Claim valid: the seller actually knew
  • Claim at serious risk: contractual notice requirements are generally enforced as written
  • Claim valid, with costs against the seller
  • The deadline is extended by 30 days

Notice clauses defeat many otherwise good claims. Diarise the deadline and follow the mechanics exactly. Module 11.

Part C: the UAE

A buyer with AED 320m of UAE cold-storage sales is buying a UAE cold-storage target with AED 60m. The parties plan to sign and close on the same day next month. What is the issue?

  • None: the deal is below any threshold
  • A merger filing is likely required, the regime is suspensory, and the filing must be made at least 90 days before completion
  • Only a post-closing notification is needed
  • The filing fee would exceed the price

Per the course research file: AED 300m of relevant UAE sales (or a 40% share) triggers a mandatory, suspensory filing at least 90 days before completion, and silence counts as rejection. A same-day sign and close is not available. Modules 10 and 12.

An employee on basic pay of AED 20,000 a month has served 8 full years. Using the course formula (21 days' basic pay per year for the first 5 years, 30 days per year after; one day = monthly basic pay ÷ 30), what is the gratuity, in AED?

Days = 21 × 5 + 30 × 3 = 195. Daily pay = 20,000 ÷ 30 = 666.67. Gratuity = 195 × 666.67 = AED 130,000, under the cap of two years' pay. If unfunded, it is a debt-like item. Module 12.

A buyer of a Dubai mainland LLC wants disputes heard in English under a common-law system, with an award it can enforce. Which choice fits the course guidance?

  • Onshore courts, UAE law
  • English, DIFC or ADGM governing law with arbitration
  • No dispute clause
  • Courts of the buyer's home country only

The target's place of incorporation does not dictate the SPA's governing law or forum. Most significant deals pair common-law governing law with arbitration, plus a short Arabic transfer instrument for the notary. Module 12.

Part D: calling the deal

Your client says: "Our last acquisition had a 30% cap, so 30% is market." This deal is uninsured. Best reply?

  • Agree and put 30% in the markup as a firm position
  • "That is one data point. Across comparable uninsured deals the range is roughly 10 to 17%; we can open at 30 but should expect to land in that range."
  • "Caps are set by law, not by negotiation."
  • "Market is irrelevant."

One deal is client data. The range across comparables is precedent. Opening high is fine; expecting to land there is not. Modules 13 and 14.

Which single fact about a deal best predicts the size of the seller's cap?

  • The governing law
  • Whether warranty insurance is in place
  • The sector
  • The number of pages in the SPA

In the precedent set, insured deals have seller caps near zero and uninsured deals cluster at 10 to 17%. Split by insurance before anything else. Module 14.

Uninsured cap band: P25 10%, median 15%, P75 16.7%. Leverage score +1 for the buyer. Openings: buyer 50%, seller 8%. Predicted cap, in % of price?

Score +1, and a higher cap favours the buyer, so move half-way from the median to P75: 15 + 0.5 × (16.7 − 15) = 15.85%. Inside the openings, so no clamp. Module 14.

Facts: bilateral deal; the seller is a multinational that will happily keep the unit; no insurance; diligence found serious issues; the eight-factor sum is positive. What should you tell the client about leverage?

  • It is strongly with the buyer, as the score shows
  • The arithmetic overstates it: a seller with no need to sell is a dominant factor, so expect little movement on anything the seller treats as a principle
  • Leverage is irrelevant to carve-outs
  • The seller will concede everything except price

The model's equal weights fail when one factor dominates. A seller who can walk away at no cost holds the decisive card. Say so and override the score. Module 14.

Price AED 150m. Deal costs AED 1.6m. Risks: (A) p = 40%, loss 5m, covered by a specific indemnity backed by escrow; (B) p = 20%, loss 12m, not covered by anything; (C) p = 50%, loss 1m, a general warranty matter surfacing at month 20 where the time limit is 18 months. What is the effective price, in AED m? (Enter 154.5 for 154.5m.)

A is fully recovered: kept 0. B: 0.2 × 12 = 2.4m. C is out of time: 0.5 × 1 = 0.5m. Kept risk = 2.9m. Effective price = 150 + 2.9 + 1.6 = AED 154.5m. Module 15.

In that deal the cap is 15%. The seller offers to extend the time limit to 24 months if the buyer drops its request to raise the cap to 40%. The largest general-warranty loss on the register is AED 1m, and the basket is small enough that risk C would be recovered in full if in time. Advice?

  • Refuse: a higher cap is always worth more than time
  • Accept: the extension is worth AED 0.5m in expected value, and the higher cap protects against nothing on the register
  • Refuse both
  • Accept only if the basket is also removed

15% of 150m is 22.5m, far above any modelled general loss, so the extra cap has no expected value. The extension brings risk C into time. Trade what is worthless to you for what is not. Module 15.

A target's only licence to operate is due for renewal before closing and may be refused. The seller offers a generous indemnity instead of a condition. Why is a condition the right protection?

  • Indemnities are unenforceable
  • If the licence is lost the business itself is gone: the buyer wants not to complete, not to claim a sum close to the whole price from a seller that may not be able to pay
  • Conditions are cheaper to draft
  • Indemnities always have a basket

Risks that go to the reason for the deal belong in conditions. Compensation does not replace the business, and recourse for a near-total loss is doubtful. Modules 10 and 15.

Which of these is NOT a sound walk-away trigger?

  • A known material risk that the seller will cover by neither indemnity nor price
  • A possible loss large enough to threaten the buyer itself
  • The client has already spent heavily on advisers
  • An approval that cannot be obtained before the long-stop date

Money already spent is a sunk cost, and it is the usual reason clients fail to walk when they should. The other three are standard triggers. Module 15.

You have predicted landings for six terms, a risk register, and a verdict of "pursue with conditions". What else must the memo contain to be usable by a decision-maker?

  • The full text of the SPA
  • The effective price against value, the conditions each with a price fallback, the walk-away triggers, and how far the key estimates can move before the verdict flips
  • A list of every clause reviewed
  • Only the predicted cap and basket

Advice needs the number, the conditions, the exits and the sensitivity. Module 15.
