Baseline diagnostic

Twelve questions before you start. Answer from instinct, without looking anything up. You are expected to get most of them wrong: the point is to see what your intuitions are, so you notice when a module overturns one. Come back after module 15 and compare.

This page teaches nothing on purpose. Each explanation names the module that covers the point.

A buyer pays 100 for all the shares in a company. A year later an unknown tax bill of 5 from before the sale arrives. With no special wording in the contract, who bears it?

  • The seller, because the tax relates to its period of ownership
  • The buyer, because it now owns the company and the company owes the tax
  • They split it equally
  • The tax authority must pursue the former owner

The company owes the tax, and the buyer owns the company. Unless the buyer bargained for protection, it bears the loss: "buyer beware". Almost everything in an SPA exists to change this default. Modules 2, 5 and 7.

What is a senior deal lawyer mainly paid for?

  • Knowing the statutes by heart
  • Drafting documents quickly
  • Judgment about risk and what terms are normal, and standing behind that advice
  • Winning arguments with the other side

Drafting and knowledge are increasingly cheap. What clients pay for is judgment about which risks matter, knowledge of where terms usually land, and accountability. Module 0.

A company is valued at 100 "cash-free, debt-free". It has debt of 30 and cash of 10. Roughly what do the sellers receive for their shares?

  • 100
  • 130
  • 80
  • 60

100 is the value of the business. The buyer takes on the debt (minus 30) and gets the cash (plus 10): 80 for the shares. Module 4.

The seller states in the contract "the company is not involved in any litigation". This turns out to be untrue. What can the buyer normally do?

  • Cancel the deal and get all its money back
  • Claim compensation, if it can prove a loss and gets past the agreed limits
  • Nothing, unless the seller lied deliberately
  • Report the seller to a regulator

This is a warranty. An untrue warranty gives a claim for damages, which the buyer must prove and which is subject to time limits, thresholds and a cap. It does not normally unwind the deal. Modules 1, 7 and 8.

The seller told the buyer about a lawsuit in a formal disclosure before signing. The lawsuit is later lost at a cost of 2. Can the buyer claim under the "no litigation" warranty?

  • Yes: the warranty was still untrue
  • No: a matter properly disclosed cannot be claimed for under the warranties
  • Yes, but only half
  • Only if the lawsuit was started after signing

Disclosure is the seller's defence to warranty claims. A buyer who knows about a problem needs a different tool: a price reduction or a specific indemnity. Modules 7 and 9.

The contract caps the seller's liability at 20% of the price and the seller is a company that will pay all the proceeds to its owners after closing. How well protected is the buyer?

  • Well: 20% is a meaningful cap
  • Possibly not at all: a right to claim is only worth what the seller still has
  • Fully, because courts can pursue the owners automatically
  • It depends only on the wording of the cap

The cap is a ceiling on a right. Whether money can actually be collected depends on who the seller is afterwards and whether money was set aside (escrow) or guaranteed. Module 9.

Why would signing the contract and completing the purchase happen on different days?

  • Tradition
  • To give the buyer time to raise the price
  • Because approvals or consents are needed before ownership can change
  • Because lawyers need time to print documents

Regulatory clearances and third-party consents take time. The contract is signed conditionally, and closing happens when the conditions are met. Modules 3 and 10.

Two bidders compete for a company. Compared with a one-to-one negotiation, the contract terms will usually be:

  • More favourable to the buyer
  • More favourable to the seller
  • The same: terms depend on the law, not on competition
  • Decided by the bidders jointly

Competition gives the seller leverage: it can choose the bidder asking for the least protection. Leverage, not law, decides most negotiated terms. Modules 2, 13 and 14.

A buyer has an insurance policy covering untrue warranties. What is the most likely effect on the seller's own liability cap?

  • It rises, because the insurer demands it
  • No effect
  • It falls to a nominal amount
  • The cap is removed

The insurer replaces the seller as the source of recovery, so the seller's cap typically drops to almost nothing. Modules 9 and 14.

"Market practice" in a negotiation is best described as:

  • A legal rule both sides must follow
  • An argument from what comparable deals did, which leverage can override
  • The first draft of the contract
  • Whatever the more senior lawyer says

Nothing obliges anyone to follow market practice. It is evidence of what is normal, used as an argument. Modules 0, 13 and 14.

Diligence finds a 30% chance of a 10 loss. The seller refuses any protection. Price is 100 and the business is worth 104 to the buyer. Ignoring other costs, should the buyer proceed?

  • No: any uncovered risk is unacceptable
  • Yes: expected cost of the risk is 3, so the deal still clears by 1
  • Yes: the risk probably will not happen, so ignore it
  • It cannot be assessed

Expected loss is 0.3 × 10 = 3. Effective price is 103 against a value of 104. Thin, but positive. Turning terms and risks into one comparable number is the end goal of the course. Module 15.

For a target in the UAE, which statement is most accurate?

  • UAE law must govern the contract
  • The country has more than one legal system, and parties choose governing law and forum deliberately
  • Foreign buyers cannot own UAE companies
  • No regulatory approval is ever needed

Onshore UAE, the DIFC and the ADGM are separate legal systems. Most significant deals choose English, DIFC or ADGM law with arbitration, and may need merger clearance. Module 12.
