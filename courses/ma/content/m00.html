How lawyers and legal work actually function

After this module you can read a law firm like an org chart with incentives, follow how a deal lawyer reasons from an issue to a recommendation, and speak in a room of senior lawyers without pretending to be one.

What a lawyer is paid for, and where the licence stops

A client can read the statute and obtain a sample agreement before calling counsel. What the board usually needs is an answer about its own deal.

  1. Judgment under uncertainty. The law rarely answers the real question. "Can the buyer walk away if the target (the company being bought) loses its largest customer before the deal completes?" depends on the wording, the facts, and how a judge would read both years later. The lawyer turns that into a decision you can act on: likely or unlikely, expensive or cheap, fixable or not.
  2. Accountability. A firm signs advice within an agreed scope and owes professional duties to its client. If a careless opinion causes loss, the client may have a claim. Read who may rely on a signed opinion, which documents the firm reviewed and which assumptions it made. Professional indemnity insurance may cover a qualifying claim under the policy.
  3. Knowing what is normal. A partner who has closed 200 deals knows which demands the other side will accept at once, which they will fight, and which make you look like an amateur. This is information asymmetry, sold by the hour. Most clients do a handful of deals in a career. The lawyer sees the distribution.

A founder prepares the first draft of a share purchase agreement. The partner proposes only a short amendment. Why would the board still ask the firm for a signed advice memo before approving the deal?

  • The fee covers only the words the partner changed
  • The board wants the firm’s judgment on the whole agreement and an adviser accountable for the advice within its agreed scope
  • Legal privilege (protection of lawyer-client communications from being disclosed in court), which only exists if a partner personally drafts the document
  • Compliance with a rule that contracts must be drafted by a law firm

Review includes deciding which terms the client can accept as drafted. A short amendment might remove the only condition preventing the buyer from drawing its loan. The signed memo can explain that decision, the remaining exposure and the assumptions on which the advice depends.

A jurisdiction is a territory with its own legal system and courts: England and Wales, New York, France, the UAE. A lawyer is admitted (licensed) in specific jurisdictions after passing that place's exams. The licence covers that place's law only. A lawyer admitted in England who advises on Saudi law is outside the licence and usually outside the firm's insurance cover. So on a cross-border deal the lead firm hires local counsel: a firm admitted where the target operates, which advises on local law and handles local filings. This is not politeness. It is how the accountability chain stays unbroken.

England splits the profession in two. A solicitor advises clients directly, drafts documents and runs deals. A barrister is a specialist advocate who argues in court and gives opinions on hard points of law, usually instructed by a solicitor. Deal lawyers are solicitors. The United States has one profession, the attorney, admitted state by state.

The UAE has three legal systems in one country: the onshore (mainland) system, and two financial free zones, the DIFC in Dubai and the ADGM in Abu Dhabi, each with its own English-language courts. International firms in Dubai are full of lawyers admitted in England, New York or Paris who are registered locally to practise as legal consultants. They draft English-law deal documents every day. For a point of onshore UAE law, or to appear in the onshore Arabic-language courts, they bring in locally qualified lawyers. When a partner says "we would need to check that with our onshore team", that is the licence boundary speaking.

A New York-admitted partner in Dubai leads the purchase of a Saudi company under an English-law contract. The client asks, in a meeting, whether Saudi law lets the target dismiss 40 staff without compensation. What is the professionally correct response?

  • Answer from experience, since she has done several Saudi deals
  • Decline, because employment law is outside deal work
  • Give a general indication flagged as such, and get Saudi-qualified counsel to confirm before the client relies on it
  • Answer under English law, because the contract is governed by English law

Experience makes her indication useful, but she is not admitted in Saudi Arabia, so she cannot stand behind the answer. The English-law option is the trap: the contract between buyer and seller can be governed by English law, but the target's relationship with its Saudi employees is governed by Saudi law regardless.

Anatomy of a law firm

A law firm is a partnership that sells hours. The ranks explain who does what and why.

RankWhat they doHow they are rewarded
Equity partnerOwns a share of the firm. Wins clients, sets strategy on the deal, handles the hardest negotiations, signs off the advice.A share of the firm's profit. No salary floor, full downside.
Salaried partnerHas the partner title and authority with clients but is not an owner.Fixed pay plus bonus.
CounselSenior specialist who is not on the ownership track, often the deepest technical expert.Salary.
Senior associateRuns the deal day to day. Drafts the main contract, manages juniors, is your daily contact.Salary plus bonus tied to hours.
AssociateDrafts supporting documents, reviews the target's contracts, tracks open points.Salary plus bonus tied to hours.
TraineeApprentice in the English system, two years before qualifying.Salary.
ParalegalNot a qualified lawyer. Handles document management, filings, signature packs.Salary.

Leverage in a law firm means the ratio of fee-earning staff to equity partners. Each associate is billed to clients at more than they cost. The surplus flows to the partners. That is why partners originate work (bring in clients and mandates) and associates produce it: one partner's relationships can keep five people billing.

Three pricing models, each with a different incentive:

  • Billable hour: time recorded in six-minute units, multiplied by an hourly rate per rank. The client bears the risk that the work takes longer. The firm has no incentive to be quick.
  • Fixed fee: one price for a defined scope. The firm bears overrun risk, so it defines the scope tightly and lists assumptions (for example "one round of comments", "no regulatory filing"). When an assumption fails, the price reopens.
  • Capped fee: hourly billing up to a ceiling. The client keeps the upside if the work is quick and the firm bears the overrun. Firms resist caps or price them high.

Illustrative numbers, not market data. A partner's team has 4 associates. Each bills 1,700 hours a year at AED 2,200 per hour. Clients actually pay 90% of what is billed. Each associate costs the firm AED 1,400,000 a year in salary and overhead. What surplus does the team generate for the partners in a year? Answer in AED.

Billed per associate: 1,700 × 2,200 = 3,740,000. Collected: 3,740,000 × 0.9 = 3,366,000. Surplus per associate: 3,366,000 − 1,400,000 = 1,966,000. Four associates: 4 × 1,966,000 = 7,864,000. The partner's own hours are extra. This is why a partner's scarce resource is client relationships, not drafting time.

A firm agrees a fee cap of AED 1,500,000 for a deal. The deal runs long. Recorded time is 200 partner hours at AED 4,000 and 900 associate hours at AED 2,000. How much recorded time does the firm write off? Answer in AED.

Partner time: 200 × 4,000 = 800,000. Associate time: 900 × 2,000 = 1,800,000. Total recorded: 2,600,000. The client pays the cap, 1,500,000. Write-off: 2,600,000 − 1,500,000 = 1,100,000. The firm recovers about 58% of its recorded time.

Before reading on: under that cap, how will the firm's behaviour change in the last weeks of the deal?

Every extra hour is now free to the client and a pure cost to the firm. Expect the firm to push work to cheaper juniors, resist extra negotiation rounds, and point to any failed assumption to reopen the cap. As a client, a cap protects your budget but reduces the firm's appetite to fight marginal points. Know which model you are on before asking for "one more turn" of a document.

The client side: in-house, engagement, conflicts, privilege

Large companies employ their own lawyers. The General Counsel (GC) is the company's chief lawyer and reports to the chief executive or the board. Legal operations is the team that runs the legal department as a function: budgets, tooling, selection of outside firms. In-house lawyers know the business and decide how much risk the company accepts. External counsel (the law firm) supplies specialist capacity and the insured signature. On a deal, the GC is the firm's real client contact and the person who translates legal risk into a business decision.

Before a firm starts work, three things happen.

  • Conflicts check. A lawyer owes a duty of loyalty to each client. A conflict of interest exists when the firm's duty to one client collides with its duty to another, or with its own interest. The firm searches its client database for every party to the deal. If it already acts for the other side on anything, it may need both clients' consent, an internal information barrier, or it must decline.
  • Engagement letter. The contract between firm and client. It fixes the scope, the fee model, who the client is (which group company), and the limit on the firm's own liability. Anything outside the scope is not covered by the firm's signature.
  • Confidentiality. A lawyer must keep a client's information secret. This duty is owed to the client and survives the end of the engagement.

Legal privilege is stronger than confidentiality. It is the client's right to refuse to hand over certain lawyer-client communications to a court, a regulator or an opponent. In English-style systems it covers confidential communications between lawyer and client made for the purpose of giving or getting legal advice, and documents prepared for litigation. The mechanism: without it, clients would hide bad facts from their own lawyers, and advice would be worse. The scope varies by jurisdiction. Some systems give weaker protection to in-house lawyers' advice, and civil-law countries often rely on a lawyer's duty of professional secrecy in place of a privilege the client can assert.

Copying a lawyer on an email does not make it privileged. A business discussion stays a business discussion. And privilege can be lost (waived) by forwarding the advice outside the company. If you write "our lawyers think we will probably lose this point" to a banker or a co-investor, an opponent may later be entitled to read it.

During a deal you email your CFO: "The target has subcontracted work without its largest customer’s consent. We should close quickly before they notice." You cc your external lawyer. Two years later the customer sues and demands internal emails. Is this one protected by privilege in an English-style system?

  • Yes, a lawyer was a recipient
  • Yes, it concerns a legal risk
  • Probably not: it is a business communication between two non-lawyers, not a request for or giving of legal advice
  • Yes, because all deal communications are confidential under the engagement letter

Privilege attaches to communications made to seek or give legal advice. A cc does not change the purpose of the email. The engagement letter binds the firm to secrecy, but it does not bind a court. The safe pattern is to ask the lawyer the question directly ("Please advise whether subcontracting required consent under clause X") and keep the commercial discussion in its proper place.

You want Firm A to act for you in buying a company from SellerCo. Firm A currently advises SellerCo on an unrelated property lease. What is the most likely outcome?

  • No issue, because the lease is unrelated to the sale
  • Firm A cannot act against a current client without that client's informed consent, and may have to decline
  • Firm A can act for both buyer and seller on the sale to save cost
  • Firm A must first drop SellerCo as a client, which solves the conflict

The duty of loyalty runs to the client, not to the matter. Negotiating against a current client is a conflict even if the matters are unrelated. Dropping a client to take a better mandate is itself restricted by many professional rules, and the firm still holds SellerCo's confidential information. Expect a conflicts check to take a few days and occasionally to cost you your first-choice firm.

How a transactional lawyer thinks

Contentious work is disputes: litigation in court and arbitration (a private tribunal chosen by contract). It looks backward and asks who is liable for what happened. Transactional work is deals: it looks forward and writes rules for events that have not happened yet. Lawyers who work on M&A (mergers and acquisitions: the buying, selling and combining of companies) are transactional lawyers, but the good ones draft every clause while picturing the dispute it will have to survive.

The core idea of transactional work is risk allocation. A contract is a table: for each bad outcome that could occur, it names who pays. If the target owes unpaid tax from 2023, who bears it? If a regulator blocks the deal, who bears the wasted cost? Silence is also an allocation: the loss stays where it falls, usually on the buyer. Under the traditional rule of caveat emptor ("buyer beware"), a buyer who did not bargain for protection has none. Every clause you will study is a move of one risk from one party's column to the other's, and the price is the balancing item.

A deal lawyer processes each point in five steps:

  1. Issue. What is the precise question? "The target's largest customer contract lets the customer terminate if the target's owner changes."
  2. Rule. What does the law or the document say? "Clause 14.2 gives a termination right on change of control, exercisable within 60 days."
  3. Application. What does that mean on these facts? "Our purchase triggers it."
  4. Risk. How likely, how large? "The customer is 30% of revenue. Termination is unlikely but would be severe."
  5. Commercial recommendation. What should the client do? "Make the customer's written consent a condition of completing. If the seller refuses, ask for a price reduction."

Junior lawyers stop at step 3. Clients pay for steps 4 and 5. A list of open points written this way is an issues list: a table with one row per disagreement, showing each side's position and a proposed resolution. It is the working document of every negotiation and the input to the prediction task this course ends with.

You estimate a 15% chance that the key customer terminates, costing you AED 20,000,000. The seller offers a choice. Option A: reduce the price by AED 2,000,000 and you keep the whole risk. Option B: no price cut, but the seller will reimburse your loss up to a limit of AED 10,000,000 if the customer terminates. What is your expected uncovered loss under Option B? Answer in AED.

If the customer terminates you lose 20,000,000 and recover 10,000,000, so 10,000,000 is uncovered. Expected uncovered loss: 0.15 × 10,000,000 = 1,500,000. Compare Option A: expected loss 0.15 × 20,000,000 = 3,000,000, less the certain 2,000,000 saving, so a net expected cost of 1,000,000. A is better on average by 500,000. B is better in the bad state by 8,000,000 (you are down 10,000,000, not 18,000,000). Which to pick depends on whether you can survive the bad state and whether the seller will still have the money to pay.

Before reading on: why might a good lawyer recommend Option B even though A has the better expected value?

Because the client's loss function is not linear. A 20m hit might breach bank covenants or end a career, while a 0.5m difference in expectation is noise. Lawyers are trained on the tail: they see the deals that went wrong. Good advice states both the expected value and the worst case, then asks the client which one it is optimising. Also note the hidden assumption in B: a promise to reimburse is only worth the seller's ability to pay when the claim arrives.

An associate's note to the client says: "Clause 9 allows the lender to demand repayment if ownership changes. This is a risk." The partner sends it back. What is the most important thing missing?

  • The clause number of the definition of "ownership"
  • A statement that the firm accepts no liability for the note
  • Size and likelihood of the exposure, and what the client should do about it
  • A longer explanation of the law on loan agreements

The note stops at application. It does not say how much debt is repayable, whether the lender would really call it, or the fix (lender consent before completion, or refinancing at closing with the cost taken off the price). More law is the tempting wrong answer: clients do not buy explanation, they buy a decision.

"Market" and why precedent is the firm's real asset

Market practice, or just "market", means the range of terms that parties in comparable deals have recently agreed. It is an empirical claim about a distribution, not a legal rule. When a lawyer says "an 18-month claims period is market", the meaning is: most counterparties accept this without a fight, so asking for it costs you nothing and refusing it costs you credibility. Market varies by region, deal size, who the seller is and the year.

The evidence for market is precedent: the firm's own signed deals and its templates. (In court work "precedent" means a binding past judgment. In deal work it means past documents.) A firm that has closed hundreds of deals holds a private dataset of where each term landed and why. That dataset is its real asset, more than any individual's memory. Published surveys add a public benchmark. For example, the CMS European M&A Study 2026 reports that the seller's total liability was capped below half of the purchase price in 58% of the deals it covered. Each survey is sample-specific, and there is no public dataset of deal terms for the Gulf, so "market" in the UAE is practitioner judgment informed by private precedent.

The seller's lawyer says: "A cap on our liability of 10% of the price is market." You act for the buyer. Which response does the most work?

  • "We disagree. Our client requires 100%."
  • "Market for which deals? Size, seller type and whether insurance is in place all move it. On a deal like this one, what precedent are you pointing to?"
  • "The law does not allow caps that low."
  • "Fine, if that is market we accept."

"Market" is a claim about a distribution, so the useful challenge is to ask which sample. A flat counter-demand gives the other side nothing to engage with, and there is no legal rule setting a minimum cap. Accepting on the word "market" alone is how inexperienced parties lose points.

Two sellers each accepted a 10% general warranty cap. What would you want to know before advising a third seller to open there?

Check whether each buyer had insurance, what price the percentage applied to, who controlled the sale process, and what each seller received in exchange. An agreed percentage records the outcome. It rarely records which concession persuaded the other side. Identify that uncertainty when you present the comparison.

How lawyers communicate, and why they hedge

Lawyers negotiate by exchanging documents, not speeches.

  • Markup or redline: a version of the draft showing one side's changes, deletions struck through and insertions underlined. It is a diff. Each exchange is called a turn. Who holds the pen on the first draft sets the baseline that the other side must argue against.
  • Issues list: the table of open points described above. Senior people negotiate from it, then juniors conform the draft.
  • "Subject to contract": a label meaning "nothing here binds us until a formal contract is signed". It keeps a negotiation email from becoming an accidental deal.
  • "Without prejudice": a label for genuine attempts to settle a dispute. Statements made under it generally cannot be shown to the court as admissions. It belongs to disputes, not to deal negotiation.
  • "Subject to": in a document or an email, it makes what follows override what came before. "We can accept clause 5, subject to the points below" is a conditional yes.
  • Caveat: a stated limit on advice. "Based on the documents provided", "we have not verified", "this is not tax advice".

Read the assumptions behind a qualified answer. If counsel thinks a consent is “likely”, ask which contract they reviewed, whose signature is needed and what happens if it arrives late. That gives the board a concrete decision about timing and risk. If the wording leaves the answer uncertain, ask what additional evidence would change the advice.

You are negotiating a purchase by email and want to signal agreement on price without being bound yet. You write: "We agree a price of AED 76m." Which label should the email carry?

  • "Without prejudice"
  • "Subject to contract"
  • "Privileged and confidential"
  • No label is needed, because emails cannot form contracts

"Subject to contract" says no binding deal exists until the formal document is signed. "Without prejudice" is the tempting wrong answer: it protects settlement talks in a dispute from being used as admissions, which is a different job. Emails can form binding contracts if the elements of a contract are present, which is exactly why the label matters.

A partner's memo says: "On the documents we have reviewed, it is arguable that the buyer could terminate." How should you read this?

  • The buyer can probably terminate
  • The partner has not formed a view
  • There is a respectable argument but the partner would not bet on it, and the view depends on documents not yet seen
  • The buyer cannot terminate

"Arguable" is deliberately weaker than "likely": a case can be made, but it is under even odds. "On the documents we have reviewed" is a scope caveat, not filler: other documents could change the answer. Reading hedged language as a confident yes is the most common client error.

In the room with a senior partner

What a partner sells is a prediction: given these parties, this leverage and this precedent, each open term will land about here, and the residual risk is or is not worth the price. That is the same output this course trains you to produce. So your position in the room is not a student of law. You are someone trying to model the partner's judgment explicitly. That is interesting to a good partner, provided you show you know what you do not know.

SayDo not say
"In the deals you have seen, where does this usually land, and what moves it?""Market is X." (Unless you name the study and its sample.)
"What would have to be true for the seller to accept that?""The law says…" about any point of local law.
"Is that a legal constraint or a negotiating position?""That clause is just boilerplate."
"If you had to put odds on it, better or worse than even?""Software will do this instead of associates."
"Which facts would change your view?"Questions about a named client's confidential deal.

"I am not going to pretend to know the law. What I want to understand is how you decide where a point will settle: what you look at first, and which facts shift your view."

Before reading on: why is "is that a legal constraint or a negotiating position?" such a productive question?

It separates two kinds of "no". A legal constraint (a statute forbids it, a court will not enforce it) cannot be traded and no amount of leverage moves it. A negotiating position can be bought with price or with a concession elsewhere. Lawyers on the other side often present positions as constraints. A partner hears that question as a sign you understand the game, and the answer tells you which rows of the issues list are actually negotiable.

A senior partner explains that sellers backed by investment funds usually refuse to leave money behind after the sale to cover claims. Which reply shows the most useful understanding?

  • "That seems unfair to buyers. Shouldn't the law require it?"
  • "Our model already knows that from the data."
  • "So when the buyer still needs cover, what usually fills the gap, and does that change where the liability limit lands?"
  • "So buyers of fund-owned companies simply accept more risk?"

The best reply treats the statement as one input and asks how it propagates to other terms, which is exactly how landing points are predicted. The last option is tempting but assumes the risk has nowhere else to go, when it can be moved to an insurer or into the price. Appeals to fairness miss that deal terms are bargained, and claiming your model already knows it closes the conversation you came to have.

Key terms

Scope of advice
The questions the adviser has agreed to answer, the law it is advising on, and the assumptions and limits on reliance.
Jurisdiction
A territory with its own legal system and courts, and the unit in which a lawyer is licensed.
Local counsel
A law firm admitted in the place where the target operates, hired by the lead firm to advise on that place's law.
Equity partner
An owner of the law firm who is paid a share of its profit and is responsible for winning work and signing off advice.
Leverage (law firm)
The ratio of fee-earning staff to equity partners, which determines how much surplus each partner's client relationships generate.
Engagement letter
The contract between a law firm and its client fixing scope, fees, the identity of the client and the firm's liability limit.
Conflict of interest
A situation where a firm's duty to one client collides with its duty to another client or with its own interest.
Legal privilege
The client's right to withhold confidential lawyer-client communications made for legal advice from courts, regulators and opponents.
Risk allocation
The assignment, by contract, of each possible bad outcome to the party who will bear its cost.
Caveat emptor
"Buyer beware": the default rule that a buyer bears any risk it did not bargain to shift to the seller.
Market practice
The range of terms that parties in comparable recent deals have agreed, used as a benchmark in negotiation.
Precedent (transactional)
A firm's past signed deal documents and templates, used as the starting point for drafting and as evidence of market practice.
Redline
A version of a draft that displays one side's insertions and deletions against the previous version.
Issues list
A table of the open points in a negotiation showing each side's position and a proposed resolution.
Subject to contract
A label stating that nothing in a communication is binding until a formal contract is signed.
