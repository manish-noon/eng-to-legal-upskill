Indemnities, tax, insurance and recourse: where the money actually comes from

After this module you can choose the right tool for each diligence finding (price, indemnity or warranty), trace a claim through an insurance stack, and test whether a legal right to be paid is backed by money you can reach.

Specific indemnities: the tool for known risks

Module 7 ended with a rule: disclosure kills the warranty claim, so a known problem needs something else. That something is a specific indemnity: a promise by the seller to reimburse a named loss if it occurs.

The Seller shall pay to the Buyer on demand an amount equal to all Losses suffered or incurred by the Buyer or any Group Company arising out of or in connection with the Distributor Claim, whether or not Disclosed.

Decode: "an amount equal to all Losses" is pound for pound reimbursement (in a dirham deal, dirham for dirham): the loss itself, not the fall in share value. "Or any Group Company" matters because the company suffers the loss and the buyer holds the claim. "Whether or not Disclosed" switches off the disclosure defence. "Losses" is a defined term that normally includes legal costs.

Why it is stronger than a warranty, mechanism by mechanism:

Warranty claimSpecific indemnity
NatureDamages for breach of contractDrafted to operate as a claim for an agreed sum once the trigger occurs (a debt claim). English courts have not settled that every indemnity works this way, so the wording matters
AmountDiminution in share value, which the buyer must proveThe loss itself
Remoteness and mitigationApplyArguably do not apply unless the clause says so. Sellers add a mitigation duty to remove the doubt
Buyer's knowledge and disclosureDisclosure defeats the claim. The buyer's own knowledge may also defeat it (module 7)Irrelevant: the risk was named because it was known
Limitations pipelineDe minimis, basket, general capOutside all three. Own cap and own time limit

Because it is so strong, sellers negotiate its edges: a cap (often the realistic worst case), a time limit matched to when the risk will resolve, a tight definition of the indemnified matter, and conduct of claims (the seller runs the defence, since it is paying).

Diligence findingIndemnity shape
Pending lawsuit by a former distributor for AED 4mAll losses and costs from that claim. Seller conducts the defence. Lasts until final judgment or settlement
VAT assessment under disputeTax, penalties and interest for the named periods. Time limit follows the appeal process
Data breach six months before signingRegulatory action, customer claims and remediation costs from that incident. Whether a fine can lawfully be indemnified varies by jurisdiction
Contaminated siteClean-up costs required by law. Long time limit, cap, and a rule that the buyer must not provoke the clean-up
Before reading on: the seller says "we will just give you a warranty that the distributor claim has no merit". Why is that worth much less than the indemnity?

Four reasons. The claim is disclosed, so the seller will argue the warranty is qualified by it. "No merit" is an opinion, and a court can find it was honestly held even if the company loses. If you do establish breach, you receive the diminution in share value at the sale date, which is a discounted number, not the AED 4m. And it runs through the de minimis, basket and general cap. The indemnity removes all four problems: it pays the actual loss when it happens.

Tax covenant and leakage indemnity

Two standing indemnities appear in most deals without any specific finding.

The tax covenant (also called a tax deed when it is a separate document) is the English-style promise that all tax of the target for periods and events up to closing is the seller's, pound for pound, unless it was provided for in the accounts used to set the price. The reasoning is allocation by period: the seller took the profits of those years, so it bears their tax. It sits alongside the tax warranties. The warranties force disclosure. The covenant pays. Its time limit is long because the tax authority can reopen old years, often 4 to 7 years in the reference data.

US-style agreements reach the same result with a pre-closing tax indemnity inside the indemnification article, plus rules allocating tax for a period that straddles closing.
Do not assume the 4 to 7 year range fits a UAE target. Ask tax counsel which periods are actually open for each tax (corporate tax, VAT, customs) before agreeing the time limit.

The leakage indemnity belongs to locked-box deals. The price was fixed at a past balance sheet date, so any value the seller extracted since then (dividends, fees, bonuses to itself) must come back pound for pound. It is outside the pipeline and has a short time limit, because leakage shows up quickly in the bank statements. Each seller usually repays only what it received.

Choosing the tool

Every diligence finding goes to one of these destinations. Pick by how certain and how measurable the problem is.

The problem isToolWhy
Certain and quantified (an unpaid tax bill)Price: treat it as a debt-like itemMoney you keep has no collection risk
Known, but uncertain in outcome or amount (a lawsuit)Specific indemnity, ideally backed by escrowA price cut overpays the buyer if the risk never happens, and underpays if it does
Known and fixable before closing (a missing consent)Condition or pre-closing covenantRemoves the problem instead of pricing it
Unknown (anything diligence did not find)Warranty, with or without insuranceOnly tool that covers what nobody has identified
Known, small or unpriceableAccepted riskNegotiating capital is finite

Diligence finds that the target under-declared VAT in two past quarters. The amount is AED 3m including penalties, the target's own advisers agree with the figure, and it will be paid on the next return. Which tool is best for the buyer?

  • A tax warranty that all VAT has been correctly declared
  • A specific indemnity for the VAT liability
  • A AED 3m reduction in the price, as a debt-like item
  • Rely on the tax covenant

The liability is certain and quantified, so it is simply debt the company owes. Deducting it leaves the buyer holding the cash, with no claim to bring and no risk the seller cannot pay. An indemnity or the tax covenant would reach the same number only after a claim process and only if the seller is still good for it. The warranty is useless: the issue is known and will be disclosed. Indemnities are for uncertainty. Certainty goes to price.

A former employee is suing the target for AED 6m. Both sides' lawyers think the claim is weak but cannot rule out a loss. It will take about two years to resolve. The seller refuses any price reduction. What should the buyer ask for?

  • A AED 6m price reduction or no deal
  • An unqualified litigation warranty with a pro-sandbagging clause
  • A specific indemnity for the claim, with part of the price held in escrow until it resolves
  • Nothing: a weak claim is an accepted risk

The risk is known but uncertain, the pattern for an indemnity. It lets the seller keep the full price if it is right about the claim, which is why a confident seller can accept it, while the buyer is covered for the actual loss. The escrow makes the promise collectable in two years. A full price cut over-compensates the buyer on the expected outcome and will be refused. A warranty for a disclosed claim fails, and pro-sandbagging wording is unreliable for known matters under English law. A possible AED 6m is too large to ignore.

The target is a software company. Diligence on its code and licences found nothing wrong, but you still worry that some component infringes a third party's rights. Which tool fits?

  • A specific indemnity for all IP infringement
  • An IP warranty, negotiated for minimal knowledge qualification
  • A price reduction for the estimated risk
  • A condition that no infringement claim exists at closing

There is no identified problem, so there is nothing to name in an indemnity, nothing to quantify for a price cut and nothing for a condition to test. Unknown risk is what warranties are for, and it is also what insurance will cover. A seller will refuse a blanket indemnity for an unidentified risk because it would bypass every limitation: it is a warranty with the filters removed. The real negotiation is the knowledge qualifier, which decides who bears infringement nobody knew about.

Recourse: can you actually collect?

A claim is an option on the seller's future solvency. Its value is the amount of the right multiplied by the probability that money is still there and reachable when you win. Three common seller profiles make that probability low:

  • A PE fund distributes sale proceeds to its investors within weeks and is later wound up.
  • Individuals can move assets, and themselves, to places where enforcing a judgment or award is slow.
  • A special purpose vehicle (SPV) seller, a company that exists only to hold the shares, is an empty shell the day after it pays the price up to its owners.

So for every protection in the SPA, ask a second question: against whom, and backed by what? The answers are forms of security.

SecurityMechanismWho it favours
EscrowPart of the price is paid to a neutral escrow agent (a bank or law firm). It is released to the seller on a set date, less amounts for claims. Release needs joint instructions or a court or tribunal decisionBuyer for collectability. Neutral on leverage, since neither side holds the cash
Holdback (retention)The buyer simply keeps part of the price and pays it later, less claimsBuyer strongly: the seller must sue to get its own money, and bears the buyer's credit risk
Deferred consideration with set-offPrice instalments or earn-out payments fall due later. A set-off right lets the buyer deduct claims from themBuyer, if set-off covers claims that are merely notified. Sellers limit it to claims agreed or finally determined
GuaranteeA parent company or individual with real assets promises to pay if the seller does notBuyer. Essential against an SPV seller
Joint and several liabilityEach seller answers for the whole claim (module 7)Buyer: the richest seller becomes the guarantor of the rest

Escrow terms to negotiate: the amount, the duration (normally the general time limit), whether release is staged, and what is kept back for pending claims at the release date.

On the Release Date the Escrow Agent shall pay to the Seller the balance of the Escrow Account less the aggregate Estimated Liability of all Outstanding Claims.

Decode: a claim notified but unresolved at the release date freezes its estimated amount in the account. Sellers ask that the estimate be reasonable and supported by counsel's opinion, to stop a buyer freezing the fund with an inflated notice.

Market data: the SRS Acquiom 2026 study (as summarised by DealLawyers.com) found an escrow or holdback in 88% of 2025 deals, with a median of 10.0% of deal value without RWI and 2.8% with RWI. Fasken puts the median indemnity-only escrow on RWI deals at 0.5%. This is US data from deals where SRS acted.

Enforcement happens where the assets are. UAE deals commonly choose arbitration, and an award from a DIFC-seated arbitration can be taken to the onshore courts for enforcement. That adds time and cost, which is one more reason to hold security instead of relying on a claim.

Price AED 76m, with 10% paid into escrow for 18 months under the release clause above. In month 9 a warranty claim is settled and AED 1.1m is paid to the buyer from the account. In month 16 the buyer notifies a second claim with an Estimated Liability of AED 2.4m, still unresolved at month 18. Ignore interest. How much is released to the seller on the Release Date? Answer in AED.

Escrow funded: 10% × 76m = 7.6m. Less the settled claim: 7.6m - 1.1m = 6.5m balance. Less the Estimated Liability of the outstanding claim: 6.5m - 2.4m = 4,100,000 released. The 2.4m stays frozen until the second claim ends. If it settles at 1.5m, the buyer receives 1.5m and the remaining 0.9m goes to the seller.

The buyer owes deferred consideration of AED 10m at month 12. The SPA lets the buyer set off only claims that are agreed or finally determined. At month 12 the buyer has an indemnity claim the seller has agreed at AED 3m, and a disputed warranty claim it values at AED 4m. How much must the buyer pay at month 12? Answer in AED.

Only the agreed 3m can be set off: 10m - 3m = 7,000,000. The disputed 4m must be pursued separately, and the buyer has just paid away the money it would have collected from. Had the clause allowed set-off of notified claims, the buyer would pay 3m and hold 4m as security. That one phrase decides who finances the dispute.

You bought a company from an SPV owned by a private equity fund. The SPA has a specific indemnity for an environmental clean-up, uncapped, with a ten-year time limit and no disclosure defence. There is no escrow and no guarantee. The SPV paid the whole price up to the fund a week after closing. Three years later the clean-up costs AED 9m and you obtain an arbitration award for that sum. Which one change at signing would have altered the outcome most?

  • A pro-sandbagging clause
  • Moving the clean-up into the warranties with a 100% cap
  • An escrow sized to the clean-up, or a guarantee from the fund
  • A longer time limit and a higher cap on the indemnity

The legal right was already as strong as drafting can make it: pound for pound, uncapped, in time, proved to an award. It failed for one reason: the debtor is an empty shell. No improvement to the right helps. Only security does. Buyer's knowledge was never the issue with an indemnity, and a warranty would be weaker and equally uncollectable. Check the seller's balance sheet after closing, not before.

Think first: a seller is confident a disputed tax position will be upheld. Would it prefer a AED 2m price cut or AED 5m in escrow for three years?

If it believes the chance of losing is low, the escrow is better: expected cost is the probability of losing times the amount, plus the time value of AED 5m for three years, which may be far below a certain AED 2m. The buyer may also prefer the escrow, because it covers the full downside. This is why known uncertain risks settle as indemnity plus escrow: each side backs its own probability estimate. A seller who insists on a small price cut and refuses an escrow is telling you something about its real estimate.

W&I insurance

W&I insurance (warranty and indemnity insurance; in the US, RWI, representations and warranties insurance) replaces the seller with an insurer as payer for unknown warranty breaches. In the standard buy-side policy the buyer is the insured.

  • Retention: the first layer of loss, borne by the buyer (or by the seller if the SPA cap is set at that level). It works like a deductible basket.
  • Policy limit: the most the insurer pays above the retention. The buyer chooses how much to buy. It plays the role of the cap.
  • Seller's cap: reduced in the SPA to a nominal amount such as AED 1, or a small percentage.
  • Subrogation is an insurer's right, after paying, to step into the insured's position and sue whoever caused the loss. The insurer waives it against the seller, except for fraud. Without that waiver the seller would have no clean exit and no reason to agree.
The aggregate liability of the Seller for all Business Warranty Claims shall not exceed AED 1. The Buyer's sole recourse for such Claims shall be under the W&I Policy, whether or not the W&I Policy responds.

Decode: the last seven words put the risk of exclusions, insurer insolvency and declined claims on the buyer.

Switch on the W&I toggle in the simulator and watch the payer change: the seller's cap drops to a nominal amount and the retention and policy limit take over from the basket and cap.

What is always excluded. The policy covers the unknown only. Standard exclusions: anything the buyer's deal team knew, including everything in the diligence reports and the disclosure letter. Forward-looking warranties such as forecasts. Price adjustments and leakage. Certain taxes. Fines that cannot lawfully be insured. Per Sidley (May 2026), Middle East policies now also carry war, civil unrest and sanctions exclusions, with Iran uninsurable and Iraq largely off-limits.

Underwriting. The insurer cannot inspect the target. It inspects your diligence: it reads your advisers' reports and questions them on a call. Its rule is mechanical. Where there was no diligence, or thin diligence, the matching warranties are excluded. The insurer is protecting itself against adverse selection: a buyer that does not look will not find, and would otherwise insure the problems it chose not to see. So insurance does not replace diligence. It requires it.

Cost and usage. Marsh (Q1 2026) reports premiums of 0.7% to 1.8% of the policy limit in the Middle East, with retentions of 0.15% to 0.5% of enterprise value. The UK is at 0.4% to 1.2% and the US and Canada at 3.0% to 3.7%. W&I appeared in 23% of European deals and 59% of large ones (CMS 2026), in 63% of US deals (ABA 2025) and in about 46% in the SRS Acquiom 2026 sample. Claims are being made: Aon 2026 reports EMEA claim notifications up 47%, from 70 to 119. Who pays the premium is negotiated. Economically it comes out of the price either way.

Enterprise value AED 100m, price AED 76m. Buy-side W&I policy: retention 0.5% of enterprise value, limit AED 20m. Seller's cap for business warranties: AED 1. An unknown, covered accounts warranty breach causes a loss of AED 8m. How much does the insurer pay? Answer in AED.

Retention = 0.5% × 100m = 500k, borne by the buyer. Insurer pays the loss above the retention, up to the limit: 8m - 0.5m = 7.5m, which is below 20m, so 7,500,000. The seller pays AED 1 at most. Note the base: the retention is a percentage of enterprise value, not of price.

Enterprise value AED 150m. W&I retention 0.4% of enterprise value, policy limit AED 15m. Seller's cap for business warranties AED 1. A covered business warranty breach causes a loss of AED 25m. Ignoring the AED 1, how much of the loss does the buyer bear itself? Answer in AED.

Retention = 0.4% × 150m = 600k. Loss above retention = 24.4m. The limit is 15m, so the insurer pays 15m. The buyer bears the retention (0.6m) plus the excess above the policy (25m - 0.6m - 15m = 9.4m): 10,000,000 in total. The policy limit is the real cap in an insured deal, and it is a purchasing decision the buyer makes for itself.

To save fees, the buyer does no tax diligence on the target's Saudi subsidiary. The deal has W&I insurance and a AED 1 seller cap. A year later the subsidiary receives an unexpected tax assessment of AED 4m for a pre-closing year. There is no tax covenant from the seller because of the insurance structure. What is the likely outcome?

  • The insurer pays above the retention: the liability was unknown
  • The seller pays: tax for pre-closing periods is always the seller's
  • The buyer bears it: the insurer excluded the undiligenced area and the seller is capped at AED 1
  • The insurer pays and then recovers from the seller by subrogation

Unknown is necessary for cover, not sufficient. The insurer covers only areas the buyer investigated, so Saudi tax would have been excluded at underwriting. "Pre-closing tax is the seller's" is true only if a tax covenant says so, and here the seller's liability is nominal. Subrogation against the seller is waived except for fraud. The saved diligence fee bought an uninsured, unindemnified gap. In an insured deal, diligence scope is cover scope.

Your diligence report flags that the target has been processing customer data without required consents. The deal has W&I insurance and a AED 1 seller cap. The SPA includes an unqualified data-protection warranty and the seller makes no disclosure of the issue. After closing a regulator acts and the cost is AED 2m. Where does the loss fall?

  • On the insurer: the warranty is unqualified and was breached
  • On the seller: it failed to disclose
  • On the buyer: the matter was known to the deal team, so it is excluded, and the seller's cap is AED 1
  • On the insurer, less the retention, because the seller did not disclose it

The policy tests the buyer's knowledge, not the seller's disclosure. A matter in the buyer's own diligence report is known and excluded, however the warranty is drafted. The seller is in breach but owes AED 1. A known issue in an insured deal needs a specific indemnity from the seller outside the nominal cap, or a price reduction. Insurance changed who pays for the unknown. It changed nothing for the known.

How insurance rewrites the negotiation

With a AED 1 cap the seller stops caring about warranty scope, knowledge qualifiers and the basket, since none of them costs it anything. The real counterparty for those terms becomes the insurer, which will not cover warranties it considers unreasonable. The fights of module 8 move into the policy: retention for basket, limit for cap, policy period for time limit. Three things stay with the seller: fundamental warranties, fraud, and every known risk. The full recourse stack for a AED 76m price with a AED 20m policy:

LayerWho paysCovers
1. Retention (AED 0.5m)Buyer, or seller if the SPA cap is set at the retentionFirst loss on unknown breaches
2. Policy (next AED 20m)InsurerUnknown breaches of covered warranties
3a. Above the policy, business warrantiesBuyerNobody stands behind this layer
3b. Above the policy, fundamental warrantiesSeller, up to the price (AED 76m)Title and capacity
Outside the stack: known risks and exclusionsSeller under specific indemnities (backed by escrow), or the priceEverything the policy excludes that the seller agrees to cover
Outside the stack: fraudSeller, unlimited. The insurer pays the buyer and may then pursue the sellerDishonesty

Use the stack above. The SPA makes the seller liable for fundamental warranty losses to the extent not recovered under the policy, up to the price. It emerges that the seller did not validly own a large block of the shares, and the covered loss is AED 40m. The insurer pays its full entitlement. How much must the seller pay? Answer in AED.

Insurer: loss above the 0.5m retention is 39.5m, limited to 20m. Unrecovered loss = 40m - 20m = 20m, which includes the retention. The seller is liable for all of it, since 20m is within the 76m fundamental cap: 20,000,000. The buyer is made whole only if the seller can pay, so recourse for the fundamental layer still needs thought even in an insured deal.

"The policy only covers what we don't know. For the three items in our red-flag report we need indemnities outside the one-dirham cap, with an escrow behind them, or we take them off the price."
Think first: with W&I in place the seller will agree to almost any warranty. Why does that not give the buyer a perfect set of warranties?

Because the seller is no longer the party bearing the cost, so its consent is no longer the constraint. The insurer is. It reviews the warranties and will exclude or read down those it finds too broad or unsupported by diligence, such as forward-looking statements or warranties with no knowledge qualifier on matters nobody could verify. An over-generous SPA warranty that the policy does not cover is worth AED 1. The buyer's real work shifts to matching the policy to the SPA and closing the gaps between them.

Think first: if the insurer waives subrogation against the seller, what stops a seller from giving careless warranties?

Three things. The waiver does not cover fraud, so a seller who knowingly gives a false warranty can be pursued for the full loss. The insurer does its own check through the buyer's diligence and excludes weak areas, so careless warranties in undiligenced areas are simply not covered. And management who give the warranties usually stay with the business under the buyer, which is its own discipline. It is still true that the seller's incentive to disclose carefully is weaker in an insured deal, which is why the buyer's diligence must be real.

Key terms

Specific indemnity
A seller's promise to reimburse the buyer for the actual loss from a named, known risk, outside the warranty limitations and unaffected by disclosure.
Pound for pound
Reimbursement of the full amount of the loss itself, as opposed to the resulting fall in the value of the shares.
Tax covenant (tax deed)
The English-style indemnity under which the seller pays the target's tax for periods and events up to closing, unless provided for in the accounts.
Leakage indemnity
The locked-box promise to repay, pound for pound, any value extracted by the seller from the target after the locked box date.
Recourse
The practical ability to collect on a claim, which depends on who the debtor is and what assets or security stand behind it.
Escrow
Part of the price held by a neutral agent and released to the seller on a set date, less amounts paid or reserved for claims.
Holdback (retention of price)
Part of the price kept by the buyer itself and paid later, less claims.
Set-off
The right to deduct an amount the other party owes you from an amount you owe it.
Guarantee
A promise by a parent company or individual to pay the seller's obligations if the seller does not.
W&I insurance (RWI)
A policy, usually bought by the buyer, under which an insurer pays for losses from unknown breaches of warranty in place of the seller.
Retention (insurance)
The first layer of loss under a W&I policy, which the insurer does not pay.
Policy limit
The maximum the insurer will pay under the policy above the retention.
Subrogation
An insurer's right, after paying a claim, to pursue the person who caused the loss, waived against the seller in W&I policies except for fraud.
SPV seller
A company that exists only to hold the target's shares and has no assets once it has passed the price to its owners.
