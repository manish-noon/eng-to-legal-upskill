Contract foundations: the physics underneath every deal

After this module you can classify any promise in a contract, work out what the innocent party can actually recover when it is broken, and explain why the same clause can behave differently in an English, DIFC or onshore UAE court.

What makes a contract

A contract is a promise or set of promises that a court will enforce. English law requires five ingredients.

  1. Offer: a proposal on definite terms, made with the intent to be bound if accepted.
  2. Acceptance: unqualified agreement to those terms. A reply that changes the terms is a counter-offer, which kills the original offer.
  3. Consideration: each side must give something of value in exchange: money, goods, an act, or a promise. A one-way promise ("I will give you my shares") is not enforceable as a contract. Courts do not check that the exchange is fair, only that it exists.
  4. Intention to create legal relations: presumed in business dealings unless the parties say otherwise, for example with "subject to contract".
  5. Certainty: the terms must be clear enough for a court to enforce. "The parties will agree a fair price later" is an agreement to agree and usually fails.

A deed is a document signed with extra formality (it must state that it is a deed and each individual's signature is witnessed). A deed is binding without consideration. Lawyers use deeds where one side gives nothing back, such as a guarantee by a parent company, and to get a longer time limit for claims, as you will see below.

Express terms are the words the parties wrote. Implied terms are added by statute, by custom, or by a court where a term is so obvious or so necessary that the contract does not work without it. English courts imply terms sparingly. So professionally drafted contracts try to be complete, and usually contain an entire agreement clause stating that the document is the whole deal and that nothing said earlier forms part of it.

A seller emails: "I offer you my company for AED 50m, open until Friday." On Wednesday the buyer replies: "Agreed at AED 50m, provided you stay on as CEO for two years." On Thursday the buyer writes: "Forget the CEO point, I accept your original offer." The seller refuses. Is there a contract?

  • Yes, the buyer accepted before Friday
  • Yes, the price was agreed on Wednesday and the CEO point is a detail
  • No, the Wednesday reply was a counter-offer that ended the original offer, so there was nothing left to accept on Thursday
  • No, because a sale of a company must be made by deed

Acceptance must mirror the offer. Adding a condition makes a counter-offer, and a counter-offer destroys the original. The Friday deadline is the tempting distraction: it only matters while the offer is alive. There is no general rule that a company sale needs a deed.

Five kinds of promise, and why the label matters

Contract terms are not all the same type. The label decides what the innocent party gets when the term is broken. A term is broken, or breached, when a party fails to do what it promised or a statement it stood behind turns out false.

TypeWhat it isIf it fails
ConditionA term so central that breaking it undermines the whole bargain.The innocent party may end the contract and claim damages.
WarrantyIn general contract law, a less central term. In deal contracts, a statement of fact about the thing sold ("the Company owns its software").Damages only. The contract stays alive.
RepresentationA statement of fact made before the contract that induces the other side to sign.If false, it is a misrepresentation. The remedy can include rescission: unwinding the contract as if it had never been made.
Covenant or undertakingA promise to do or not do something in future ("the Seller will not compete for two years").Damages, and often a court order to comply.
IndemnityA promise to reimburse a defined loss if a defined event occurs ("the Seller will pay the Buyer the amount of any pre-sale tax").A claim for the stated sum itself.

Damages means money a court orders the party in breach to pay as compensation. Terms that fit neither label cleanly are treated by English courts as innominate: the remedy depends on how serious the actual breach turns out to be.

Now the mechanism behind a classic negotiation. Buyers ask the seller to "represent and warrant". Sellers under English law strike out "represent". Why? A warranty claim is a contract claim: damages, limited by everything the contract says about limits. A representation claim sits partly outside the contract, in the law of misrepresentation. It opens the door to rescission and to damages measured a different way (putting the buyer where it would be had it never signed, not where it would be had the statement been true). Nobody wants a sold company handed back two years later.

US contracts say "represents and warrants" as a fixed pair and little usually turns on the distinction. Protection there comes from a negotiated chapter of the contract on reimbursement, called indemnification, which covers breaches of every statement. English-style contracts split the work: warranties with a damages claim for the unknown, and specific indemnities for known problems.

The Seller warrants to the Buyer that each of the Warranties is true and accurate at the date of this Agreement. The Buyer acknowledges that it has not relied on any representation other than the Warranties, and its sole remedy for any breach of the Warranties shall be a claim for damages for breach of contract.

Decode: the seller gives warranties only, the buyer confirms nothing else induced it to sign (closing off misrepresentation claims), and rescission is excluded.

A buyer discovers a year after buying a company that a statement in the contract about its licences was false. The buyer would like to hand the company back and recover the price. Under an English-law contract containing the clause above, what is the likely position?

  • The buyer can rescind, because a false statement always allows rescission
  • The buyer is limited to damages, because the statement was given as a warranty only and rescission was excluded
  • The buyer has no remedy, because it should have checked the licences
  • The buyer can terminate, because the licence statement is a condition

The drafting did its job: warranty only, no reliance on other statements, damages as sole remedy. Rescission belongs to misrepresentation, which the clause shuts out (fraud is always an exception). "Buyer should have checked" is wrong here because the buyer did bargain for the warranty. Nothing makes the statement a condition.

Breach and what you can recover

The default remedy is expectation damages: money to put the innocent party where it would have been if the contract had been performed. Not a punishment, not a refund. Four filters then cut the claim down.

  • Proof of loss. The claimant must prove it actually lost something, with evidence. A breach with no provable loss gives nominal damages (a token sum).
  • Causation. The breach must have caused the loss. If the loss would have happened anyway, it is not recoverable.
  • Remoteness. Loss that was too unforeseeable is excluded. The rule comes from Hadley v Baxendale (1854) and has two limbs. Limb one: loss arising naturally from the breach in the ordinary course of things. Limb two: unusual loss that both parties had in contemplation when they signed, because the special circumstances were communicated. A carrier who delivers a mill shaft late pays for ordinary delay, but not for the mill standing idle unless it was told the mill had no spare.
  • Mitigation. The innocent party must take reasonable steps to reduce its loss. Loss it could reasonably have avoided is not recoverable.

A supplier agreed to deliver a machine for AED 2,000,000, not yet paid. It fails to deliver. An identical machine was available elsewhere for AED 2,600,000 with one week's lead time. The buyer waited, and its replacement machine arrived three weeks after the breach. The buyer loses profit of AED 150,000 for each week without a machine, a loss the supplier knew about. What can the buyer recover? Answer in AED.

Extra cost of the replacement: 2,600,000 − 2,000,000 = 600,000. Lost profit: three weeks were lost, but a reasonable buyer would have had the replacement in one week, so only 1 × 150,000 = 150,000 survives mitigation. Total: 600,000 + 150,000 = 750,000. The other 300,000 of lost profit was caused by the buyer's own delay.

A software vendor delivers a system two months late. The customer, a retailer, claims (a) the cost of temporary staff to process orders manually and (b) the loss of a uniquely lucrative government contract it could not bid for without the system, which it never mentioned to the vendor. Which is recoverable?

  • Both, because the delay caused both
  • (a) only: it arises naturally from the delay, while (b) is unusual loss the vendor was never told about
  • (b) only, because it is the larger loss
  • Neither, because delay is not a breach

Causation is satisfied for both, which is why "both" tempts. Remoteness is a separate filter. Temporary staffing is limb-one loss. The government contract is limb-two loss, recoverable only if the special circumstance was in both parties' contemplation at signing. The practical lesson: if you depend on something unusual, say so in the contract.

Contracts often exclude "indirect or consequential loss". Check how the exclusion treats lost profits. English courts have traditionally read this exclusion as covering limb-two loss. Ordinary lost profit is often limb one, so it is direct loss and not excluded unless the clause names "loss of profit" expressly.

Debt, indemnities and non-money remedies

A debt claim is a claim for a fixed sum that the contract says is payable when an event occurs. The claimant proves the event and the amount. Remoteness and mitigation, the filters built for damages claims, generally do not apply in the same way. An indemnity is drafted to create a debt-like claim: "if X happens, pay me the amount of X". That is why lawyers call an indemnity pound for pound (dollar for dollar in the US). How far mitigation applies to an indemnity depends on its wording, so careful drafters say so expressly.

The Seller shall pay to the Buyer on demand an amount equal to all Losses suffered or incurred by the Buyer or the Company arising out of or in connection with the Customs Investigation.

Decode: a named risk, a promise to pay its full cost, "on demand", for loss to the buyer or to the company it bought. No need to show that the shares fell in value.

After buying a company you find it owes AED 3,000,000 in pre-sale tax. Had you filed a voluntary disclosure promptly, the bill would have been AED 2,200,000, but you did not. Assume the fall in the value of your shares equals the tax bill. Route 1: a warranty claim for damages. Route 2: an indemnity for "an amount equal to any pre-sale tax liability", with no mitigation wording, which you should treat as a pure debt claim. How much more do you recover under Route 2 than Route 1? Answer in AED.

Route 1 is a damages claim, so mitigation applies: you recover the loss a reasonable buyer would have suffered, 2,200,000. Route 2 is a claim for the stated sum: 3,000,000. Difference: 3,000,000 − 2,200,000 = 800,000. A seller's lawyer who spots this adds "the Buyer shall take reasonable steps to mitigate" to the indemnity.

You paid 8 times annual profit for a company. The seller warranted that annual profit was AED 5,000,000. True recurring profit is AED 4,000,000 because one contract had already been lost. An indemnity in the contract covers "any loss suffered by the Company in the first year from the loss of that contract", which is AED 1,000,000. How much more is the warranty damages claim worth than the indemnity claim? Answer in AED.

Warranty damages put you where you would be if the statement were true. Value as warranted: 8 × 5,000,000 = 40,000,000. Actual value: 8 × 4,000,000 = 32,000,000. Damages: 8,000,000. The indemnity pays the stated loss only: 1,000,000. Difference: 7,000,000. An indemnity is not always better. It reimburses a cost, while a warranty claim can capture lost value at the price multiple.

Two remedies are not money. Specific performance is a court order to do what was promised. An injunction is a court order to stop doing something. Common-law courts grant them only when damages would be inadequate. Shares in a private company are unique, so a court will usually order a seller to complete a signed sale. A breach of a promise not to compete is hard to price, so courts grant injunctions.

Termination ends the contract from now on: future duties fall away, but rights already built up, including the right to damages, survive. Rescission, defined above, wipes the contract out from the start and returns each side what it gave. Termination is a response to serious breach or the exercise of a right the contract grants. Rescission is the response to a defect in how the contract was formed, such as misrepresentation.

Before reading on: a buyer has signed but not yet paid, and the seller gets a higher offer and refuses to complete. Why is the choice between damages and specific performance worth so much to the buyer?

Damages would be the difference between the contract price and the true value, which the buyer must prove, with years of litigation and a seller who may have spent the money. Specific performance delivers the company itself. It also changes the seller's incentives before any breach: if the seller knows a court will order the sale, the higher offer is worth nothing to it, so it does not breach. A remedy's main effect is on behaviour before the breach.

Agreed sums, liability limits and time limits

Parties can fix the consequences of breach in advance. Liquidated damages (LDs) are a sum agreed in the contract as payable on a specified breach, typically a daily rate for delay. The claimant does not need to prove its loss, and it cannot claim more if its real loss is higher. English law will not enforce a penalty: an agreed sum out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in having the contract performed. The modern test comes from Cavendish v Makdessi (2015), itself a dispute about a company sale. Commercial parties of equal strength rarely succeed in calling a clause a penalty.

A limitation clause caps liability, in amount or in time. An exclusion clause removes liability for a type of loss altogether. Both are risk allocation in its purest form: the price reflects that one side's exposure is bounded. Courts read them narrowly, and no clause can exclude liability for a party's own fraud.

An English-law contract with a price of AED 4,000,000 sets liquidated damages for delay at AED 40,000 per day, capped at 10% of the price. Delivery is 12 days late. The customer's real loss from the delay is AED 900,000. How much can the customer recover for the delay? Answer in AED.

LDs: 12 × 40,000 = 480,000. Cap: 10% × 4,000,000 = 400,000. The lower figure applies: 400,000. The real loss of 900,000 is irrelevant in both directions. LDs replace the damages claim for that breach. They are a floor with no need to prove loss, and also a ceiling.

Separately, statute sets a deadline for bringing any claim, called a limitation period. In England it is 6 years from the breach for an ordinary contract and 12 years for a deed. After that the claim is barred. Sellers do not want exposure for 6 or 12 years, so deal contracts replace the statutory period with a much shorter contractual one, and require the buyer to give written notice of a claim within it. The CMS European M&A Study 2026 reports that only 32% of deals allowed warranty claims for longer than 24 months.

A seller sold her company under a deed governed by English law. The deed says nothing about time limits for claims. Four years later the buyer finds a breach of warranty that occurred at signing. The seller says the claim is too late because "these claims always expire after two years". Who is right?

  • The seller: two years is market practice
  • The seller: the statutory period for contract claims is six years but warranty claims are shorter
  • The buyer: with no contractual time limit, the statutory period applies, and for a deed it is 12 years
  • The buyer: there is never a time limit on a claim for breach of warranty

Short claim periods exist only because contracts create them. Market practice is not law: if the clause is missing, the statute governs. The claim is within both the 6-year and the 12-year period, so the buyer wins either way, but the deed makes it 12. This is why a seller's lawyer never lets a time-limit clause be deleted.

Which law, which forum

Two families of legal system matter. In common law systems (England, the US, the DIFC, the ADGM) much of the law is made by judges through decided cases, and the guiding principle is freedom of contract: the court enforces what sophisticated parties wrote, even if harsh. In civil law systems (France, Germany, most of the Middle East including onshore UAE) the law is set out in a comprehensive code, and the code builds in general duties and judicial powers that the parties cannot always remove.

PointCommon law (England)Civil law (typical)
Good faithNo general duty. Each side may pursue its own interest.A general duty to perform, and often to negotiate, in good faith.
Agreed sums on breachEnforced as written unless a penalty.Penalty clauses are allowed, but the judge can often adjust the sum.
Main remedyDamages. Specific performance is exceptional.Performance is the starting point.
Contract lengthLong. The document must say everything.Shorter. The code fills gaps.

Onshore UAE is a civil-law system. Its courts may adjust agreed compensation to match actual loss, and some clauses common in English-style contracts, such as very short notification periods, may not work as drafted. The DIFC is a common-law jurisdiction with its own statutes, and the ADGM applies English common law directly. This is why most serious UAE deals are written under English, DIFC or ADGM law even when the target is onshore. Treat any onshore point as a question for locally qualified counsel.

Three separate choices sit at the back of every contract, and people confuse them.

  • Governing law: which system's rules are used to interpret the contract.
  • Jurisdiction: which courts hear disputes. A French court can apply English law, slowly.
  • Arbitration: an agreement to send disputes to a private tribunal in place of any court. The seat is the legal home of the arbitration, whose courts supervise it. The institution (such as the ICC, the LCIA or Dubai's DIAC) administers the case under its rules. The tribunal's decision is called an award.

The reason arbitration dominates cross-border deals is enforcement. Under the New York Convention of 1958, a treaty joined by most trading nations including the UAE, courts must enforce foreign arbitral awards with very limited grounds to refuse. No treaty of similar reach exists for court judgments. A judgment you cannot enforce where the loser keeps its assets is worth nothing. CMS 2026 reports an arbitration clause in 44% of European deals.

This Agreement is governed by English law. Any dispute arising out of or in connection with this Agreement shall be finally resolved by arbitration under the DIAC Arbitration Rules. The seat of arbitration shall be the DIFC. The language shall be English.

Decode: English rules of interpretation, a Dubai institution, and a common-law seat whose English-language courts supervise the process.

You are buying a company from a seller whose assets are all in a country whose courts are slow and rarely enforce foreign court judgments, but which has joined the New York Convention. You are choosing the dispute clause. Which is best for you?

  • English governing law with the English courts, because they are the most experienced
  • The seller's home courts, because the assets are there
  • English governing law with arbitration seated in a neutral Convention jurisdiction
  • No dispute clause, to keep all options open

The question is where you can collect. An English judgment is excellent paper but may not be enforced against assets abroad. An arbitral award travels under the Convention. Going to the seller's home courts gives up neutrality and speed. No clause at all means a preliminary fight over where to sue.

Before reading on: your English-law contract sets liquidated damages of AED 5m for a breach. The dispute ends up in a civil-law court that may adjust agreed sums. What two things could go wrong, and for whom?

If actual loss is AED 1m, the judge may cut the 5m down, hurting the claimant who thought it had certainty. If actual loss is AED 9m, some civil-law systems let the judge increase the sum, hurting the party in breach who thought 5m was its ceiling. The clause was meant to remove the need to prove loss, and the forum has brought it back. Choice of forum is part of the risk allocation, not an afterthought.

Drafting vocabulary that changes meaning

These phrases look like filler. Each one moves money.

  • Defined Terms. A capitalised word has only the meaning given in the definitions section. "Business Day", "Losses" and "Group" mean what the contract says, not what the dictionary says. Read the definition before the clause: it is where the type signature lives.
  • "Material". A threshold with no fixed number. "No material litigation" lets the seller ignore small claims, and moves the argument to what counts as material. Buyers replace it with a figure ("claims above AED 500,000").
  • "Best endeavours" v "reasonable endeavours". Both are promises to try, not to achieve. Under English law best endeavours is the heavier one: take every step a determined, prudent person acting in its own interest would take, even at some cost. Reasonable endeavours is lighter: take a reasonable course, with no duty to sacrifice your own commercial interests. "All reasonable endeavours" sits between them.
  • "Including without limitation". Stops a list from being read as complete. Without it, a court may confine a general word to things like the listed examples.
  • "Subject to". The clause yields to the one it names. "Subject to clause 10, the Seller shall pay…" means clause 10 wins.
  • "Notwithstanding". The opposite: this clause overrides the one it names. "Notwithstanding clause 10" means clause 10 loses.
  • "For the avoidance of doubt". Meant to clarify, not to change. Watch for a party using it to slip in a new right.
  • "Joint and several". Where several people owe one obligation, the claimant can recover the whole amount from any one of them, who must then chase the others. Several liability means each owes only its own share.

US contracts say "best efforts", "reasonable best efforts" and "commercially reasonable efforts". US courts do not apply a consistent ranking between them, so US drafters often define the standard in the contract.

Three sellers held 60%, 30% and 10% of a company and sold it for AED 100,000,000. They gave the warranties on a joint and several basis. The buyer has a valid claim of AED 10,000,000. The 60% and 30% sellers have moved abroad with their money. How much can the buyer claim from the 10% seller? Answer in AED.

Joint and several liability lets the buyer recover the whole claim from any one seller: 10,000,000, which here is the 10% seller's entire share of the price. Under several liability the answer would be 10% × 10,000,000 = 1,000,000. This is why minority sellers insist on several liability, each capped at the money it received.

Clause 8 says: "The Seller's total liability under this Agreement shall not exceed AED 20m." Clause 12 says: "Notwithstanding any other provision of this Agreement, the Seller shall indemnify the Buyer for all Losses arising from the Tax Audit." The tax audit costs AED 35m. What can the buyer most plausibly recover?

  • AED 20m, because clause 8 covers total liability
  • AED 35m, because "notwithstanding" makes clause 12 override the cap
  • AED 55m, because the cap and the indemnity add together
  • Nothing until a court decides which clause is valid

"Notwithstanding any other provision" puts clause 12 above clause 8, so the cap does not apply to the indemnity. The cap answer is tempting because "total liability" sounds absolute, but the order of precedence is set by the override word. A seller's lawyer would rewrite clause 12 to begin "Subject to clause 8".

Before a sale completes, the seller must obtain a regulator's approval. The regulator signals it will approve only if the seller also sells a profitable side business. Under which wording is the seller most likely obliged to make that sacrifice under English law?

  • "shall use reasonable endeavours to obtain the approval"
  • "shall use best endeavours to obtain the approval"
  • "shall cooperate in good faith in seeking the approval"
  • None: an endeavours clause can never require a party to act against its own interest

Best endeavours can require a party to bear real cost, though not to ruin itself, so whether this particular sacrifice is required would still be argued. Reasonable endeavours does not require giving up your own commercial interests. Because the standard is fuzzy, good drafting spells it out: "including by offering divestments of businesses with revenue below AED X".

Before reading on: why do lawyers write 80-page contracts when the commercial deal fits on one page?

In a common-law system the court will not rescue you with implied terms or good faith, so every bad outcome you have not allocated stays where it falls. Each page is a row in the risk allocation table: who pays if X. The length is the cost of freedom of contract. A civil-law contract can be shorter because the code supplies defaults, at the price of less control over what those defaults are.

Key terms

Consideration
The thing of value each party gives or promises in exchange, without which a promise is not enforceable as a contract.
Deed
A formally signed and witnessed document that binds without consideration and carries a 12-year limitation period in England.
Condition
A term so central that its breach lets the innocent party terminate the contract as well as claim damages.
Warranty
A contractual statement or term whose breach gives a claim for damages only.
Misrepresentation
A false statement of fact that induced a party to contract, which can allow rescission.
Rescission
Unwinding a contract from the start so that each party is returned to its pre-contract position.
Indemnity
A promise to pay the amount of a defined loss if a defined event occurs, claimed pound for pound as a debt.
Expectation damages
Money that puts the innocent party in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed.
Remoteness
The rule from Hadley v Baxendale limiting damages to loss arising naturally or loss both parties contemplated at signing.
Mitigation
The requirement that an innocent party take reasonable steps to reduce its loss, failing which the avoidable part is not recoverable.
Liquidated damages
A sum fixed in the contract as payable on a specified breach, recoverable without proof of loss unless it is a penalty.
Limitation period
The deadline for bringing a claim, set by statute or shortened by the contract.
Governing law
The legal system whose rules are used to interpret and apply the contract.
Seat of arbitration
The legal home of an arbitration, whose law and courts supervise the proceedings.
Joint and several liability
Liability under which a claimant may recover the full amount from any one of several obligors.
