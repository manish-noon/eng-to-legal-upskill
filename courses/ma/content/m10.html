Conditions, the gap and the right to walk

After this module you can read the conditions, covenants and termination clauses of an SPA and say three things: can either party still walk, who pays if the business gets worse before closing, and whether the timetable fits inside the long-stop date.

Why there is a gap, and what a condition does

You already know that signing (the parties become bound) and closing (shares and money change hands) can be separate days. They split whenever something must happen that the parties cannot do themselves on signing day:

  • Regulatory approvals. A competition authority, a foreign investment screener or a sector regulator (central bank, telecoms, insurance) must clear the deal first.
  • Third-party consents. A key customer, landlord or lender has a change-of-control right and must agree.
  • Financing. The buyer needs time to draw its loans or call money from its investors.
  • Reorganisations. The seller must first carve the business out of its group, or move assets into the company being sold.

A condition precedent (CP) is an event that must occur before the parties are obliged to close. It is an if guard on the closing obligation: signed and bound, but the transfer executes only when every guard evaluates true. Until then nobody can force closing. If a CP can never be satisfied, the deal dies without anyone being in breach, unless a party caused the failure.

That last point is the whole economics of this module. Every CP is a possible exit. A CP that depends on a party's own choices is a free option for that party. So the fight is always about three things: how many CPs, how objective they are, and how hard each party must work to satisfy them.

Waiver means giving up the benefit of a condition and closing anyway. Only the party the CP protects can waive it, and legally mandatory CPs cannot be waived at all. A well-drafted clause says who may waive each CP, because a CP that both parties must waive gives each a veto.

Completion is conditional on: (a) the Competition Approval having been obtained; and (b) the Key Customer Consent having been obtained in a form reasonably satisfactory to the Buyer. The Buyer may waive Condition (b) by written notice to the Seller. Condition (a) may not be waived.

Decode: two guards. The buyer can drop the customer guard if it decides to take that risk. Nobody can drop the regulatory one. Note "reasonably satisfactory to the Buyer": without "reasonably", the buyer could reject any consent letter and so hold an option to walk.

An SPA has two CPs: merger clearance, and consent of the target's largest customer to the change of control. Six weeks after signing the customer refuses. The buyer still wants the deal. The seller, who has received a better offer, says the deal is dead because a CP has failed. The SPA says the customer CP is "for the benefit of the Buyer and may be waived by it". Who is right?

  • The seller: an unsatisfied CP releases both parties automatically
  • The buyer: it can waive the customer CP and hold the seller to closing once clearance arrives
  • Neither: both parties must agree to waive any CP
  • The buyer, and it can also waive the merger clearance CP to close faster

The CP protects the buyer (it is the one who loses the customer), so only the buyer can rely on it or give it up. The seller cannot use a condition written for the other side as its own exit. The last option is the tempting error: a mandatory, suspensory clearance is not the parties' to waive.

How hard must you try?

A CP in someone else's hands (a regulator, a customer) needs an efforts standard: the level of effort a party promises towards satisfying it. English-law drafting uses a ladder: reasonable endeavours (take a reasonable course of action, without sacrificing your own commercial interests), all reasonable endeavours (try every reasonable route), best endeavours (do everything a prudent, determined party would do, even at some cost). The exact meaning of each rung is contested and fact-dependent, so good drafting lists the specific steps instead of trusting the label.

For competition clearance the top of the ladder is hell or high water: the buyer must do whatever the authority demands to get clearance, including selling businesses and accepting restrictions. It moves the regulatory risk entirely to the buyer. Sellers want it because a buyer on mere reasonable endeavours can refuse a remedy, let the CP fail, and walk for free.

US drafting uses "commercially reasonable efforts", "reasonable best efforts" and "best efforts". The words differ; the ladder idea is the same, and US courts are also reluctant to draw sharp lines between the rungs.

The buyer has promised "reasonable endeavours" to obtain merger clearance. The authority will clear the deal only if the buyer sells one of its own profitable divisions. The buyer refuses, the long-stop date passes, and the buyer terminates. Is the buyer in breach?

  • Yes: any endeavours clause obliges a party to accept the authority's conditions
  • Probably not: reasonable endeavours does not normally require a party to sacrifice its own commercial interests, which is why sellers ask for hell or high water
  • Yes: the buyer caused the CP to fail, so it can never terminate
  • No, because efforts clauses are unenforceable statements of intent

The lowest rung lets the buyer stop when the cost to itself becomes significant. A seller that wants the buyer to carry antitrust risk must say so expressly (hell or high water, or a list of remedies the buyer must offer). Option 3 confuses two things: the buyer did not breach its obligation, so the usual "no termination by the party at fault" rule does not bite. Efforts clauses are enforceable, just imprecise.

The long-stop date, certainty and financing

The long-stop date is the date by which all CPs must be satisfied or waived, failing which either party (typically) may terminate. It exists because a deal cannot hang open forever: the seller's business is frozen and the buyer's capital is reserved. A standard carve-out says a party cannot terminate if its own breach caused the CP to fail.

US agreements call this the outside date (or "drop-dead date"), often with an automatic extension if the only missing condition is antitrust clearance.

Setting it is arithmetic plus buffer: longest realistic regulatory timetable, plus filing preparation, plus the days between the last CP and closing. Too short and a slow regulator kills a good deal. Too long and the seller is locked up while the buyer watches the market.

Signing is day 0. The buyer needs 20 days to prepare a merger filing. The authority then has 14 days to confirm the filing is complete, after which a 90-day review clock runs. The SPA says closing happens 14 days after the last CP is satisfied. The draft long-stop date is day 120. Assume the authority uses its full time. By how many days does the worst-case closing date overshoot the long-stop date? Answer in days.

20 + 14 + 90 = day 124 for clearance. Add 14 days to closing: day 138. 138 − 120 = 18 days over. Strictly the long-stop tests CP satisfaction (day 124, already 4 days late), so the deal fails either way. A lawyer would push the long-stop to roughly day 150 to 180, or add an automatic extension while the review is pending.

Deal certainty is a currency. A seller comparing two bids does not compare prices. It compares price multiplied by the probability of closing, minus the damage of a failed deal (staff and customers know the company was for sale; other bidders have left). So sellers accept a lower price from a buyer with no financing risk and no antitrust overlap. Buyers, in turn, value optionality: every condition is an option to walk if the world changes, and options are worth money. A buyer who wants many CPs should expect to pay for them in price, and a buyer who strips its conditions can win an auction with a lower number.

A financing condition makes closing conditional on the buyer actually obtaining its funding. Sellers refuse it almost by reflex. It hands the buyer an exit that depends on the buyer's own relationship with its banks, which the seller cannot observe or control. The usual answer is the opposite: the buyer warrants it has the funds, shows signed commitment letters from its lenders and investors at signing, and stays liable if the money does not arrive.

Before reading on: a bidder offers AED 520m with a financing condition. Another offers AED 500m with committed funds and no antitrust overlap. Why might a rational seller take the lower bid?

Assume the first bid closes with probability 80%, and that after a failed deal the business resells for only AED 380m (lost momentum, a stale second auction). Expected value: 0.80 × 520 + 0.20 × 380 = AED 492m. The second bid, at 99% certain: 0.99 × 500 + 0.01 × 380 = AED 498.8m, which is worth more. The numbers are illustrative, but the structure is how sellers and their bankers actually think.

Running the business in the gap

Between signing and closing the seller still owns and runs a business whose value now belongs, economically, to the buyer. That is a classic agency problem: the operator no longer bears the consequences. Gap covenants (also called pre-closing or interim covenants) are the seller's promises about how it will run the target in that period:

  • Ordinary course covenant: run the business as it has been run, consistent with past practice.
  • Consent rights: a list of things the target will not do without the buyer's consent (not to be unreasonably withheld): large capex, new debt, hiring or firing senior staff, changing key contracts, settling litigation, paying dividends.
  • Access and information: the buyer may see management accounts, visit sites and plan integration.

The limit on the buyer's side is gun-jumping: taking control of the target, or coordinating with it as if the deal were done, before competition clearance. Under a suspensory regime the two companies must remain independent competitors until cleared. Consent rights that protect the value of the investment are accepted. Rights that let the buyer steer day-to-day commercial behaviour (prices, customers, bids) are not, and competition authorities fine for it. The same rule limits information: competitively sensitive data goes to a ring-fenced "clean team", not to the buyer's sales staff.

A buyer competes with the target. Clearance is pending. Which gap-covenant request is the gun-jumping problem?

  • Target must not borrow more than AED 5m without buyer consent
  • Target must not sell a factory without buyer consent
  • Target must send all customer quotations above AED 1m to the buyer's sales director for approval before issue
  • Target must give the buyer monthly management accounts

Options 1 and 2 stop extraordinary acts that would change what the buyer is buying: classic value protection. Approving live customer quotes means the buyer is setting a competitor's prices before clearance, which is control of ordinary commercial conduct. Monthly accounts are normally fine if granular competitive data is kept to a clean team.

Who bears a loss in the gap

Suppose the target gets worse between signing and closing. Four mechanisms decide who pays, and they interact.

1. The price mechanism. Under a locked box, economic risk passed to the buyer at the locked box date, which is before signing. Trading profits and trading losses since then are the buyer's. The seller answers only for leakage (value it extracted). Under completion accounts, the price is trued up for cash, debt and working capital at closing, so a trading loss that burns cash reduces the price: the seller bears balance-sheet deterioration up to closing. But notice what completion accounts do not capture: loss of future earning power. If the biggest customer leaves in the gap, the closing balance sheet may look almost unchanged while the enterprise value has dropped. The headline price is fixed in both mechanisms.

2. Warranty repetition. Warranties speak at signing. The buyer wants them repeated at closing (the US term is bring-down), so that a new problem arising in the gap is a breach. The seller resists: repetition makes it the insurer of events it does not control. Typical English-style landing: only fundamental warranties (title, capacity) repeated, or business warranties repeated with a damages claim but no right to walk, and often with a right for the seller to update its disclosures.

US-style agreements make the bring-down a closing condition: the buyer need not close unless the representations are still true at closing, usually tested as "except where the failure would not have a Material Adverse Effect". That converts the warranties into a walk right, filtered by the MAE definition.

3. Gap covenants. A loss caused by the seller breaking a covenant (say, an unapproved contract on bad terms) is a damages claim against the seller.

4. A material adverse change clause. Covered next.

Locked box deal. Locked box date 31 December, signing 1 March, closing 30 June. Between March and June the target makes an ordinary trading loss of AED 6m. No warranties are repeated at closing, there is no MAC clause, and the seller has complied with every covenant. Who bears the AED 6m?

  • The seller: it still owned the company when the loss arose
  • Split equally, as losses in the gap are shared by default
  • The buyer: it took economic risk from the locked box date, and a trading loss is not leakage
  • The seller, through the pound-for-pound leakage indemnity

Ownership and economic risk are deliberately separated in a locked box. The buyer gets the upside from 1 January and so takes the downside. Leakage means value transferred to the seller's side (dividends, fees, bonuses), not value lost to the market. Under completion accounts the answer flips for the cash effect of the loss.

Same locked box deal, price AED 200m. In the gap the target (i) makes a trading loss of AED 6m, (ii) pays the seller an unpermitted dividend of AED 2m, and (iii) pays AED 1.5m of salaries in the ordinary course, listed as permitted leakage. How much can the buyer recover from the seller? Answer in AED millions.

Only the AED 2m dividend is leakage, recoverable pound for pound. The trading loss is the buyer's risk. Ordinary salaries are permitted leakage (and were priced in). Answer: 2.

Completion accounts deal, English style, no MAC clause, warranties given at signing only. In the gap the target's largest customer (25% of revenue) gives notice that it will not renew next year. Cash, debt and working capital at closing are exactly on target. The seller ran the business in the ordinary course. What is the buyer's position?

  • The price falls automatically, because completion accounts put gap risk on the seller
  • The buyer can terminate, since losing 25% of revenue is obviously a material adverse change
  • The buyer must close at the agreed price: completion accounts adjust the balance sheet, not future earnings, and nothing else in this SPA shifts the risk
  • The buyer can claim under the warranty that no customer has given notice, since it is untrue at closing

This is the trap in "completion accounts protect the buyer". They protect against movements in cash, debt and working capital. A lost future revenue stream changes enterprise value, which was fixed at signing. Without a MAC clause there is no implied right to walk, however large the event. The warranty was true when given and is not repeated. A buyer who cares about this customer needed a CP, a repeated warranty or a price mechanism tied to it.

MAC clauses, termination and fees

A material adverse change (MAC) clause, also called material adverse effect (MAE), lets the buyer refuse to close if something seriously bad happens to the target in the gap. Its definition has a standard three-layer structure:

"Material Adverse Effect" means any event that is, or would reasonably be expected to be, materially adverse to the business, financial condition or results of operations of the Group taken as a whole, excluding any effect arising from: (a) changes in general economic or financial market conditions; (b) changes affecting the industry in which the Group operates; (c) changes in law or accounting standards; (d) war, terrorism, natural disaster or pandemic; (e) the announcement of this Agreement; except, in the case of (a) to (d), to the extent the Group is disproportionately affected compared with other participants in its industry.

Decode. Layer one, the trigger: materially adverse to the group as a whole. Layer two, the carve-outs: market-wide and external events are excluded, so systemic risk stays with the buyer. Layer three, the disproportionate-effect exception: a carved-out event counts again to the extent it hits this target harder than its peers. The allocation is: the buyer takes the risk of the world, the seller takes the risk of the company.

Why MACs are rarely invoked successfully: there is no number in the definition, courts read "material" from the viewpoint of a long-term acquirer (a durable hit to earning power, not a bad quarter), the buyer carries the burden of proof, and a buyer that wrongly refuses to close is itself in breach. In practice a MAC is a renegotiation lever: the buyer threatens it and the price moves a little.

Usage differs sharply. CMS 2026 found MAC clauses in only 14% of European deals, against 96% in the US. European sellers treat certainty of closing as the default and courts set a very high bar.

UAE law does not define a MAC. If the parties want one, the drafting does all the work: trigger, carve-outs and, ideally, an objective test.
"If we need protection against one specific event in the gap, let's write that event as a condition with a number, rather than rely on a general MAC we would struggle to prove."

The SPA contains the MAE definition above and a CP that no MAE has occurred. After signing, a regional conflict cuts air travel. The target, an airline caterer, sees EBITDA fall 30%. Its listed peers fall between 26% and 32%. Can the buyer walk?

  • Yes: a 30% fall in EBITDA is material on any view
  • Yes: the conflict is carved out but the disproportionate-effect exception brings it back in
  • Very unlikely: the cause is a carved-out market-wide event and the target is hit about as hard as its peers
  • No, because MAC clauses are unenforceable under English-style SPAs

Run the three layers in order. Trigger: arguably met. Carve-out: war and industry-wide change, so excluded. Exception: only "to the extent disproportionately affected", and 30% sits inside the peer range. Had peers fallen 10%, the excess 20 points could count. MAC clauses are enforceable, just hard to trigger.

Termination rights between signing and closing are deliberately few: CPs not satisfied by the long-stop date, a MAC if one was agreed, sometimes a material breach of warranty or covenant found before closing, and failure by the other side to perform at closing. After termination the SPA falls away except surviving clauses (confidentiality, costs, governing law), and accrued claims for breach survive. Termination is not a remedy for the innocent party's losses by itself.

That gap is filled by fees. A break fee is a fixed sum the seller side pays the buyer if the deal fails for a seller-side reason (it is mostly a public-deal tool, where the target's board accepts a better bid). A reverse break fee is a fixed sum the buyer pays the seller if the deal fails for a buyer-side reason, usually no clearance or no financing. It prices the buyer's option to walk, and it is often drafted as the seller's only remedy, which caps the buyer's downside. Whether a fixed sum is enforceable as drafted depends on governing law, so it is sized to be defensible as proportionate to the loss it protects against.

Price AED 400m. The seller estimates a failed deal would cost it AED 30m (lost staff and customers, a weaker second sale process). Its advisers put the chance of clearance being refused at 20%. What reverse break fee, as a percentage of the price, makes the seller whole if the deal fails for lack of clearance? Answer in %.

The fee is paid only in the failure state, so it must equal the loss in that state: 30 / 400 = 7.5%. The 20% probability is a distractor here. It matters for a different question: what the buyer's promise is worth in expectation today (0.2 × 30 = AED 6m), which is how the buyer prices giving it.

Merger control and foreign investment approval

Merger control is review of a deal by a competition authority before (or sometimes after) it closes, to stop combinations that would substantially reduce competition. It is the dominant CP in private M&A. The mechanics are similar across countries:

  • Thresholds. A filing is triggered when the parties' turnover or market share in that country exceeds set levels. Each country where the parties sell must be checked separately, including countries where the target has no company at all.
  • Mandatory or voluntary; suspensory or not. In a mandatory regime you must file if you meet the thresholds. In a suspensory regime you may not close until cleared. A few regimes are voluntary: you may close without filing, but the authority can investigate and unwind later.
  • Phases. Many regimes run a short first review (Phase 1) that clears unproblematic deals, and a long in-depth review (Phase 2) for deals that raise concerns. The long-stop date must be set for the phase you realistically expect.
  • Remedies. Conditions for clearance: structural (sell a business) or behavioural (promises about future conduct). The efforts clause decides who must accept them.

Foreign direct investment (FDI) screening is a parallel review of whether a foreign buyer may own a sensitive business (defence, data, infrastructure). It asks a national-interest question, not a competition question, and is less predictable.

UAE merger control (from the reference course). The regime is mandatory and suspensory and has applied since 31 March 2025. A filing is triggered at AED 300m of sales in the relevant UAE market or a 40% combined market share. File at least 90 days before completion. Under Executive Regulations in force from 30 July 2026, the Ministry checks completeness within 10 business days, then a 90-day clock runs. Practice is reported at about 30 to 45 days. If no decision issues in time, the deal is deemed rejected, the reverse of most regimes where silence means clearance. Third parties can object. The filing fee is 0.02% of aggregate turnover, capped at AED 150,000. Open points to raise with counsel: whether the thresholds apply per party, how far the regime reaches foreign deals, and the level of penalties.
Saudi Arabia has its own merger control regime with its own thresholds and timing. This course does not state them. Confirm with KSA counsel on any deal with Saudi sales.

A buyer has AED 350m of sales in the UAE market for commercial kitchen equipment. The target sells AED 40m in the same UAE market. Combined share is about 22%. Using the thresholds above, what do you tell the deal team?

  • No filing: the target is below AED 300m and the share is below 40%
  • A UAE filing is likely triggered on the turnover limb, so add a clearance CP and size the long-stop date for it, and have counsel confirm the market definition
  • No filing: both limbs (turnover and share) must be met
  • File voluntarily after closing to be safe

The limbs are alternatives: AED 300m of relevant-market sales or 40% share. Sales in the relevant market exceed AED 300m here whichever way the open "per party or combined" point is resolved, because the buyer alone is above it. Market definition can change the numbers, which is why you confirm. The regime is suspensory, so filing after closing is not an option.

A UAE filing was accepted as complete. The 90-day review period has expired and the Ministry has said nothing. The seller's CFO says: "No objection in time means we are cleared, let's close on Monday." Correct response?

  • Agree: silence is deemed approval, as in most merger regimes
  • Agree, provided the buyer gives the seller an indemnity for any fine
  • Do not close: in the UAE no decision in time means deemed rejection, so the CP is not satisfied
  • Close, then re-file within 30 days

The UAE rule inverts the default an experienced deal-maker carries from other regimes. Closing would be closing without clearance under a suspensory regime. This is also why the long-stop date and the CP wording need a plan for silence (re-filing time, extension rights).

Before reading on: the UAE "silence means rejection" rule changes the negotiation of which two SPA clauses?

The long-stop date (it needs room for a second filing or an extension mechanism if the clock runs out) and the efforts clause (the seller wants the buyer obliged to file early, answer Ministry questions fast and chase a decision, because delay alone can kill the deal). It can also feed into a reverse break fee if the buyer controls the filing.

Closing mechanics

Closing is a scripted exchange. Each side must deliver a list of closing deliverables, set out in a schedule to the SPA:

  • Seller delivers: signed share transfer instruments and proof of title, resignations of outgoing directors, releases of bank security over the target with payoff letters (a lender's statement of the exact amount needed to repay it and release its security), board resolutions approving the transfer, company books, signed ancillary agreements.
  • Buyer delivers: the closing payment, its own signed documents and board approvals, and the W&I policy if there is one.

The funds flow memo lists every payment on the day: who pays whom, how much, to which account, in what order. A typical flow sends part of the price straight to the target's lenders (against the payoff letters), part to the escrow agent, part to advisers, and the balance to the seller. It must reconcile to the price clause to the last dirham.

Because each side fears performing first, the SPA says all closing steps are treated as happening simultaneously, and none is effective unless all are. If one side fails to deliver, the other may postpone, close anyway as far as practicable, or terminate.

All documents and items delivered and payments made at Completion shall be deemed delivered and made simultaneously, and no delivery or payment shall be effective unless all are.

After closing come post-closing filings: updating the share register and company registry, notifying regulators, registering new directors and bank signatories.

For an onshore UAE LLC, the transfer needs a notarised document and an updated trade licence, so "simultaneous" needs engineering: the seller should not sign away the shares before funds are secured, and the buyer does not want to pay before the transfer is registered. An escrow of the price, released on the updated licence, is a common bridge. Module 12 covers this.
Before reading on: why does the buyer pay the target's bank directly at closing instead of paying the seller and letting it repay the loan?

The price was set cash-free and debt-free, so the debt must actually disappear at closing, and the bank's security over the target's assets must be released at the same moment. Paying the bank directly against a payoff letter removes the risk that the seller takes the money and the buyer inherits a company still charged to a lender.

Before reading on: a seller offers "warranties repeated at closing, but the buyer's only remedy for a breach at closing is damages, not termination". Is that worth anything to the buyer?

Yes, but less than it looks. The buyer must still close and then sue, subject to the de minimis, basket, cap and time limits you already know. It protects against moderate gap problems the seller should answer for. It does nothing for a catastrophic event: for that the buyer needs a condition or a termination right.

The long-stop date is 30 September. Clearance has not arrived because the seller, in breach of its covenant, took eleven weeks to supply the data the buyer needed for the filing. On 1 October the seller serves a termination notice, having found a higher bidder. The SPA has the usual wording that a party may not terminate if its breach caused the condition not to be satisfied. Result?

  • Valid: the long-stop date is an absolute cut-off
  • Invalid: the seller's own breach caused the failure, so it cannot use the long-stop right, and the buyer can still terminate or seek to extend and claim damages
  • Valid, but the seller must pay a break fee by operation of law
  • Invalid, because only the buyer ever has a long-stop termination right

Without the "not the party at fault" carve-out, a long-stop date would reward delay: slow-walk the CP, then walk. The clause removes the right only from the party whose breach caused the failure. There is no automatic break fee: fees exist only if the contract provides them.

Key terms

Condition precedent (CP)
An event that must occur (or be waived) before the parties are obliged to close.
Waiver
A party's decision to give up the benefit of a condition or right that exists for its protection.
Efforts standard
The agreed level of effort (reasonable, all reasonable or best endeavours) a party must apply to achieve something outside its sole control.
Hell or high water
An obligation on the buyer to accept whatever remedies a competition authority requires in order to obtain clearance.
Long-stop date
The date by which CPs must be satisfied, after which a party not at fault may terminate; called the outside date in US deals.
Gap covenants
The seller's promises about how the target will be run between signing and closing, including ordinary course conduct and buyer consent rights.
Gun-jumping
The buyer exercising control over, or coordinating commercially with, the target before competition clearance.
Warranty repetition (bring-down)
Treating the warranties as given again at closing, so that problems arising in the gap become breaches.
MAC / MAE
A clause allowing the buyer to refuse to close if a defined, seriously adverse and company-specific change hits the target in the gap.
Disproportionate-effect exception
Wording that brings a carved-out market-wide event back into the MAC definition to the extent the target is hit harder than its peers.
Break fee
A fixed sum payable by the seller side to the buyer if the deal fails for a specified seller-side reason.
Reverse break fee
A fixed sum payable by the buyer to the seller if the deal fails for a specified buyer-side reason such as no clearance or no financing.
Suspensory regime
A merger control regime under which the deal may not close until clearance is obtained.
Deemed rejection
The UAE rule that a merger filing with no decision within the review period is treated as refused.
Funds flow
The closing-day schedule of every payment, payer, payee, amount, account and sequence.
