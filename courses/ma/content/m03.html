The deal lifecycle and the documents before the SPA

After this module you can place any deal on its timeline, say which document governs each stage and which of its terms actually bind, and predict who holds the leverage at that point and why.

The timeline of a private deal

A private deal is a sequence of documents. Each one does two jobs: it releases more information to the buyer, and it increases both sides' commitment. The main contract, the SPA (share purchase agreement, or sale and purchase agreement: the contract under which the shares are sold), comes late. Most of the bargaining position is fixed before it is drafted.

These durations are practitioner rules of thumb, not statistics. A simple bilateral deal with no approvals can run from first call to closing in three months. A regulated or cross-border deal can take a year.

The NDA: the price of admission

An NDA (non-disclosure agreement, also called a confidentiality agreement) is the contract under which the seller releases non-public information and the buyer promises to restrict what it does with it. The problem it solves: the buyer cannot price the business without seeing its secrets, and the most motivated buyer is often a competitor who could exploit them if the deal dies.

Its working parts:

  • Confidential Information: defined widely (everything supplied about the target, plus the buyer's own analyses derived from it, plus the fact that talks are happening). Standard exceptions: information already public, already known to the buyer, independently developed, or lawfully received from a third party.
  • Use restriction: the information may be used only to evaluate this deal. This matters more than the secrecy promise. A competitor who keeps the data secret but uses it to reprice its own tenders has breached the use restriction only.
  • Permitted recipients: the buyer may share with its Representatives (directors, employees, advisers, lenders) who need to know, and is liable for their breaches.
  • Non-solicit: the buyer will not poach the target's employees (sometimes customers) for a period, often one to two years. Without it, diligence becomes a recruiting catalogue.
  • Standstill: the buyer will not buy shares in the target, or make an offer direct to its shareholders, without the board's consent for a period. It matters mainly when the target or its parent is listed: it stops a bidder using inside knowledge to go hostile.
  • Term: obligations last a fixed period, often two to three years. Sellers want longer for trade secrets; buyers resist perpetual obligations because nobody can police them forever.
  • Return or destroy, and a statement that the seller gives no promise that the information is accurate. Promises about accuracy come later, as warranties, and are paid for.
The Recipient shall use the Confidential Information solely for the purpose of evaluating the Proposed Transaction and shall not disclose it to any person other than its Representatives who need to know it for that purpose. The Recipient shall be liable for any breach of this agreement by its Representatives.

Decode: one permitted purpose, a closed list of recipients, and strict liability for what your advisers do with it.

In an auction the seller imposes its own form of NDA on every bidder. With twenty bidders it cannot negotiate twenty different documents, it needs identical protection from all, and a bidder who refuses simply does not get the information. A bidder's lawyer marks up only what truly matters.

A competitor signs an NDA, reviews the target's customer-level pricing, then drops out. Six months later it undercuts the target on three tenders by small margins. It never showed the data to anyone. Which NDA term is the seller's best ground of complaint?

  • The non-disclosure obligation
  • The use restriction
  • The standstill
  • The non-solicit

Nothing was disclosed, so the secrecy promise is intact. The breach is using the information for a purpose other than evaluating the deal. Standstill concerns buying shares; non-solicit concerns people. Note the practical weakness: proving use is hard, which is why sellers stage disclosure and hold back the most sensitive data from competitors until late.

Before reading on: remedies for breach of an NDA are usually damages. Why do sellers insist the NDA also says they can seek an injunction?

Because the loss from leaked secrets is hard to quantify and impossible to reverse. An injunction (a court order to stop) prevents the harm; damages only price it afterwards, and the seller must prove the amount. The clause records the parties' agreement that damages alone are inadequate, which helps persuade a court to grant the order.

Auction documents: teaser to binding offer

In an auction the seller's bank controls a staged release of information to keep bidders competing for as long as possible.

  1. Teaser: a one or two page anonymous profile sent to potential buyers before any NDA. It reveals enough to interest, not enough to identify.
  2. Information memorandum (IM): a 50 to 100 page selling document about the business, released after the NDA. It is marketing. It carries a disclaimer that nobody may rely on it, so a buyer who wants a statement in the IM to be enforceable must get it repeated as a warranty.
  3. Process letter: the rules of the game: deadlines, what an offer must contain, and a statement that the seller may change the rules or stop at any time without liability.
  4. Indicative offer (first-round or non-binding offer): price, funding sources, key assumptions, approvals needed. The seller uses these to shortlist a few bidders for full diligence.
  5. Binding offer (final offer): a price plus a mark-up of the seller's draft SPA. "Binding" is loose usage: it normally means the bidder says it is ready to sign that mark-up, not that a contract exists.

The mechanism to notice: the seller scores bids on price and on contract terms together. A bidder who offers 100 with a heavy SPA mark-up can lose to 97 with a clean one, because the seller discounts price for risk that it will be clawed back later.

The IM states that the target "holds all licences required for its operations". After closing, the buyer finds one licence was never obtained. The SPA contains no warranty on licences and says the buyer has not relied on any statement outside the SPA. What is the buyer's position under an English-law style SPA?

  • It can claim damages because the IM statement was false
  • It has, in practice, no claim unless it can show fraud
  • It can rescind the deal because the IM induced it to sign
  • It can claim against the seller's bank, which wrote the IM

The IM disclaimer plus the SPA's non-reliance wording are designed to make the SPA the only source of enforceable promises. Fraud is the usual exception. The tempting answer treats the IM as a promise; it is a brochure. The lesson for diligence: any IM statement you are relying on must be converted into a warranty.

The letter of intent

A letter of intent (LOI), also called a term sheet, heads of terms or memorandum of understanding, is a short document recording the main commercial terms before the parties spend money on diligence and drafting. It is split deliberately into two classes of term.

Usually not bindingUsually binding
Price and how it is calculated; structure (share or asset deal); form of payment; key conditions; outline of warranties and liability limits; timetableExclusivity; confidentiality; costs (each side bears its own, sometimes with a cost reimbursement if one side walks); governing law and dispute forum
Except for paragraphs 8 (Exclusivity), 9 (Confidentiality), 10 (Costs) and 11 (Governing Law and Jurisdiction), which are legally binding, this letter is not intended to create legal relations and is subject to contract.

Decode: subject to contract is the English-law formula that tells a court the parties do not intend to be bound until a formal contract is signed. Four paragraphs are carved out and bind immediately. Why the split? The commercial terms cannot bind yet because the buyer has not done diligence. The process terms must bind now because they protect the investment each side is about to make.

A non-binding LOI still anchors everything, for three reasons. First, moral commitment: the people who shook hands on a number must explain any departure to their own boards. Second, the burden of proof flips: whoever wants to move away from an LOI term must produce a new fact to justify it. Third, re-trading (reopening an agreed term later) costs credibility, and the other side responds by reopening something else. So an LOI term is not enforceable, but it is expensive to change.

What to settle at LOI stage, if you are the seller: the price and the method for adjusting it (Module 4), the form of payment, whether part of the price is held back, the overall limit on the seller's liability, the conditions, and the length of exclusivity. Everything left vague will be negotiated later, when the seller is weaker. If you are the buyer, the logic reverses: keep the LOI short, state your assumptions explicitly ("price assumes normal working capital and no debt"), and preserve room to adjust.

Some US courts will enforce an express promise in an LOI to negotiate in good faith, and may award damages if a party walks away in bad faith. English law does not enforce a bare agreement to negotiate (Walford v Miles). Read a US-law LOI for any "good faith" wording before assuming it is toothless.
UAE onshore law is civil law and contains general good-faith duties. Whether, and how far, they reach pre-contract negotiations is a question to put to your lawyer, not to assert. It is one reason parties choose English, DIFC or ADGM law for the LOI itself.

An English-law LOI headed "subject to contract" contains: (a) price of AED 120m; (b) seller will not negotiate with others for 45 days; (c) each party bears its own costs; (d) seller will give "customary warranties"; (e) disputes go to DIFC-seated arbitration. The carve-out clause is in standard form. Which set binds?

  • (a), (b) and (e)
  • (b), (c), (d) and (e)
  • (b), (c) and (e)
  • None: "subject to contract" covers the whole letter

Exclusivity, costs and dispute forum are process terms and are carved out as binding. Price and the warranty outline are commercial terms and are not. "None" is tempting but ignores the carve-out, which is the whole point of the standard clause. Always check the actual carve-out list in the document: it is drafted, not implied.

You are selling. The buyer's draft LOI says "Price: AED 100m on a cash-free, debt-free basis, subject to due diligence" and asks for 60 days' exclusivity. Which response best uses your position?

  • Sign quickly to lock in the AED 100m before the buyer changes its mind
  • Refuse exclusivity altogether so that leverage never shifts
  • Before granting exclusivity, get the LOI to define what counts as debt, the working capital basis, the liability cap and any holdback
  • Insist that the price paragraph be made legally binding

The price is not locked: it is non-binding and its two key terms (debt and working capital) are undefined, so the real price is still open. Your leverage peaks just before you grant exclusivity, so spend it on the definitions that move money. Refusing exclusivity usually means a serious buyer will not spend on diligence. A binding price before diligence is something no informed buyer will sign.

"Before we grant exclusivity I would like the heads of terms to cover the price mechanism, the debt-like items in principle and the cap. Those are much cheaper to agree now than in week six."

Exclusivity

Exclusivity (a no-shop or lock-out) is the seller's binding promise, for a fixed period, not to solicit, negotiate with, or give information to any other buyer. It solves the buyer's problem: diligence and drafting can cost millions, and without exclusivity the seller could use the buyer's offer as a stalking horse to extract a higher bid elsewhere.

During the Exclusivity Period the Seller shall not, and shall procure that its Representatives shall not, directly or indirectly solicit, initiate or continue discussions with, or provide information to, any person other than the Buyer in connection with a Competing Transaction, and shall promptly notify the Buyer of any approach received.

The buyer wants a long period (8 to 12 weeks), automatic extension while it negotiates in good faith, and a duty on the seller to report approaches. The seller wants it short (4 weeks), ending early if the buyer lowers its price or misses a milestone, and no extension. A common landing is 4 to 8 weeks with extension by agreement only. Note what exclusivity is not: it does not oblige the seller to sell to the buyer, or the buyer to buy. Under English law the negative promise (do not talk to others for a defined period) is enforceable; a positive promise to keep negotiating with the buyer is not.

The remedy for breach is normally the buyer's wasted costs, sometimes fixed in advance as a cost cover (an agreed reimbursement of documented adviser fees up to a cap). It is rarely the lost profit on the deal, because the buyer cannot prove the deal would have signed.

The LOI says: if the seller breaches exclusivity, it reimburses the buyer's "documented third-party adviser costs, up to AED 1,500,000". The seller signs with a rival in week 5. The buyer has spent AED 1,250,000 on lawyers, AED 450,000 on accountants and AED 400,000 of internal staff time. How much is recoverable under the clause? Answer in AED.

Third-party adviser costs are 1,250,000 + 450,000 = 1,700,000. Internal staff time is not a third-party cost, so it is excluded. The cap is 1,500,000, which is lower than 1,700,000, so the answer is 1,500,000. The buyer is out of pocket by 600,000 even with a breach proven: a cost cover prices the breach, it does not make it unprofitable for a seller who has a much better offer.

Before reading on: the seller has granted 6 weeks' exclusivity. In week 5 the buyer asks for 4 more weeks. What does the request itself tell the seller?

Two things. The buyer is not ready, so signing is further away than planned. And the seller briefly regains leverage: extension is the seller's to give, so it can charge for it. Typical asks: confirmation that the price stands, a closed list of open points, or a shorter extension tied to milestones. A seller who extends for free has given away the one bargaining chip exclusivity leaves it.

Signing, the gap and closing

Signing is when the SPA becomes a binding contract. Closing (English usage: completion) is when the shares are transferred and the price is paid. They are separate events because some things must happen in between that neither side can do before the contract exists. These are conditions precedent (CPs): events that must occur, or be waived by the party they protect, before the obligation to close arises. Typical CPs: merger control clearance (a competition authority's approval of the deal), a sector regulator's approval, a key customer's consent to the change of owner, release of the bank's security.

The period between the two is the gap. Three devices manage it. A long-stop date is the deadline by which CPs must be satisfied, failing which either party may terminate. Pre-closing covenants (gap covenants) require the seller to run the business in the ordinary course and to get the buyer's consent for major decisions, because the seller still owns a business whose value now belongs economically to someone else. And the SPA says who bears the risk if the business deteriorates in the gap (Module 6 onward).

A simultaneous sign-and-close has no gap: the SPA is signed and performed in one sitting. It is possible only when no approval or consent is needed first. It removes gap risk entirely, which is why both sides prefer it when available.

UAE merger control is mandatory and suspensory: a deal that meets the thresholds (AED 300m of relevant UAE sales or a 40% combined share, in force since 31 March 2025) cannot close before clearance, the filing must be made at least 90 days before completion, and silence at the end of the review period is a deemed rejection. That alone forces a gap. Separately, transferring shares in a mainland LLC needs a notarised amendment to its constitutional document and an updated trade licence, so onshore "closing" is a sequence of government steps, not a single signature. DIFC and ADGM companies transfer by share transfer form and register update.

At closing the seller delivers a list of closing deliverables: signed share transfers, board approvals, director resignations, releases from the target's lenders, the company's registers. The buyer delivers the money, following a funds flow memo: a table of every payment, payer, payee, account and amount (Module 4). Read the release instructions with the funds flow. A closing arrangement can make delivery of the signed transfers conditional on confirmation of funds. Identify who holds each document and who may release it. Otherwise one side could part with its shares or cash while the other side still has work to do.

Which deal can most plausibly sign and close on the same day?

  • A buyer with AED 900m of UAE sales buying a competitor with AED 350m of UAE sales in the same market
  • A target whose largest customer contract terminates automatically on a change of owner unless the customer consents, where consent has not yet been requested
  • A DIFC holding company with no regulated activities, sold by its founder to a financial investor with no overlapping business, with the customer consents already in hand
  • A licensed insurer being sold to a foreign group

A gap exists only where something must happen after the contract is binding and before transfer. Option three has no regulator, no competition overlap, and consents were obtained before signing. The first triggers suspensory merger control. The second can be solved before signing, but as described has not been. The fourth needs a sector regulator's approval.

An SPA is signed on 1 March with one CP (merger clearance) and a long-stop date of 30 June. On 15 May the target loses its second-largest customer for reasons unrelated to the deal. The SPA has no clause letting the buyer exit for deterioration in the business. Clearance arrives on 1 June. What is the buyer's position?

  • It can walk away because the business is not what it agreed to buy
  • It can wait until 30 June and then terminate
  • It must close at the agreed price; its only remedies are whatever the warranties and covenants give it
  • It can reopen the price because the LOI assumed that customer

After signing the buyer is bound. The only exits are the ones written in the SPA: an unsatisfied CP at the long-stop date, or an express termination right. Here the CP was satisfied in time and no deterioration right exists, so gap risk sits with the buyer. Waiting does not help: once the CP is met the obligation to close arises. The LOI is superseded by the SPA. This is why buyers negotiate for gap protections, and why sellers resist them.

Where leverage moves

Leverage is each side's cost of walking away compared with the other's. It changes at each stage because alternatives and sunk costs change.

StageWho is strongerMechanism
Before exclusivitySellerSeveral bidders are live. Each bidder's concessions are driven by fear of losing to another.
Early exclusivityRoughly balancedRival bidders are still warm; the buyer has started spending.
Late diligence, before signingBuyerRivals have redeployed. A relaunched sale is "tainted": the market asks what the first buyer found. Management is tired, staff may know, and the seller has mentally spent the money.
Signing to closingDepends on the CPsBoth are bound. Whoever controls satisfaction of a condition holds an option.
After closingSeller, unless money was held backThe seller has the cash and the buyer must sue to recover any. An amount held back reverses this.

Sunk costs work on both sides. Rationally they should be ignored. In practice a buyer's deal team that has spent AED 3m in fees and told its board the deal is happening is reluctant to walk, and good sellers know it. So the buyer's late-stage strength is real but bounded: a re-trade works only if it is tied to a diligence finding and is smaller than the seller's cost of starting again.

LOI price AED 100m. Late in exclusivity the buyer demands a price cut. The seller's alternative is to walk and return to the runner-up, who bid AED 97m. The seller estimates a 70% chance that deal completes; if it fails the seller keeps the business, which it values at AED 85m. Restarting costs AED 1m in fees either way. Assuming a risk-neutral seller, what is the largest price cut the buyer can demand before the seller is better off walking? Answer in AED m.

Value of walking = 0.7 × 97 + 0.3 × 85 − 1 = 67.9 + 25.5 − 1 = 92.4. The seller should accept any price above 92.4, so the largest cut is 100 − 92.4 = 7.6. Two lessons. The buyer's late leverage is exactly the gap between the LOI price and the seller's decayed alternative. And the seller can shrink it by keeping the runner-up warm (raising the 70%) for as long as the exclusivity wording permits, which is why buyers insist the seller cut off all contact.

Auction. Three bidders submit final offers. The seller grants bidder A exclusivity for 4 weeks. In week 3, A says diligence has found an unpaid tax liability and asks for a 5% price cut. Bidder B, whose offer was 2% below A's, has told the bank it remains interested. Compared with a bilateral deal at the same stage, how strong is A's hand?

  • Stronger, because A won the auction and the seller has publicly chosen it
  • Weaker, because the seller has a live alternative only 2% lower and exclusivity expires in a week
  • The same: leverage late in exclusivity always sits with the buyer
  • Stronger, because a tax finding is a new fact

The buyer's late-stage strength comes from the decay of the seller's alternatives. Here the alternative has not decayed: B is live, close on price, and exclusivity is short. A 5% cut leaves A below B, so the seller can simply wait a week. The new fact justifies opening the point but does not set the size of the cut; B will find the same liability, so a rational seller might concede something well under 5%. "Always" answers ignore the mechanism.

A founder is selling to a single PE buyer. Exclusivity has run 10 weeks after two free extensions. The founder has told key staff and two customers about the sale. The buyer now asks for 15% of the price to be held back for two years, which was not in the LOI. What best describes the position?

  • The founder is strong: the buyer has sunk 10 weeks of fees and will not walk
  • The LOI protects the founder because it did not mention a holdback
  • The founder is weak: no rival bidder, a business that staff and customers know is for sale, and extensions granted for nothing
  • Neither side has leverage because nothing is binding

Both sides have sunk costs, but the founder's walk-away value has fallen much further: there is no alternative buyer, and a failed sale now damages the business itself because staff and customers know. The LOI is non-binding and silent on holdback, so it gives no protection; silence is exactly the gap the buyer is exploiting. The correct earlier moves were to cover holdback in the LOI and to charge for each extension.

A buyer signs an SPA with a single CP: approval by the buyer's own shareholders, with no obligation on the buyer to recommend or pursue that approval. Who holds the leverage during the gap?

  • The seller, because the buyer is contractually bound
  • Neither: both are bound equally
  • The buyer, because it effectively holds a free option to close or not
  • The seller, because it still owns and controls the business

A condition within one party's control is an option for that party. The buyer can let the condition fail if the business or the market worsens, and close if it improves; the seller is locked in and off the market meanwhile. This is why sellers refuse CPs the buyer controls, or pair them with an obligation to use best endeavours and a fee payable if the condition fails. "Bound" is only as strong as the conditions are narrow.

Before reading on: after closing, the seller has the money. Why does that single fact change how every later dispute plays out?

Because the party who must bring the claim bears the cost, delay and proof burden. If the seller holds all the cash, the buyer must sue (or arbitrate) to recover anything, and many small or uncertain claims are not worth it. If part of the price is held by the buyer or a neutral agent, the seller must act to get it released, so the same weak claim now has settlement value. Possession of the cash decides who has to move.

Before reading on: why would a well-advised buyer ever agree to a long, detailed LOI when a short one preserves its flexibility?

In an auction it has no choice: the process letter demands detail and the seller scores on it. In a bilateral deal, detail buys things the buyer values: a longer exclusivity period, the seller's commitment to a liability framework the buyer needs for its financing or insurance, and less risk of spending heavily on diligence only to find the parties were never close. Detail is a currency the buyer trades for exclusivity.
