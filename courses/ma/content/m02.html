Companies, deals and the cast of characters

After this module you can say exactly what is being bought in any deal, choose between a share purchase, an asset purchase and a merger for a given set of facts, and predict how a buyer or seller will behave on risk from what type of party it is.

What a company is

A company is a legal person separate from the people who own it. It can own property, sign contracts, employ staff, sue and be sued in its own name. Two consequences drive everything in M&A.

  • Limited liability. The company's debts are the company's, not its owners'. An owner can lose what it invested and no more. Courts set this aside ("pierce the corporate veil") only in rare cases such as fraud.
  • Continuity. The company's contracts, licences, staff and liabilities stay with it when its owners change. The company does not notice who owns it.

Ownership is divided into shares. A shareholder owns shares and with them a bundle of rights: votes, a share of any profit distributed (a dividend), and a share of whatever is left if the company is wound up. Shareholders do not manage. They appoint directors, who together form the board and run the company. Directors owe their duties to the company, not to whoever appointed them. The rulebook between the company, its shareholders and its directors is the constitution, called the articles of association in English-style systems. It sets share classes, voting thresholds and any limits on transferring shares.

Businesses are rarely one company. A group is a tree of companies. The holding company (holdco) at the top owns shares in subsidiaries (companies it controls). The operating company (opco) holds the actual business: staff, customers, licences. Groups use separate companies to ring-fence liabilities (a claim against one opco cannot reach its sister), to fit local licensing rules, and to create clean units that can be sold one at a time. Control usually means more than 50% of the votes, which is enough to appoint the board. Control and economic interest are different things, and both multiply down a chain in different ways.

Holdco owns 80% of Midco. Midco owns 60% of Opco. Each share carries one vote. Opco pays a dividend of AED 100. What percentage of that dividend ultimately belongs to Holdco? Answer as a number of percent.

Economic interest multiplies down the chain: 0.80 × 0.60 = 0.48, so 48%. Control does not multiply. Holdco controls Midco (80% is more than half), and Midco controls Opco (60% is more than half), so Holdco controls Opco's board while owning under half of its economics.

Three company flavours matter in the UAE. An onshore LLC (mainland limited liability company) is licensed by an emirate's economic department and can trade anywhere in the UAE. Foreigners can own 100% by default, except in activities designated as having strategic impact. A free zone company is licensed by one of the many free zone authorities, with its own registry and rules. A DIFC or ADGM company sits in one of the two financial free zones, under common-law company statutes and English-language courts. Many groups put a DIFC or ADGM holdco on top of onshore opcos, so that investors deal with each other under familiar common-law rules. The mechanics differ too: transferring shares in an onshore LLC needs a notarised document and an updated licence, while a DIFC or ADGM transfer is a signed form and a register entry.

Three ways to buy a business

M&A means mergers and acquisitions: transactions that transfer ownership or control of a business. The company or business being bought is the target. There are three structures.

  • Share purchase: the buyer buys the target company's shares from its shareholders. The company itself is untouched. It simply has a new owner.
  • Asset purchase: the buyer buys chosen assets (and agrees to take chosen liabilities) from the company. The seller is the company, which stays behind with its old owners and everything not sold.
  • Statutory merger: two companies combine into one by a procedure laid down in a statute. All assets and liabilities pass automatically, and a required majority of shareholders can bind those who voted against.

The seller generally prefers a share sale: it leaves with cash and no company to clean up. The buyer accepts the inherited liabilities and manages them by investigation, contract protection and price. So share purchases dominate private M&A. Asset purchases are chosen when the history is the problem (an insolvent or tainted company), when only part of a company is being sold, or when the buyer wants to pick specific assets. In the ABA 2025 study of US private deals, asset deals were 21% of the sample. Statutory mergers are the standard tool for buying US listed companies, where thousands of shareholders cannot each sign a contract. They are uncommon in UAE private M&A.

In an asset purchase in the UAE, employees do not transfer automatically. Each one is terminated by the seller and rehired by the buyer, which triggers payment of their accrued end-of-service gratuity (a statutory lump sum owed when employment ends). In a share purchase the employer does not change, so the accrued gratuity stays inside the target as a liability the buyer inherits.

Before reading on: if an asset purchase leaves the liabilities behind, why does every buyer not insist on one?

Because the friction is real and the seller's price goes up. Every customer contract needs consent, which gives each customer a chance to renegotiate. Licences may need fresh applications. Staff must be moved. The seller is left with a shell holding liabilities and possibly two layers of tax, so it demands more money. And the protection is imperfect: some liabilities follow the business under local law. The share deal with contractual protection is usually the cheaper way to reach the same risk position.

A UAE logistics company is under investigation for historic customs under-payments of unknown size. It has 12 warehouses, 3 large customer contracts with no restrictions on transfer, and 40 staff. Its owner is willing to sell by either route. You want the warehouses and the customers. Which structure is the best starting point?

  • Share purchase, because it is the market standard for private deals
  • Asset purchase, because the unquantifiable liability stays in the seller's company and the transfer friction here is small
  • Statutory merger, because liabilities are extinguished when companies combine
  • Share purchase with a lower price, because price can always compensate for risk

The liability is unknown in size, so it cannot be priced, and the things you want are few and transferable. That is the asset-deal pattern. A merger does the opposite of extinguishing liabilities: they all pass to the combined company. A price cut works for a risk you can estimate, not for an open-ended one. You would still pay gratuity costs on rehiring 40 staff and would ask local counsel whether any customs liability can follow the assets.

A software company has 900 customer contracts, most of which forbid transfer without the customer's consent but say nothing about a change of owner. It holds a regulatory licence that cannot be transferred. Its history is clean. Which structure fits?

  • Share purchase: contracts and licence stay in the company and no customer consent is triggered
  • Asset purchase: it is always safer for the buyer
  • Asset purchase, with consent sought from only the 50 largest customers
  • Either: the structures are equivalent when the history is clean

In a share purchase the contracting party does not change, so a ban on transfer is not triggered, and the licence stays where it is. An asset purchase would need 900 consents and a new licence. Seeking only 50 consents leaves 850 contracts that were not validly transferred. Structures are never equivalent: they differ in what moves automatically.

You are choosing a structure. Share route: you estimate a 10% chance of an inherited claim costing AED 30,000,000, with no protection from the seller. Asset route: no inherited claim, but you pay AED 1,800,000 of gratuity on rehiring staff, AED 700,000 of transfer costs, and face a 20% chance that a key customer refuses consent, costing AED 4,000,000. By how much does the expected cost of the asset route exceed that of the share route? Answer in AED.

Share route: 0.10 × 30,000,000 = 3,000,000. Asset route: 1,800,000 + 700,000 + (0.20 × 4,000,000) = 2,500,000 + 800,000 = 3,300,000. Difference: 300,000 in favour of the share route. But the share route's worst case is 30,000,000 and the asset route's is 6,500,000. The expected values are close and the tails are not, which is why the real answer is usually a share deal plus contractual protection against the claim.

Why deals happen, and why many destroy value

A buyer pays more than a target is worth on its own only if it expects to make the difference back. The standard reasons:

  • Synergy: the combined business is worth more than the two apart, through lower costs (one head office, one tech stack) or higher revenue (selling each side's products to the other's customers).
  • Scale: more volume over the same fixed costs, or more bargaining power with suppliers.
  • Capability: buying a technology, a team, a licence or a market position that would take years to build.
  • Financial return: buying a business to improve it and resell it at a profit.
  • Exit: the deal exists because the seller needs to turn its shares into cash. This is the seller's reason, and it shapes the timetable.

The sale price splits the expected gain between the two sides. The seller captures everything the buyer pays above standalone value. The buyer keeps only what remains after the cost of combining the businesses. Buyers lose money on deals when they overestimate synergies, underestimate the cost and time of integration, or get carried past their own number in a competitive sale. Cost synergies are more reliable than revenue synergies, because the buyer controls its own costs but not its customers.

This matters for advice later. A recommendation to pursue a deal, pursue it with conditions, or walk away is a comparison between the value at stake and the risk the buyer will be left holding once the terms have settled. If the value case is thin, a small unprotected risk is enough to say walk.

A target is worth AED 100m on its own. You estimate synergies with a present value of AED 30m and one-off integration costs of AED 8m. What is the highest price you can pay without destroying value for your own shareholders? Answer in AED millions.

Value to you: 100 + 30 − 8 = 122. At 122 the seller has captured the whole gain and you break even. At 125 you destroy 3. Note what the number depends on: if only half the synergies arrive, your ceiling is 100 + 15 − 8 = 107. Any risk you keep under the contract, such as an inherited liability, comes off the ceiling too.

Who buys, who sells, and how each behaves on risk

A party's type predicts its negotiating behaviour better than anything its lawyers say. Start with buyers.

  • Strategic buyer: an operating company buying a business related to its own. It can pay for synergies, intends to hold for the long term, and cares about integration and reputation.
  • Financial buyer: an investor buying to resell. The main kind is a private equity (PE) fund: a pool of money raised from investors for a fixed term, typically about ten years, managed by a firm called the sponsor. It buys companies, largely with borrowed money, improves them over several years and sells. It has no synergies, so it is disciplined on price and intensely focused on terms.
  • Sovereign wealth fund: a state-owned investment fund. It has very long horizons and large capital, often takes minority positions, and its involvement can add regulatory and political review in the target's country. The PwC TransAct 2026 report records Middle East sovereign funds investing US$127bn globally in 2025.

Sellers differ more sharply, because what happens to the money after the sale differs.

When a seller will not stand behind the business after the sale, the buyer's protection has to come from somewhere else. The main tool is warranty and indemnity (W&I) insurance: a policy, usually bought by the buyer, under which an insurer pays for losses from breaches of the seller's warranties that nobody knew about at signing. It lets a PE seller walk away clean while the buyer still has someone to claim against. The CMS European M&A Study 2026 found W&I insurance in 23% of its deals and in 59% of large deals.

The US name is representations and warranties insurance (RWI). The ABA 2025 study found it in 63% of its sample of US private deals.

Before reading on: a PE fund is selling a company for AED 200m. The buyer asks for AED 20m to be held back for two years to cover claims. Why does the fund resist this far harder than a founder would?

The fund's performance is measured by how much cash it returns to its investors and how fast. Money held back is cash it cannot distribute, and a contingent liability means it cannot close the fund. Its managers may have to return part of their own profit share if a claim later succeeds. A founder just has less money in the bank for two years. Same clause, very different cost, so the PE seller will trade price or pay for insurance to avoid it.

The buyer in that deal agrees to drop the holdback and buy a W&I policy with a limit of AED 20,000,000. The insurance broker Marsh's Q1 2026 data puts Middle East premiums at 0.7% to 1.8% of the policy limit. The quote is 1.5% of the limit. What is the premium? Answer in AED.

1.5% × 20,000,000 = 300,000. Compare that with what the holdback cost the seller: AED 20m locked up for two years. The insurance is cheap relative to the friction it removes, which is why the argument is usually only about who pays the premium.

A PE fund in its ninth year is selling a company it owns. Two bids arrive. Bid A: AED 210m, with 10% of the price held back for 24 months and the seller liable for warranty claims up to 30% of the price. Bid B: AED 204m, nothing held back, the seller's liability limited to AED 1, and the buyer taking out W&I insurance. Which is the fund more likely to choose?

  • Bid A, because a fund's duty is to maximise price
  • Bid B, because certainty and immediate distribution of all proceeds are worth more to a late-life fund than a 3% price difference
  • Bid A, because the holdback will probably be returned in full anyway
  • Neither: it will wait for a strategic buyer

Bid A's extra AED 6m comes with AED 21m locked up and up to AED 63m of exposure for two years, in a fund that needs to wind up. Bid B lets it distribute everything now. "The holdback will probably come back" is the tempting error: probably is not certainly, and the fund cannot distribute or close on probably. A bidder who understands the seller's type can win with a lower headline price.

You are buying a business from its founder, who will keep 20% of the shares and stay as CEO for three years. Compared with buying from a PE fund, which prediction is most reliable?

  • The founder will refuse to give any warranties, since she is staying anyway
  • She can give fuller warranties, but you will be reluctant to sue your own CEO, so you will want protection that does not depend on a lawsuit, such as money held back or adjustments to her deferred payments
  • Warranties do not matter, because her 20% stake aligns her interests with yours
  • She will insist on W&I insurance, because all sellers now do

She has the knowledge to stand behind detailed statements, and she has the money for now. But a claim against the person running your new business is self-harm, so buyers prefer mechanisms that work without litigation. Her 20% stake aligns her on the future, not on problems that already existed at the sale. W&I insurance is used in a minority of deals overall (23% in CMS 2026).

Private or public, auction or bilateral

Private M&A is the purchase of a company whose shares are not traded on a stock exchange. There are a few sellers, the terms are freely negotiated, and the buyer can investigate the target in depth. Public M&A is the purchase of a listed company. There are thousands of shareholders, so the buyer makes an offer to all of them under a takeover code enforced by a regulator, with a fixed timetable, equal treatment of shareholders, limited access to information and almost no contractual protection after the deal. This course is about private M&A.

A private sale runs in one of two ways.

  • Auction: the seller's financial adviser invites several buyers, shares information in stages, and collects bids against deadlines. Bidders must comment on the seller's draft contract, and the seller compares bids on price and on terms together.
  • Bilateral (or proprietary) deal: one buyer and one seller negotiate. The buyer normally asks for exclusivity: a binding promise by the seller not to talk to other buyers for a fixed period.

In an auction with four bidders, you submit the highest price along with 60 changes to the seller's draft contract. A rival bids 4% less with 8 changes. The seller chooses the rival. What is the best explanation?

  • The seller made a mistake: price is what matters
  • Your changes must have been legally wrong
  • The seller prices the bid as a package. Sixty changes signal more risk coming back to the seller and a lower chance of signing on time, which can outweigh 4%
  • Sellers always prefer the bidder with the fewest comments

A heavy markup moves risk back to the seller and makes the deal less certain, and both have a money value. The seller compares price minus expected cost of your terms. "Always fewest comments" overstates it: a big enough price gap wins. The skill is choosing the few changes that carry real value, which requires knowing where each term would have landed anyway.

Another bidder has told the seller it is still interested. Ten days into your 30-day exclusivity period, your investigation finds a problem, and you ask for a 10% price cut. Twenty-five days in, with the same facts, you ask again. When is the seller more likely to agree, and why?

  • Day 10, because the seller has had less time to become stubborn
  • Day 10, because the seller cannot yet turn to another buyer, while by day 25 it can simply wait five days and restart the competition
  • Day 25, because deadlines always pressure the seller
  • No difference: the facts are the same

Exclusivity is an option the buyer holds, and it decays. Early on, the seller's alternative is 20 days of doing nothing. Near expiry, its alternative is another bidder next week. The deadline pressures the party with no alternative, which at day 25 is the buyer. Leverage is a function of each side's best alternative at that moment, not of the facts alone. With no second bidder waiting the picture changes: the longer a process runs, the colder the seller's alternatives get, as Module 3 shows.

Buying less than everything: minority stakes, venture rounds, joint ventures

Not every deal transfers control. A minority investment buys less than 50%. A venture round is a minority investment in a young company, usually for newly issued shares, so the money goes into the company and not to its existing owners. A joint venture (JV) is a business owned and run by two or more parties together, often through a new company.

In all three, the investor cannot simply outvote the others, so its protection is contractual. The shareholders' agreement (SHA) is a contract between the shareholders on how the company is run and how anyone gets out. Four concepts recur.

  • Reserved matters: a list of decisions (new debt, a change of business, issuing shares, selling the company) that need the minority investor's consent. They are a veto, not a power to act.
  • Pre-emption: a right of first refusal. A shareholder who wants to sell must first offer the shares to the others, and new shares must first be offered to existing holders. It stops strangers arriving and stakes being diluted.
  • Drag-along: if holders above a stated threshold agree to sell the company, they can force the rest to sell on the same terms. It protects the majority's ability to deliver 100% to a buyer.
  • Tag-along: if the majority sells, the minority can require the buyer to take its shares too, on the same terms. It protects the minority from being left behind with a new controller.
If Shareholders holding at least 75% of the Shares (the "Dragging Shareholders") wish to transfer all of their Shares to a bona fide third-party purchaser, they may require all other Shareholders to transfer all of their Shares to that purchaser on the same terms and at the same price per Share.

Decode: holders of 75% can deliver the whole company. "Bona fide third-party" stops a majority dragging the minority into a sale to its own affiliate at a low price. "Same terms and price" is the minority's protection.

A company has three shareholders: A with 55%, B with 25% and C with 20%. The clause above applies. A buyer offers a good price for 100% only. A and C want to sell. B refuses. Can the sale go ahead?

  • No, a sale of 100% needs every shareholder's signature
  • Yes, A alone controls the company with 55%
  • Yes, A and C together hold 75%, which meets the threshold, so they can drag B on the same terms
  • No, A and C hold only 75% and the clause requires more than 75%

55% + 20% = 75%, and the clause says "at least 75%". B must sell at the same price. A's 55% controls the board but cannot force B to sell B's own shares: control of a company is not ownership of other people's shares. This is why a buyer's lawyers check the drag threshold against the list of who has agreed to sell.

Before reading on: you are a 15% investor. Which of the four SHA protections is worth most to you, and what does it not give you?

Reserved matters protect you while you are in: the majority cannot load the company with debt or change its business without you. But a veto gives you no way to make anything happen, and none of the four gives you a way to force a sale of your stake. Tag-along only helps if the majority chooses to sell. So minority investors also negotiate exit rights, such as a right to force a sale or a listing after a set number of years. A stake you cannot exit is worth far less than its percentage suggests.

The seller's investment banker tells you, a bidder: "Honestly, the lawyers are overcomplicating this. Sign the seller's draft as it stands and you will win." How should you weigh this?

  • As neutral advice from a market expert who sees many deals
  • As a statement from someone paid by the seller on closing, whose fee does not fall if you carry more risk after the deal
  • As false, because bankers do not understand contracts
  • As binding on the seller, because the banker is the seller's agent

The banker may well be right that a clean markup wins. But the banker works for the seller and is paid on completion and on price, not on how the risk is split afterwards. Use the information about what the seller values, and let your own lawyers decide which risks you can afford to accept. Treating it as false throws away useful signal. Casual remarks by an adviser do not bind the seller.

Key terms

Limited liability
The rule that a company's debts belong to the company alone, so its shareholders can lose only what they invested.
Articles of association
A company's constitution, setting the rights of shares, voting thresholds and limits on share transfers.
Holding company
A company whose main asset is shares in the subsidiaries it controls.
Share purchase
An acquisition of a company's shares from its shareholders, leaving the company with all its assets, contracts and liabilities.
Asset purchase
An acquisition of selected assets and liabilities from a company, which itself stays with its existing owners.
Statutory merger
A combination of two companies into one under a statutory procedure, with all assets and liabilities passing automatically.
Change-of-control clause
A contract term giving the counterparty rights, usually to terminate, if the ownership of the other party changes.
Synergy
The extra value created when two businesses are combined, through lower costs or higher revenue than they would achieve apart.
Private equity fund
A fixed-life pool of investor money that buys companies, largely with debt, to improve and resell them.
Clean exit
A sale in which the seller receives the full price at closing and keeps minimal liability afterwards.
W&I insurance
A policy under which an insurer pays for loss from unknown breaches of the seller's warranties in place of the seller.
Auction
A sale process in which several buyers bid in stages on the seller's draft contract, which gives the seller leverage.
Exclusivity
A binding promise by the seller not to negotiate with other buyers for a fixed period.
Drag-along
A right of shareholders above a set threshold to force the remaining shareholders to sell on the same terms.
Due diligence
The buyer's structured investigation of the target before it commits to the deal.
